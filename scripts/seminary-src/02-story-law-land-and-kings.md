# Leviticus: how a holy God lives with sinful people
@ref LEV 1:1-9; LEV 11:44-45; LEV 19:2; LEV 19:18; HEB 10:1-4
@q Why would a holy God give his people so many instructions about sacrifice and cleanness?

Leviticus is the book most readers abandon, and that is a loss, because it answers a hard question: if God has just moved into the middle of a camp of sinners, how do they stay alive? The tabernacle is finished at the end of Exodus. Leviticus opens with God speaking to Moses from it, and the rest of the book explains how the people can come near.

The first seven chapters list five kinds of offering. The burnt offering is given wholly to God and pictures complete devotion. The grain offering is a gift of the first fruits of the harvest. The peace offering is a shared meal. The sin offering and guilt offering deal with wrongs, one with sin as a condition and one with specific debts that need paying back. In each case a worshiper lays a hand on the animal, the animal dies in the worshiper's place, and the priest carries out the ritual.

The middle chapters cover the priesthood, clean and unclean animals, skin diseases, bodily discharges and the great Day of Atonement. The second half applies holiness to ordinary life: honest scales, fair wages, care for the poor, respect for the old. The refrain is "be holy, because I am holy" (Leviticus 11:44). Hidden in the middle of this is the verse Jesus calls the second great commandment: "love your neighbor as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18).

The book also shows the limits of the system. The same sacrifices had to be offered again and again. The letter to the Hebrews says that the law was only "a shadow of the good things to come" and that the blood of bulls and goats could never take away sins (Hebrews 10:1-4). Leviticus therefore creates a longing it cannot fill. It teaches Israel that sin is serious, that atonement costs a life, and that only God can provide a way home. Christians read it as the vocabulary in which the cross is explained.

# The Day of Atonement
@ref LEV 16:1-22; LEV 16:29-31; HEB 9:6-14; HEB 10:19-22
@conf scholars

Once a year, on the tenth day of the seventh month, the high priest of Israel did something no one else could do. He passed through the curtain and entered the Most Holy Place, where the ark of the covenant stood beneath the golden mercy seat. Leviticus 16 gives the whole procedure, and it is a study in how seriously the Bible takes the gap between God and sinful people.

The high priest first washed and put on plain linen. He offered a bull for his own sin, since he was a sinner too. He took a censer of burning coals and incense into the inner room, so that a cloud of smoke covered the mercy seat. He then sprinkled the blood of the bull there, and afterward the blood of a goat chosen "for the LORD" for the sins of the people. Blood was applied to the sanctuary itself, because even the holy place was affected by the uncleanness of those who used it.

Then came a second goat. The priest laid both hands on its head and confessed over it all the sins of Israel. The goat was led away into the wilderness, and it carried on itself all their iniquities to a solitary land (Leviticus 16:22). That is the origin of the word scapegoat in English. People were told to "afflict" themselves, which Jews have always understood as fasting, and to do no work. The day is still observed in Judaism as Yom Kippur.

The letter to the Hebrews reads the day as a picture. Christ entered not an earthly tent but heaven itself, not with the blood of goats but with his own, and not once a year but once for all (Hebrews 9:11-12). Because of that, the writer says, believers can "draw near with a sincere heart in full assurance of faith" (Hebrews 10:22). The way past the curtain is open.

# Forty years in the wilderness
@ref NUM 13:30-33; NUM 14:1-4; NUM 14:33-34; NUM 21:4-9; JHN 3:14-15
@conf scholars

Numbers takes its English name from two censuses of Israel's fighting men, but it is really the story of a journey that went wrong. Israel leaves Sinai in the second year after the exodus and travels toward the promised land. At Kadesh Barnea on the southern edge of Canaan, Moses sends twelve men to explore it. They come back with grapes so large that two men carry a single cluster on a pole, and they agree that the land is rich. But ten of them add that the people there are giants and the cities are fortified. Only Caleb and Joshua say, "We must go up and take possession of the land" (Numbers 13:30).

The people weep all night and talk about choosing a new leader to take them back to Egypt. God's judgment is exact. Because they refused to enter, none of the adults aged twenty and over, except Caleb and Joshua, will see the land. One year for each day of the exploration, the nation will wander for forty years (Numbers 14:33-34). A generation dies in the desert, and their children enter instead.

The wilderness years hold other scenes: rebellions against Moses, water from a rock, a talking donkey in the story of Balaam, and a plague of snakes. When the snakes come, God tells Moses to make a bronze serpent and set it on a pole, and anyone bitten who looks at it lives (Numbers 21:4-9). Jesus uses this in his talk with Nicodemus: "Just as Moses lifted up the snake in the wilderness, so the Son of Man must be lifted up, that everyone who believes in Him may have eternal life" (John 3:14-15).

Paul looks back on this generation as a warning. They all passed through the sea and ate the same spiritual food, yet most of them fell in the wilderness, and Paul says these things "took place as examples to keep us from craving evil things as they did" (1 Corinthians 10:1-6). Numbers shows that being rescued is not the same as trusting, and that God's patience is long but real.

# Deuteronomy: the covenant renewed at Moab
@ref DEU 6:4-9; DEU 30:11-20; DEU 34:1-8; MAT 22:36-40
@conf scholars

The Israelites are camped on the plains of Moab, east of the Jordan, with the promised land in view. Moses is a hundred and twenty years old, and he knows he will not cross. Deuteronomy is a collection of his final speeches, a long sermon in which he reminds a new generation what God has done and calls them to love him in the land they are about to receive. The name means "second law," because Moses restates and applies the instruction given at Sinai to people who were not there to hear it.

At its heart is a short declaration Jews call the Shema, after its first word in Hebrew, "Hear." "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one. And you shall love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your strength" (Deuteronomy 6:4-5). The next verses tell parents to teach it to their children, to speak of it at home and on the road, to bind it on hand and forehead. Jesus quotes this passage when he is asked which commandment is greatest (Matthew 22:36-40).

Scholars have long noticed that the book's shape resembles ancient treaties between a great king and a smaller nation. It opens with a history of the relationship, lays out the terms, calls on witnesses, and ends with blessings for loyalty and curses for betrayal. That fits Deuteronomy's own understanding: this is the covenant between God and Israel put into words.

The climax is a choice. Moses says that the word is not far off, and he sets before them "life and prosperity, as well as death and disaster," urging them, "choose life, so that you and your descendants may live" (Deuteronomy 30:15-19). The book ends with Moses on Mount Nebo, viewing the whole land, dying in Moab and buried by God himself, and a short verse that says no prophet since has been like him.

# Joshua and the land
@ref JOS 1:6-9; JOS 2:8-13; JOS 6:1-5; JOS 24:14-15
@conf debated

Joshua, Moses' assistant, takes the lead and crosses the Jordan with the people. God tells him three times to be strong and courageous, and to keep the book of the law on his lips. The first city is Jericho, and the way it falls is strange. For six days the army marches once around the walls in silence, with seven priests carrying trumpets and the ark. On the seventh day they march seven times, the trumpets sound, the people shout, and the wall collapses (Joshua 6).

Before that, two spies had stayed in the house of a woman named Rahab, a Canaanite. She tells them that her people have heard what God did at the Red Sea and that she believes "the LORD your God is God in the heavens above and on the earth below" (Joshua 2:11). She helps them escape, and she and her family are spared. She later appears in the family tree of Jesus (Matthew 1:5), and Hebrews commends her faith.

The book then describes campaigns in the central, southern and northern parts of the land, and the dividing of territory among the tribes. It says plainly that Canaanite cities were to be put under the ban, a Hebrew term for being devoted to destruction, as an act of judgment on peoples whose sins God had been waiting out for centuries (compare Genesis 15:16). This is the hardest material in the book to read. Christians have taken it in different ways. Some stress that this was a unique, time-bound judgment that cannot serve as a pattern for any later war. Some argue that the language is partly stylized and that the text elsewhere says the peoples were not fully driven out. All agree that no New Testament writer treats it as a model for how Christians should deal with their neighbors.

Joshua ends with a gathering at Shechem where the old leader tells the people, "choose for yourselves this day whom you will serve," and then adds, "as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD" (Joshua 24:15).

# The cycle of the Judges
@ref JDG 2:6-19; JDG 6:11-16; JDG 16:28-30; JDG 21:25
@q Where do you see the cycle of Judges in your own life or in the culture around you?

Judges covers the long stretch between Joshua and the first king, and it is one of the bleakest books in the Bible. The writer explains the pattern in chapter 2. After Joshua's generation dies, a new generation arises "who did not know the LORD or the works that He had done for Israel" (Judges 2:10). The people start worshiping the Baals, the local gods of Canaan, and the LORD gives them into the hand of raiders and oppressors. They cry out, God raises up a judge to rescue them, and while the judge lives there is peace. Then the judge dies, and the people sink back lower than before.

That cycle of sin, oppression, crying out, rescue and rest repeats at least six times, and each repetition is worse. The judges themselves decline. Othniel and Ehud are clear heroes. Deborah is a prophet who leads with Barak and wins a famous victory. Gideon starts as a timid man threshing wheat in a winepress, defeats an army with three hundred men and torches in jars, and then makes a golden object that becomes a snare. Jephthah makes a tragic vow. Samson, set apart from birth as a Nazirite, is a man of enormous strength and almost no self-control, who dies bringing down a temple on himself and on the Philistines.

The book closes with two ghastly stories of idolatry and violence within Israel itself, and with a sentence repeated in the last chapter: "In those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25).

That line sets up what follows. Israel needs more than a series of temporary rescuers. It needs a king who will lead faithfully, and ultimately a deliverer whose rescue does not wear off when he dies.

# Ruth: a redeemer in dark days
@ref RUT 1:16-17; RUT 2:1-12; RUT 3:9-13; RUT 4:13-17; MAT 1:5
@conf scholars

Ruth begins "in the days when the judges ruled," and so it is a small window of light in an otherwise dark age. A famine drives Elimelech and Naomi from Bethlehem to Moab, where their two sons marry Moabite women. Then the men all die, and Naomi is left a widow with two daughters-in-law. She decides to go home. One daughter-in-law, Orpah, turns back. The other, Ruth, clings to her and says, "wherever you go, I will go, and wherever you live, I will live; your people will be my people, and your God will be my God" (Ruth 1:16).

In Bethlehem the two women are destitute, and Ruth goes into the barley fields to pick up the leftover grain that the law allowed the poor to gather. By God's providence she lands in the field of Boaz, a relative of Naomi's dead husband, who treats her with extraordinary kindness. Following Naomi's plan, Ruth later lies down at Boaz's feet on the threshing floor and asks him to spread his cloak over her.

Boaz is what Israelite law called a kinsman-redeemer, the Hebrew go'el: a close relative who could buy back family land and marry a widow to preserve her husband's line. At the town gate he deals with a nearer relative who passes on the duty, and then he marries Ruth. They have a son, Obed, who becomes the grandfather of David.

The last lines of the book give the genealogy. A Moabite woman, a foreigner from a people Israel had good reasons to distrust, stands in the family line of the greatest king, and so in the line of Jesus (Matthew 1:5). Ruth shows that the promise to Abraham was always meant for outsiders, and that God's redeemer is a relative who pays what is owed to bring a stranger home.

# Hannah, Samuel and the demand for a king
@ref 1SA 1:9-20; 1SA 2:1-10; 1SA 3:1-10; 1SA 8:4-9; LUK 1:46-55
@conf scholars

First Samuel opens with a woman in pain. Hannah is married to Elkanah, and she has no children. Year after year she goes to the sanctuary at Shiloh and weeps, and one year she prays so intensely that Eli the priest thinks she is drunk. She vows that if God gives her a son she will give him back. Samuel is born. When he is weaned she brings him to Shiloh to serve in the house of the LORD, and her song of thanks, which says God lifts the poor and brings down the mighty, is echoed centuries later in Mary's Magnificat (Luke 1:46-55).

Samuel grows up in a corrupt age. Eli's sons are priests who steal from the offerings, and the word of the LORD is rare. One night as a boy Samuel hears a voice, and after Eli explains who is speaking, he answers, "Speak, for Your servant is listening" (1 Samuel 3:10). Samuel becomes a prophet, a priest and the last of the judges. Israel loses a battle to the Philistines, brings out the ark of the covenant as a kind of good luck charm, and loses both the battle and the ark.

When Samuel grows old and his sons prove unworthy, the elders come to him and say, "appoint a king to judge us like all the other nations" (1 Samuel 8:5). Samuel is displeased, and God tells him the real meaning of the request: "it is not you they have rejected, but they have rejected Me as their king" (1 Samuel 8:7). God tells Samuel to warn them about what a king will take, from their sons, daughters, fields and flocks. They insist. The desire for a king is not wrong in itself, since God had foreseen it in Deuteronomy 17, but the motive is, because they want to be like everyone else.

# Saul: the king the people wanted
@ref 1SA 9:1-2; 1SA 10:1; 1SA 13:8-14; 1SA 15:22-23; 1SA 31:1-6
@conf scholars

Saul looks like a king. He is the son of a wealthy man from the tribe of Benjamin, handsome, and a head taller than anyone else in Israel. He is out looking for lost donkeys when he meets Samuel, who anoints him privately with oil, and later he is chosen by lot at Mizpah, where Saul is found hiding among the baggage. His early reign goes well. He rescues the town of Jabesh Gilead from an Ammonite threat, and the people shout for joy.

The trouble begins with two failures of obedience. In 1 Samuel 13 the Philistine army is gathering at Gilgal, Samuel has not come at the appointed time, and Saul, with his men beginning to scatter, offers the sacrifice himself, which was a priest's task. Samuel arrives and tells him that his kingdom will not last: "the LORD has sought a man after His own heart" (1 Samuel 13:14). Later God tells Saul to destroy the Amalekites completely, and Saul spares their king and the best of the animals, then claims he kept them to sacrifice. Samuel's answer is one of the great sentences in the Bible: "obedience is better than sacrifice" (1 Samuel 15:22).

After this the Spirit of the LORD leaves Saul, and a tormenting spirit troubles him. He becomes jealous and paranoid, especially of David, and spends years hunting a man who has done him no wrong. He consults a medium on the night before his final battle, and dies on Mount Gilboa by his own sword, together with his sons including Jonathan.

Saul's story is a warning about outward success without inward trust. He has the right look, the right start and the Spirit's help for a while, but he cannot bring himself to obey.

# David: a king after God's heart
@ref 1SA 16:7; 1SA 17:45-47; 2SA 5:1-5; ACT 13:22
@conf scholars

When God sends Samuel to Bethlehem to anoint a new king, Samuel looks at Jesse's oldest son and assumes he is the one. God corrects him with a principle that runs through the whole Bible: "the LORD does not see as man does. For man sees the outward appearance, but the LORD sees the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7). The chosen son is David, the youngest, away tending the sheep.

David first enters Saul's court as a musician who calms the king with his harp. Then comes Goliath, the Philistine champion, nine feet tall in the traditional Hebrew text (some ancient manuscripts give a shorter height), who has been taunting the army of Israel for forty days. David goes out with a sling and five smooth stones and says to him, "You come against me with sword and spear and javelin, but I come against you in the name of the LORD of Hosts" (1 Samuel 17:45). The stone strikes Goliath in the forehead.

The years that follow are dangerous. Saul turns against him, and David lives as a fugitive in caves and wilderness hideouts, twice sparing Saul's life when he could have killed him. After Saul's death, David is crowned king of Judah in Hebron, and about seven years later the northern tribes also make him king. According to the book of 2 Samuel, he reigned forty years, probably from around 1010 to 970 BC. He captures the Jebusite stronghold of Jerusalem, makes it his capital, and brings the ark there.

Paul sums up David this way: God said, "I have found David son of Jesse a man after My own heart; he will carry out My will in its entirety" (Acts 13:22). That does not mean David was without sin. It means his heart was turned toward God, as his psalms show, and that when he failed he came back in repentance.

# The covenant with David
@ref 2SA 7:1-16; PSA 89:3-4; MAT 1:1; LUK 1:30-33
@conf scholars

David is settled in his palace in Jerusalem, and he feels uncomfortable that the ark of God sits in a tent. He tells the prophet Nathan that he wants to build a house for God. Nathan first says go ahead, then that night God corrects him with a message that turns the whole idea upside down. David wants to build God a house. God says that he will build David a house, meaning a dynasty.

The promise in 2 Samuel 7 has several parts. God will make David's name great. He will give Israel a secure home. He will raise up one of David's descendants after him, who will build a house for God's name. And then comes the center: "I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever" (2 Samuel 7:13). God adds that he will be a father to that son, and if the son does wrong, God will discipline him, but "My loving devotion will never be removed" as it was from Saul (2 Samuel 7:15).

This covenant becomes the foundation of the hope for a Messiah, a word that means "anointed one." After the kingdom divides, falls and goes into exile, the prophets keep returning to the promise: a future king from David's line who will rule with justice and peace. The Psalms recall it (Psalm 89), and Isaiah and Jeremiah and Ezekiel and Micah all depend on it.

The New Testament opens with it. Matthew's first line calls Jesus "the son of David, the son of Abraham" (Matthew 1:1). When the angel appears to Mary, he says that her son will be given "the throne of His father David," and that "His kingdom will never end" (Luke 1:32-33). The apostles preach the resurrection as the proof that God has set this king on his throne.

# David's fall and what it cost
@ref 2SA 11:1-5; 2SA 12:1-13; PSA 51:1-4; PSA 32:1-5
@conf scholars

The most troubling chapters about David come in the middle of his reign. In the spring, when kings go to war, David stays home in Jerusalem. From his roof he sees a woman bathing, finds out she is Bathsheba, the wife of Uriah the Hittite, one of his own soldiers, and sends for her. She becomes pregnant. David first tries to cover it up by bringing Uriah home from the front so that he will sleep with his wife, and when Uriah refuses to enjoy comforts while his men are in the field, David sends him back with a sealed letter ordering the commander to put him at the front line and withdraw. Uriah is killed.

The chapter ends with one short sentence: "the thing that David had done was evil in the sight of the LORD" (2 Samuel 11:27). God sends Nathan, who tells David a story about a rich man who took a poor man's only lamb. David is furious at the man, and Nathan says, "You are that man!" David answers with six words, "I have sinned against the LORD" (2 Samuel 12:13). Nathan tells him the sin is forgiven, but the consequences will remain. The child born to Bathsheba dies, and "the sword will never depart from your house."

The cost is visible in the rest of the story: the rape of David's daughter Tamar, the murder of Amnon by Absalom, and Absalom's revolt, which sends David running from Jerusalem. Yet out of this ruin came a prayer that the church has used ever since. Psalm 51, headed as David's psalm after Nathan came to him, pleads, "Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me" (Psalm 51:10).

# Solomon: wisdom, a temple, and a slow drift
@ref 1KI 3:5-14; 1KI 6:1-2; 1KI 8:22-30; 1KI 11:1-6
@conf scholars

Solomon becomes king after David and, early in his reign, goes to Gibeon to offer sacrifices. That night God appears to him in a dream and says, "Ask, and I will give it to you!" Solomon asks for a discerning heart to judge the people. God is so pleased that he adds the wealth and honor Solomon did not ask for. The wisdom is soon put to the test in the famous case of two women and one living baby, and in the years that follow Solomon is known across the ancient world for his proverbs and songs.

His greatest project is the temple in Jerusalem. According to 1 Kings 6:1, he began it in the fourth year of his reign, which most calculate as about 966 BC, and it took seven years to complete. It was built on the hill north of David's city, modeled on the tabernacle's plan but on a much grander scale, with cedar from Lebanon and gold overlay. At its dedication the ark was placed in the inner room, and a cloud filled the house so that the priests could not stand to minister. Solomon's prayer asks, "will God indeed dwell upon the earth? The heavens, even the highest heavens, cannot contain You, much less this temple I have built" (1 Kings 8:27).

The ending is sad. Solomon marries many foreign women, and as he grows old they turn his heart toward other gods. He builds high places for the gods of his wives. God tells him that the kingdom will be torn from his son. A man who began by asking for wisdom ended by ignoring the very law the king was told to copy and read, which warned against many wives and many horses (Deuteronomy 17:16-17).

# The kingdom splits
@ref 1KI 11:29-39; 1KI 12:1-20; 1KI 12:26-30; 2KI 17:21-23
@conf scholars

After Solomon's death around 931 BC, his son Rehoboam goes to Shechem to be crowned by all Israel. The northern tribes ask him to ease the heavy labor and taxes of his father's reign. The older advisors tell him to give them a kind answer. The young men he grew up with tell him to say, "Whereas my father burdened you with a heavy yoke, I will add to your yoke." Rehoboam takes the second advice, and the north responds with a cry that echoes David's earlier rebellion: "To your tents, O Israel!" (1 Kings 12:16).

The nation breaks into two kingdoms. The ten northern tribes call themselves Israel, with Jeroboam as their king. The two southern tribes, Judah and Benjamin, remain loyal to David's family, and are called Judah, with Jerusalem as their capital. A prophet had told Jeroboam beforehand that God would do this because of Solomon's idolatry, and had promised him a lasting dynasty if he would keep God's ways.

Jeroboam is afraid that if his people keep going to the temple in Jerusalem to worship, they will return to Rehoboam. So he sets up two golden calves at Bethel and Dan, the southern and northern ends of his territory, and says, "Here, O Israel, are your gods, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt" (1 Kings 12:28). He also creates a new priesthood and a new festival. The charge that follows him through the rest of Kings is that later northern kings repeated the sin of Jeroboam, who caused Israel to sin.

Every northern king after him is judged to have done evil, and none of the dynasties lasts long. The southern kings are a mixture, with a few faithful rulers among many unfaithful ones, but David's line continues without a break until the exile.

# Elijah, Elisha and the contest on Carmel
@ref 1KI 17:1; 1KI 18:20-39; 1KI 19:9-13; 2KI 2:9-12
@conf scholars

In the ninth century BC the northern kingdom is ruled by Ahab and his Phoenician wife Jezebel, who promote the worship of Baal, the Canaanite storm and fertility god, and who kill many of the LORD's prophets. Into this comes Elijah, a prophet from Gilead, who suddenly tells Ahab that there will be neither dew nor rain in the next years except at his word. A three-year drought follows. The contest is set up as a direct test of who controls the rain.

On Mount Carmel, Elijah faces 450 prophets of Baal before the king and the people. He tells them to prepare a bull for sacrifice and call on their god to light it, while he does the same for the LORD. The prophets of Baal shout and cut themselves from morning until evening, and nothing happens. Elijah mocks them. Then he rebuilds the altar of the LORD, soaks the offering with water three times, and prays a short prayer. Fire falls and consumes the sacrifice, the wood, the stones and the water. The people fall on their faces and say, "The LORD, He is God!" (1 Kings 18:39). Then rain comes.

Elijah is not a man of steel, though. Jezebel threatens his life, and he runs into the desert and asks to die. At Mount Horeb God does not speak in the wind, the earthquake or the fire, but in "a still, small voice" (1 Kings 19:12).

Elijah never dies. He is carried up to heaven in a whirlwind with a chariot of fire, and his disciple Elisha, who asks for a double share of his spirit, takes his place. Malachi's last words promise that Elijah will return before the great day of the LORD, and the New Testament says that John the Baptist came "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17).
