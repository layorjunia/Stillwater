# Luther and the Ninety-five Theses
@ref ROM 1:16-17; EPH 2:8-9; GAL 2:16
@conf scholars

Martin Luther was born in 1483 in Eisleben, Germany, the son of a miner. He was studying law in 1505 when a violent thunderstorm threw him to the ground, and he cried out a vow to become a monk if he were spared. He entered an Augustinian monastery at Erfurt, where he fasted, confessed for hours and tormented himself over whether he was good enough for God. Later he became a professor of Bible at the new university in Wittenberg, and as he lectured on the Psalms and on Romans, a discovery slowly changed his life. He saw that the righteousness of God in Romans 1:17 was not the standard that condemns but the gift that saves, given to those who trust Christ.

Meanwhile, the church was raising funds for the new St. Peter's Basilica in Rome by selling indulgences, documents that were understood to reduce the time a soul would spend in purgatory. The Dominican preacher Johann Tetzel, who sold them near Wittenberg, was said to use the line "As soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs." Luther was alarmed that people thought they could buy forgiveness. According to tradition, on 31 October 1517 he nailed a list of ninety-five theses for debate to the door of the castle church in Wittenberg. Historians doubt the nailing, but they agree that he sent the theses to the archbishop that day.

They were quickly translated and printed, and within weeks they were known across Germany. Luther did not set out to start a new church. He wanted to reform the existing one. But in 1520 the pope threatened him with excommunication, and he burned the bull in public. The reform movement now had a name, and his ideas, justification by faith alone, Scripture alone as the final authority, and the priesthood of all believers, became the heart of Protestantism.

# Worms, the Wartburg and the German Bible
@ref ACT 5:29; 2TI 3:16-17; PSA 119:130
@conf scholars

In April 1521 Luther was summoned to the imperial assembly, called the Diet, at Worms, before the young emperor Charles V. His books were laid out on a table and he was asked whether he would take back what he had written. After a night's reflection he answered that unless he was convinced by Scripture or by plain reason, he would not recant, for his conscience was captive to the word of God. According to a printed account, he ended with the words "Here I stand. I cannot do otherwise." Scholars doubt that he said the last sentences, but they agree about the refusal.

The emperor declared him an outlaw. On his way home, friends of the Elector of Saxony pretended to kidnap him and took him to the Wartburg Castle, where he hid for ten months disguised as a knight called Junker Jörg. There he did the work that probably affected Germany more than any other: he translated the New Testament from Greek into German in about eleven weeks. It was printed in September 1522, and thousands of copies sold. He and his colleagues completed the whole Bible in 1534.

His German was vivid and plain, taken from the speech of ordinary people, and his translation did much to shape the written language itself. He insisted that the Bible should be in the language of the plowman, and he loved the idea that every Christian could read it. He married Katharina von Bora, a former nun, in 1525, and the Lutheran parsonage became a model for the family life of the pastor. He wrote hymns, including "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God." He died in 1546, in the town where he was born. The Reformation had already spread beyond his control.

# Zwingli, Calvin and the Reformed churches
@ref ROM 8:28-30; PSA 115:1; 1CO 10:31
@conf scholars

At almost the same time as Luther's movement in Germany, another stream of reform arose in the Swiss cities. Huldrych Zwingli, a priest in Zurich from 1519, began to preach straight through the Gospels and then through the New Testament, applying it to the life of the city. He rejected practices he could not find in the Bible, such as fasting rules and images in churches, and he was killed in 1531 in a battle between Protestant and Catholic cantons. His death left the work to others.

John Calvin was a French lawyer's son born in 1509, who studied humanities and law in Paris and underwent what he called a sudden conversion. In 1536, aged twenty-six, he published the first edition of his Institutes of the Christian Religion, a clear, orderly statement of Protestant belief that he went on to revise until the final edition of 1559. That same year the reformer Guillaume Farel persuaded him to stay in Geneva, from which he was expelled in 1538 and recalled in 1541. He was a pastor, a teacher of pastors and a writer of commentaries on most of the Bible.

Geneva became a model city and a school for reformers from across Europe, including John Knox, who took its pattern back to Scotland. Calvin taught that God's glory is the end of everything, that salvation is wholly by grace, and that the whole of life is lived before God. His followers are called the Reformed. They produced the Heidelberg Catechism in 1563 and later the Westminster Confession, and their churches are now found in Presbyterian, Reformed and many other denominations.

Calvin also has a dark side in the story. In 1553 Geneva executed the Spanish physician Michael Servetus for denying the Trinity. Calvin agreed with the verdict, though he asked for a less cruel death than burning. Even those who honor Calvin generally regard this as a failure of the age that he shared. The chief lesson is that reform of belief is not the same as reform of the heart.

# The radical Reformation
@ref ACT 2:38-41; MAT 5:33-37; JHN 18:36
@conf scholars

Luther, Zwingli and Calvin all kept some link between church and state, and all baptized infants. A group of younger men in Zurich who had studied with Zwingli concluded that this did not follow Scripture. They argued that the church should be a community of voluntary believers and that baptism belonged only to those who confess faith. On 21 January 1525 Conrad Grebel baptized George Blaurock in a private home, and then Blaurock baptized others. Their opponents called them Anabaptists, "re-baptizers," since they had all been baptized as infants.

The movement spread quickly and was fiercely persecuted, by Catholics and by Protestants alike. Felix Manz, one of the founders, was drowned in the Limmat River in Zurich in 1527, by the authorities of a city that called itself reformed. Thousands were killed over the next decades. Early leaders drew up the Schleitheim Confession in 1527, which called for adult baptism, separation from the world, the refusal to swear oaths and a rejection of the sword.

Most Anabaptists were peaceful, but in 1534 a group took over the German city of Münster and tried to set up a violent, apocalyptic kingdom, which was crushed the next year. That episode damaged the reputation of the entire movement for generations. The peaceful tradition was gathered by a Dutch priest, Menno Simons, whose followers became the Mennonites. Others became the Hutterites and the Amish.

Their descendants include the Mennonites, Brethren, and in some respects the Baptists, who arose in England in the early seventeenth century. Their heritage, in the ideas of believers' baptism, religious freedom, and the separation of church and state, has spread much more widely than their numbers would suggest. Many Christians who are not Anabaptist have learned from their emphasis on following Jesus' teaching about peace and about the cost of discipleship.

# England: Tyndale, Cranmer and the church of the nation
@ref 2TI 3:15-17; ISA 40:8; HEB 4:12
@conf scholars

The English Reformation started for political reasons. When Pope Clement VII would not annul Henry VIII's marriage to Catherine of Aragon, Parliament passed the Act of Supremacy in 1534, making the king head of the Church of England. But beneath the politics were real reformers.

William Tyndale was a scholar who wanted every English plowboy to know the Scriptures better than the clergy. When the church forbade English translations, he left for the Continent, and in 1526 his New Testament was printed at Worms and smuggled into England in bales of cloth. He had begun translating the Old Testament from the Hebrew when he was betrayed, arrested near Brussels and, in October 1536, strangled and burned. His reported last words were a prayer, "Lord, open the king of England's eyes." Within three years, the king had authorized an English Bible, the Great Bible of 1539, much of it from Tyndale's work.

Thomas Cranmer, Archbishop of Canterbury from 1533, gave the church the Book of Common Prayer in 1549 and a revised one in 1552, in beautiful English that shaped the country's worship and speech. When the Catholic queen Mary took the throne in 1553, she restored the old faith and burned about 280 Protestants, including Cranmer in 1556, who held his right hand in the flames because it had signed a recantation. Mary's half-sister Elizabeth came to the throne in 1558, and the Elizabethan settlement of 1559 set up a church that was Protestant in doctrine and kept bishops and a liturgy.

The result was a church of the nation, which was a compromise that satisfied no one entirely. Some wanted further reform and were called Puritans. Others held on to the older ways. The tensions of that settlement would shape English and American history for centuries.

# The Catholic Reformation and Trent
@ref EPH 4:11-16; 2CO 4:1-2; MAT 28:19-20
@conf scholars

The Catholic Church did not stand still while Protestantism grew. In the sixteenth century it carried out its own internal reform, sometimes called the Counter-Reformation, and the better term is the Catholic Reformation, because many of its changes had begun before Luther.

The central event was the Council of Trent, which met in three phases between 1545 and 1563. It answered the Protestants point by point. It affirmed that Scripture and tradition together are the sources of revelation and that the Latin Vulgate is the authoritative text. It taught that justification involves inward renewal and cooperation with grace, and not faith alone. It confirmed seven sacraments and the doctrine of transubstantiation. It also reformed abuses: bishops had to live in their dioceses, seminaries were set up to train priests, and the sale of indulgences was curbed.

New orders arose, the most important of which was the Society of Jesus, the Jesuits, founded by a Spanish soldier, Ignatius of Loyola, and approved by the pope in 1540. They emphasized education, discipline and mission, and opened schools and universities across Europe. Francis Xavier, one of the first Jesuits, carried the gospel to India, Southeast Asia and Japan. Teresa of Avila and John of the Cross, Spanish mystics, renewed the life of prayer in the Carmelite order.

Catholic reform also fed a flowering of art and music in the baroque era, and a vast missionary expansion into the Americas, Africa and Asia. The result was that the church held Southern Europe, and Europe was left divided between Catholic and Protestant lands. Both sides fought a series of terrible wars, ending with the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, which in Germany alone had killed millions in the Thirty Years' War.

# The King James Bible
@ref PSA 23:1-6; 1CO 13:1-13; JHN 1:1-14
@conf scholars

When King James VI of Scotland became James I of England in 1603, the Puritans asked him for a new translation of the Bible. At a conference at Hampton Court in 1604 he agreed. The Geneva Bible, the favorite of the Puritans, had marginal notes that he disliked, and the official Bishops' Bible was not widely loved. He appointed about fifty scholars, in six companies working at Westminster, Oxford and Cambridge, to produce a version that all could use.

The translators were told to follow the Bishops' Bible and to consult the earlier English versions, and they drew heavily on Tyndale's work. Their method was careful. Each scholar translated a portion alone, then the company reviewed it together, and finally a small committee checked the result. The preface they wrote describes the aim of putting the word into a language the people could understand. The book was printed in 1611.

It took some decades to displace the Geneva Bible, but then it became the Bible of the English-speaking world for three centuries. Its cadence entered the language, in phrases such as "the skin of my teeth," "a still small voice," and "the powers that be." Its rhythm shaped English prose, poetry, and oratory. It is still loved for the beauty of passages such as the twenty-third Psalm, and by many for its dignity in worship.

Later discoveries of older manuscripts, and changes in English, led to many new translations, beginning with the Revised Version of 1881. The King James Version rested on a Greek text, the Textus Receptus, drawn from a handful of late manuscripts, and scholars now have much earlier ones. But the 1611 Bible remains one of the great works of English, and the hope of its translators, that ordinary people might read God's word, has been widely realized.

# The Puritans and the Westminster Assembly
@ref ROM 12:1-2; COL 3:17; 1CO 10:31
@conf scholars

The word Puritan began as an insult for English Protestants who wanted to purify the Church of England of what they saw as leftovers of Rome. Over time it came to describe a movement as much about the spiritual life as about church order. The Puritans stressed Bible reading, preaching, careful self-examination and a disciplined life in which every ordinary task was done for God, whether farming, trade or family duty.

They suffered for their convictions. Under James I and Charles I many were fined, imprisoned or driven from their pulpits. Some left. A group of separatists who had settled in Holland sailed on the Mayflower to Plymouth in 1620. A much larger Puritan migration followed from 1630 to Massachusetts Bay, where their governor John Winthrop spoke of the colony as "a city on a hill." Those colonies laid the foundations of much of New England.

At home the Puritans won power in the Civil War of the 1640s. The Westminster Assembly of divines, which met in London from 1643 to 1649, drew up the Westminster Confession of Faith in 1646 and the Larger and Shorter Catechisms in 1647. The Shorter Catechism begins with the question "What is the chief end of man?" and answers, "Man's chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him forever." These documents became the standard of Presbyterian churches around the world.

Puritan writers left a body of devotional literature that is still read today, including John Owen, Richard Baxter and Thomas Watson. John Bunyan, a tinker who spent years in Bedford jail, wrote The Pilgrim's Progress, published in 1678, which became one of the best-selling books in English. The Puritans had faults, including harshness and, at times, intolerance, but they also taught the church to take holiness and the whole of life seriously.

# Pietists and Moravians
@ref ROM 8:15-16; 1JN 4:7-12; JAS 2:14-17
@conf scholars

By the late seventeenth century, many Lutheran and Reformed churches in Germany had become dry, caught up in doctrinal quarrels while ordinary believers felt little warmth. In 1675 a Lutheran pastor in Frankfurt named Philipp Jakob Spener published Pia Desideria, "Pious Desires," a proposal for reform. He wanted small groups to meet for Bible study and prayer, lay people to share in the work of the church, and practical Christian living to count as much as correct belief. His movement was called Pietism.

His follower August Hermann Francke built a great complex at Halle, in Germany, with an orphanage, schools and a mission center, supported by faith and gifts. Pietism emphasized personal conversion, devotion and care for the poor, and it sent missionaries to India and elsewhere.

The most influential outgrowth was the Moravian community. In 1722 persecuted Protestants from Moravia found refuge on the estate of Count Nikolaus von Zinzendorf in Saxony, who let them build a village that they called Herrnhut. In 1727 the community experienced a profound spiritual renewal, and they began a round-the-clock prayer meeting that went on for more than a hundred years. In 1732 they sent out their first missionaries to the slaves of the Caribbean, and the Moravians soon became the first large Protestant missionary movement.

Their influence reached further than their size. John Wesley met Moravians on a ship to Georgia in 1735 during a storm, and was impressed by their calm, and Peter Böhler later helped him toward the faith he sought. Pietism and the Moravians changed Protestant Christianity by showing that the heart and the head belong together, and that mission is the work of the whole church.

# John Wesley and the Methodists
@ref ROM 5:1-5; 1TH 5:23-24; JAS 1:22-25
@conf scholars

John Wesley was born in 1703, one of the nineteen children of an Anglican clergyman and his remarkable wife Susanna. At Oxford he and his brother Charles led a group of students who met for prayer, Bible study and charitable work, and were mocked as the Holy Club, or "Methodists," for their methodical habits. Wesley went to Georgia as a missionary in 1735 and returned feeling a failure. "I went to America to convert the Indians," he wrote, "but oh, who shall convert me?"

On 24 May 1738, in a meeting on Aldersgate Street in London, while someone read Luther's preface to Romans, he felt his heart "strangely warmed." He knew that he did trust Christ alone for salvation, and that Christ had taken away his sins. The next year his friend George Whitefield invited him to preach in the open air to coal miners near Bristol, and though he thought it improper at first, he did it. For the next fifty years he rode and walked thousands of miles on horseback, preaching, forming societies and organizing classes for prayer and mutual accountability.

Wesley taught that salvation is for all, that it can be known, and that it leads to holiness of heart and life. He cared for the poor, set up schools and clinics, opposed slavery and wrote on health. His brother Charles wrote thousands of hymns, including "Hark! The Herald Angels Sing" and "O for a Thousand Tongues to Sing."

Wesley never wanted to leave the Church of England, and he died in 1791 still a priest. But after his death the Methodist societies became a separate church. Methodism spread across Britain and especially America, where it became one of the largest Protestant bodies, and it later gave rise to the Holiness and Pentecostal movements.

# The Great Awakening
@ref ACT 2:37-41; JHN 3:3-8; 2CO 5:17
@conf scholars

In the 1730s and 1740s a wave of religious revival swept through the English-speaking world, known in America as the Great Awakening. It began in the congregation of Jonathan Edwards, a pastor in Northampton, Massachusetts. In 1734 and 1735 many people in his town became deeply concerned about their souls. Edwards, who was an acute thinker and wrote with great care, described what he saw in A Faithful Narrative of the Surprising Work of God.

The revival took off with the arrival of George Whitefield, a young Anglican preacher from England with a powerful voice, who toured the colonies from 1739 to 1741, preaching outdoors to crowds of thousands. Benjamin Franklin, who did not share his beliefs, calculated that Whitefield could be heard by about thirty thousand people at once. The effect spread across denominations and colonies, and people from different backgrounds heard the same message of new birth.

Edwards's most famous sermon, "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God," preached in Enfield, Connecticut, in July 1741, is often remembered for its imagery of judgment. But he was a theologian of the beauty of God, and his writings on religious affections and on the true marks of a work of the Spirit remain valuable. The Awakening also had a downside: it divided churches between those who welcomed the revival and those who resisted it, and it produced some extremes.

A second wave, the Second Great Awakening, came after 1800, with camp meetings such as the one at Cane Ridge, Kentucky, in 1801. Together, the awakenings reshaped American Christianity. They made personal conversion central, they helped Baptists and Methodists become the largest denominations, and they fed a wave of mission, education and social reform.

# William Carey and the century of missions
@ref MAT 28:18-20; ROM 10:13-15; ISA 54:2-3
@conf scholars

At the end of the eighteenth century, most Protestants did not think of sending missionaries overseas. Some argued that the Great Commission had been given only to the apostles. A shoemaker and Baptist pastor in England changed that. William Carey, born in 1761 in a village in Northamptonshire, taught himself Greek, Hebrew and Latin while mending shoes, and he kept a map of the world on the wall of his workshop.

In 1792 he published An Enquiry into the Obligations of Christians to Use Means for the Conversion of the Heathens, and preached a sermon that is summed up in the line "Expect great things from God; attempt great things for God." That year the Baptist Missionary Society was formed. In 1793 Carey sailed with his family to India. He was there for forty years and never returned home.

The early years were hard. His son died of dysentery, his wife suffered a mental breakdown, and it was seven years before his first convert. But Carey worked on. He translated the Bible, in whole or in part, into dozens of Indian languages, founded a college at Serampore, campaigned against the burning of widows, and wrote grammars and dictionaries. Others followed: Adoniram Judson to Burma in 1813, Robert Morrison to China in 1807, Robert Moffat and David Livingstone to Africa, and Hudson Taylor, who founded the China Inland Mission in 1865 and urged missionaries to dress like the people they served.

The nineteenth century is sometimes called the great century of Christian missions. The work was often mixed up with Western colonial power, and missionaries made mistakes. But many of them gave their lives, brought the Bible to peoples who had no written language and opened schools and hospitals. Their work is the reason that Christianity is now a global faith.

# The church and slavery
@ref GAL 3:28; PHM 1:15-16; EXO 21:16; 1TI 1:9-10
@conf scholars

The history of the church and slavery is painful, because Christians were on both sides, and each side cited the Bible. For centuries many Christians owned slaves and defended the practice, often by pointing to the regulation of slavery in the Old Testament and to Paul's instruction that slaves obey their masters. Slave ships sailed under Christian flags, and ministers preached to slaveholders. At the same time, the strongest voices for abolition were also Christian, and they drew on the Bible too.

John Newton, a slave ship captain, was converted after a violent storm in 1748, and later became a clergyman and wrote the hymn "Amazing Grace," published in 1779. Late in life he publicly repented of his part in the trade. William Wilberforce, an evangelical Member of Parliament, led the campaign to end the slave trade through some twenty years of defeats, until the Slave Trade Act of 1807, and the bill to abolish slavery itself was moving through Parliament when he died in 1833. He worked with a circle of friends known as the Clapham Sect, and with Quakers who had opposed slavery from the beginning.

In America, the enslaved people themselves transformed Christianity. They heard in the story of the exodus a promise of their own deliverance, and they sang it in the spirituals. Richard Allen, a freed slave who had been pulled from his knees in prayer at a white church in Philadelphia, founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church in 1816. The black church became a center of community life and later of the civil rights movement.

The lesson of this history is not just about the past. The same Bible was read in opposite ways, and the difference was often what the readers wanted to hear. Christians now almost universally agree that slavery is a sin, citing texts such as Paul's statement that in Christ there is neither slave nor free. The episode is a caution to read Scripture humbly, and with an ear for those who suffer.

# Doubt, criticism and fundamentalism
@ref ACT 17:11; 1TH 5:21; JUD 1:3
@conf scholars

In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries new currents of thought challenged the church in the West. The Enlightenment elevated human reason, and many scholars began to question miracles and the authority of the Bible. Friedrich Schleiermacher, a German pastor and professor who died in 1834, tried to answer the skeptics by defining religion as a feeling of dependence on God, and he is called the father of liberal theology. German scholars applied the methods of historical criticism to the Bible, asking about sources and dates, and at times concluded that large parts of it were not what they claimed to be.

Charles Darwin published On the Origin of Species in 1859, which raised new questions about how to understand Genesis. Some Christians welcomed the theory of evolution and others resisted it. The debates grew bitter, and a conservative reaction took shape. Between 1910 and 1915 a series of twelve booklets called The Fundamentals, financed by two California businessmen, set out the essential doctrines, including the inspiration of Scripture, the virgin birth, the atonement and the resurrection. Those who held them were called fundamentalists. The Scopes trial of 1925, over the teaching of evolution in Tennessee, made them objects of ridicule in the press, though the trial settled little.

The twentieth century brought other responses. The Swiss theologian Karl Barth, shocked by the support of liberal theologians for the German war effort in 1914, published a commentary on Romans in 1919 that stressed the sovereignty and otherness of God and began what was called neo-orthodoxy. Evangelicals, such as Billy Graham, carried on the conservative tradition in a more open way. Many Catholic scholars also joined the work of critical study.

Today Christians still disagree about how far to use historical criticism and how to relate Genesis and science. The good that came from it was that the church had to ask hard questions, and that faith that cannot take questions is not strong. The Bible's own advice is to test everything and hold to what is good.

# Bonhoeffer and the church under Hitler
@ref MAT 16:24-26; ROM 13:1-7; ACT 5:29
@conf scholars

In 1933 Adolf Hitler came to power in Germany, and many in the Protestant churches welcomed him. A group of pastors and laity, called the German Christians, tried to bring the church into line with Nazi ideas, including the exclusion of Christians of Jewish descent. A minority resisted. In 1934 they met at Barmen and issued a declaration, written mainly by Karl Barth, which said that Jesus Christ is the one Word of God that Christians must hear and obey, and that no other power, state or leader may claim their ultimate loyalty. They became known as the Confessing Church.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer was a young theologian, born in 1906, who joined the resistance from the first. He warned against the cult of the Führer in a radio talk two days after Hitler became chancellor, and he ran an illegal seminary for the Confessing Church at Finkenwalde. His book The Cost of Discipleship, published in 1937, distinguished "cheap grace," forgiveness without repentance, from "costly grace," which calls us to follow Christ. He was offered a safe place in New York in 1939 but chose to return to Germany.

Eventually he joined a conspiracy that planned to remove Hitler, which was a painful moral decision for a man committed to peace. He was arrested in April 1943, held in prison for two years, and executed on 9 April 1945 at Flossenbürg, a few weeks before the war ended. His letters from prison, published as Letters and Papers from Prison, are still read widely.

Bonhoeffer's story is held up as an example of costly discipleship, and also as a warning of what happens when the church is silent. Many German Christians went along with evil, and others resisted at great cost. His own words from The Cost of Discipleship are often quoted: "When Christ calls a man, he bids him come and die."

# Pentecostals, Vatican II and the push for unity
@ref ACT 2:1-4; JHN 17:20-23; EPH 4:3-6
@conf scholars

The twentieth century saw some of the greatest changes in the church since the Reformation. The first was the rise of Pentecostalism. A Bible school in Topeka, Kansas, in 1901 saw students speaking in tongues, and in 1906 a revival at a former church on Azusa Street in Los Angeles, led by the African American preacher William Seymour, drew worshipers of every race and spread across the world. Within a century Pentecostal and charismatic Christians numbered hundreds of millions, concentrated in Latin America, Africa and Asia.

A second change was the movement toward Christian unity. Missionaries had seen how absurd it was to export Western divisions to the mission field, and in 1910 a great missionary conference in Edinburgh began a series of efforts that led to the founding of the World Council of Churches in 1948. Evangelicals met at the Lausanne Congress on World Evangelization in 1974 and set out a common vision.

A third was the Second Vatican Council, which Pope John XXIII opened in 1962 and which ended in 1965 under Paul VI. It allowed the Mass to be celebrated in local languages, encouraged Catholics to read the Bible, said that other Christians are brothers and sisters in Christ, and affirmed religious freedom. It marked the end of centuries of hostility between Catholics and Protestants, and today they cooperate in many ways, while serious differences remain.

The century also brought severe persecution. In the Soviet Union, China, and many other places, thousands of Christians were imprisoned or killed, and by some estimates the twentieth century saw more Christian martyrs than all earlier centuries together. Yet the church survived, and in places grew. Jesus' prayer in John 17, "that all of them may be one," is still being answered in part, but not completely.

# A global faith
@ref REV 7:9-10; MAT 28:19; ACT 1:8

Today about 2.3 to 2.4 billion people, roughly three in ten of the world's population, identify as Christians, which makes it the largest religion in the world. The story of the last century is a story of change in the shape of the church. In 1910 about two out of three Christians lived in Europe. A hundred years later fewer than three in ten did. The center of gravity has moved south and east.

Sub-Saharan Africa now has one of the largest Christian populations of any region, growing from about nine million in 1910 to hundreds of millions today. Latin America is home to a large share of the world's Catholics. In Asia, Christianity is growing fast in places such as South Korea, where roughly a quarter to a third of the people are Christian, and in China, where the number of Protestants is estimated at several tens of millions and much of the growth takes place in house churches outside state control. Meanwhile, in Europe and in parts of North America, church attendance has declined.

The face of the church is changing. The typical Christian today is not a white European but is more likely to be a young woman in Nigeria, Brazil or the Philippines. Worship is in thousands of languages. The Bible, in whole or in part, has been translated into over 3,000 languages, with the work of Bible translators continuing in many more.

This is a long way from a small group of Galilean fishermen, and yet John's vision in Revelation, of a multitude from every nation, tribe, people and language standing before the throne, is the goal of the story from Genesis 12. The task of every generation is to hand on the faith to the next, and no generation has been left without a witness.
