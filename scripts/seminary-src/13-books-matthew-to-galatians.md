# Matthew
@id book-mat
@ref MAT 1:1; MAT 5:1-12; MAT 28:18-20

Matthew has twenty-eight chapters and opens the New Testament. It is the Gospel most clearly written to show that Jesus is the long-promised Messiah of Israel, and it is full of quotations from the Old Testament, which Matthew says were fulfilled in Jesus' birth, ministry and death.

Early church tradition says the author was Matthew, also called Levi, the tax collector whom Jesus called to follow him. Many scholars date the Gospel between about AD 70 and 90, after Mark, whose outline it follows and expands, while others place it earlier. Its audience seems to have been Jewish believers who knew the Scriptures well, and its Greek is polished and orderly.

## How it is arranged
Matthew gathers Jesus' teaching into five great discourses, each ending with a phrase such as "When Jesus had finished saying these things."
- Chapters 1 and 2: genealogy, birth, the visit of the wise men, and flight to Egypt.
- Chapters 3 and 4: baptism and temptation.
- Chapters 5 to 7: the Sermon on the Mount.
- Chapters 8 to 10: miracles and the sending out of the Twelve.
- Chapters 11 to 13: growing opposition, and parables of the kingdom.
- Chapters 14 to 20: travels, the church (chapter 18), and teaching on the way to Jerusalem.
- Chapters 21 to 25: the last week of teaching, ending with the Olivet discourse.
- Chapters 26 to 28: the arrest, trial, crucifixion and resurrection.

## What to look for
The genealogy puts Jesus in the line of Abraham and David, and the book answers the question of what the kingdom of heaven is like. It ends with a commission: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me," and therefore, go and make disciples of all nations. The first words in the Gospel say Jesus is "Immanuel," God with us, and the last say that he is with us to the end of the age.

# Mark
@id book-mrk
@ref MRK 1:1; MRK 8:27-30; MRK 10:42-45

Mark has sixteen chapters and is the shortest Gospel and, by the view of most scholars, the earliest. It moves quickly, using the word "immediately" again and again, and it gives a large share of its length to the last week of Jesus' life. Mark's Jesus is a man of action, who is also the suffering Son of God.

The author, according to a very early tradition, was John Mark, the companion of Peter, who wrote down what Peter preached. Mark appears in Acts and in Paul's letters. Most date the Gospel to about AD 55 to 70, probably written for Gentile readers in Rome, who are often given explanations of Jewish customs and Aramaic words.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 8: Jesus' ministry in Galilee and the surrounding regions, with miracles, parables and growing opposition, and ending with Peter's confession that Jesus is the Christ.
- Chapters 8 to 10: the road to Jerusalem, with three predictions of his death and teaching about discipleship.
- Chapters 11 to 16: Jerusalem, the last teaching, the cross and the empty tomb.

## What to look for
Mark keeps Jesus' identity half hidden. Demons recognize him and are told to be silent, and the disciples, who are slow to understand, only grasp it partway. At the midpoint Peter says, "You are the Christ," and Jesus at once begins to teach that the Son of Man must suffer. The turning point is 10:45: "the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many." Note too that the earliest manuscripts end at 16:8, with the women fleeing in fear, and the longer ending appears in later copies.

# Luke
@id book-luk
@ref LUK 1:1-4; LUK 15:11-24; LUK 19:10

Luke has twenty-four chapters and is the longest Gospel by word count, and it is the first volume of a two-part work, with Acts as the second. It is written in the best Greek of the New Testament, in the style of an educated historian, and it is the Gospel with the most stories about outsiders: women, the poor, Samaritans, tax collectors and sinners.

The author is Luke, whom Paul calls his dear friend and physician (Colossians 4:14), and who traveled with Paul on some of his journeys. He was probably a Gentile, the only one to write a book of the New Testament. In the preface he says that he has investigated everything from the beginning and is writing an orderly account for Theophilus, so that he may know the certainty of what he was taught. It was probably written between AD 60 and 80.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 and 2: the births of John the Baptist and Jesus, with songs from Mary, Zechariah and Simeon.
- Chapters 3 to 9: baptism, temptation and ministry in Galilee.
- Chapters 9 to 19: the long journey to Jerusalem, which holds most of the material found only in Luke, such as the Good Samaritan, the lost sheep and coin, the prodigal son, and Zacchaeus.
- Chapters 19 to 24: Jerusalem, the crucifixion, the resurrection and the road to Emmaus.

## What to look for
Luke is the Gospel of the seeking God. The key verse is 19:10: "the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost." Watch for prayer, joy, the Holy Spirit, and the way Jesus lifts up the overlooked. The three parables in chapter 15 are the heart of it. Because Luke goes on to write Acts, he shows how the story of Jesus continues through his church.

# John
@id book-jhn
@ref JHN 1:1-5; JHN 3:16; JHN 20:30-31
@conf scholars

John has twenty-one chapters and stands apart from Matthew, Mark and Luke. It has almost no parables, but long conversations, such as those with Nicodemus and the woman at the well, and seven "signs," or miracles. Seven times Jesus makes an "I am" statement, such as "I am the bread of life" and "I am the good shepherd." Its first sentence goes back before creation, to the Word who was with God and was God.

Tradition names the apostle John son of Zebedee, "the disciple whom Jesus loved," as the author, writing late in life from Ephesus, perhaps in the 80s or 90s AD. The Gospel does not name him, and some scholars suggest it came from a circle around him or from another John, but the church has always valued its eyewitness character. Its purpose is stated near the end.

## How it is arranged
- 1:1 to 18: the prologue, about the Word who became flesh.
- 1:19 to 12:50: the Book of Signs. Seven miracles, from water into wine to the raising of Lazarus, with the teaching that goes with each.
- Chapters 13 to 17: the upper room, where Jesus washes the disciples' feet, gives his farewell teaching and prays.
- Chapters 18 to 21: the arrest, crucifixion, resurrection and an appearance by the lake.

## What to look for
John says why he wrote: "these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in His name." Watch for the great contrasts of light and darkness, life and death, belief and unbelief. The Gospel says very plainly that Jesus is God, from the opening verse to Thomas's cry, "My Lord and my God!"

# Acts
@id book-act
@ref ACT 1:8; ACT 2:42-47; ACT 28:30-31

Acts has twenty-eight chapters and is the second volume of Luke's work, the only narrative book in the New Testament after the Gospels. Its full title, Acts of the Apostles, is a little misleading, since it focuses on Peter in the first half and Paul in the second. Many have said that it is better named the acts of the Holy Spirit through the apostles.

Luke wrote it, and in some stretches he uses "we," showing that he was present as a companion of Paul. The book ends with Paul under house arrest in Rome, probably around AD 60 to 62, which suggests it was finished about then, though some date it later. It covers about thirty years, from the ascension of Jesus about AD 30 to Paul's two years in Rome.

## How it is arranged
Acts 1:8 gives the outline: witnesses in Jerusalem, in Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth.
- Chapters 1 to 7: Jerusalem. The ascension, Pentecost, the early church, persecution, and the death of Stephen.
- Chapters 8 to 12: Judea and Samaria and beyond. Philip, Saul's conversion, Peter and Cornelius, and the church at Antioch.
- Chapters 13 to 20: Paul's three missionary journeys, and the council at Jerusalem in chapter 15.
- Chapters 21 to 28: Paul arrested in Jerusalem, tried before governors and a king, shipwrecked, and brought to Rome.

## What to look for
Watch the Spirit: he comes at Pentecost, falls on Samaritans and Gentiles, and sends out missionaries. Watch how the gospel crosses boundaries of culture and language. The ending is deliberate. Paul is in chains, and yet "boldly and freely he proclaimed the kingdom of God and taught about the Lord Jesus Christ." The word of God is not bound, and the story goes on.

# Romans
@id book-rom
@ref ROM 1:16-17; ROM 5:1-2; ROM 8:1-2; ROM 12:1-2

Romans has sixteen chapters and is Paul's longest and most systematic letter. It is the clearest explanation in the New Testament of what the gospel is, and it has shaped Christian thought more than any other book, from Augustine through Luther and Wesley.

Paul wrote it from Corinth, probably in the winter of AD 56 to 57, near the end of his third journey. He had never visited Rome, where the church included both Jews and Gentiles, and he wanted their help for a mission to Spain. Because he was about to take a collection to Jerusalem, and because he did not know what awaited him, he set out his gospel carefully.

## How it is arranged
- 1:1 to 17: greeting, and the theme: the gospel is the power of God for salvation, and the righteous will live by faith.
- 1:18 to 3:20: the whole world, Gentile and Jew, is under sin.
- 3:21 to 5:21: God makes people right through faith in Christ, as with Abraham, and Adam and Christ are compared.
- Chapters 6 to 8: new life in the Spirit, freedom from sin and from condemnation.
- Chapters 9 to 11: God's faithfulness to Israel, and the place of Gentiles.
- 12:1 to 15:13: how to live, including love, government and welcoming those who disagree.
- 15:14 to 16:27: travel plans and greetings.

## What to look for
Follow the argument. Chapters 1 to 3 diagnose the problem, 3 to 5 give the remedy, and 6 to 8 describe a life lived in the Spirit. The result is "no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (8:1). Then Paul says that all this, from sin to glory, is mercy, and he breaks into praise at the end of chapter 11. Read the last chapters as the practical fruit of the earlier doctrine.

# 1 Corinthians
@id book-1co
@ref 1CO 1:18-25; 1CO 13:1-8; 1CO 15:1-8

First Corinthians has sixteen chapters and is Paul's answer to a church that had a lot of problems. Corinth was a wealthy, cosmopolitan port, known for its immorality, and the believers there had absorbed many of the habits of the city. The letter is a model of how to deal with them.

Paul planted the church in about AD 50 to 51 on his second journey (Acts 18) and wrote this letter from Ephesus, about AD 53 to 55. He was responding both to a report he had heard and to a list of questions that the church had sent him. He refers to an earlier letter that has not survived (5:9).

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 4: divisions in the church, and the contrast between the wisdom of the world and the cross.
- Chapters 5 and 6: immorality and lawsuits among believers.
- Chapter 7: marriage and singleness.
- Chapters 8 to 10: food offered to idols, and the limits of Christian freedom.
- Chapters 11 to 14: order in worship, the Lord's Supper, spiritual gifts, and the way of love.
- Chapter 15: the resurrection of Christ and of believers.
- Chapter 16: the collection for Jerusalem, and greetings.

## What to look for
Each problem is answered by returning to the gospel. Chapter 13, the famous hymn to love, is placed in the middle of a discussion about gifts, to say that the gifts mean nothing without love. Chapter 15 is the earliest and fullest account of the resurrection, and it makes everything depend on it. The tone moves between sharp rebuke and warm affection.

# 2 Corinthians
@id book-2co
@ref 2CO 4:7-12; 2CO 5:17-21; 2CO 12:7-10
@conf scholars

Second Corinthians has thirteen chapters and is Paul's most personal letter. He opens his heart about his sufferings, his weakness and his anxiety for a church that had turned against him. It shows what the apostle was like when he was not writing theology.

It was written from Macedonia, probably in AD 55 or 56, after a painful visit to Corinth and a severe letter that is now lost. Titus had brought good news of the church's repentance, and Paul writes with relief. Some scholars think that chapters 10 to 13, which change tone, were part of a different letter, but the manuscripts have always carried them together.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 7: Paul's ministry. God's comfort in affliction, the new covenant, treasure in clay jars, and the ministry of reconciliation.
- Chapters 8 and 9: the collection for the poor in Jerusalem, and the principles of generous giving.
- Chapters 10 to 13: Paul defends his apostleship against rivals, whom he calls "super-apostles," and describes his "thorn in my flesh."

## What to look for
The theme is power in weakness. "We have this treasure in jars of clay," Paul says, "to show that this surpassingly great power is from God and not from us." He is told in answer to prayer, "My grace is sufficient for you, for My power is perfected in weakness." Chapter 5 explains the heart of the gospel: God reconciled the world to himself in Christ, and we are given the message of reconciliation.

# Galatians
@id book-gal
@ref GAL 2:15-21; GAL 3:26-29; GAL 5:22-23
@conf scholars

Galatians has six chapters and is a short and passionate letter, written in anger. Paul has heard that some teachers are telling his Gentile converts that they must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to be saved, and he writes at once. It is the charter of Christian freedom, and the clearest statement in the New Testament that people are put right with God by faith and not by works of the law.

The date is debated. If "Galatia" means the southern region where Paul planted churches on his first journey (Acts 13 and 14), the letter could be as early as AD 48, before the council at Jerusalem. If it means the northern region, it would be about AD 53 to 55. Either way it is among the earliest of Paul's letters.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 and 2: Paul defends his gospel and his apostleship, including how he confronted Peter at Antioch.
- Chapters 3 and 4: the argument from Abraham, who was counted righteous by faith, and from the purpose of the law, which was a guardian until Christ came.
- Chapters 5 and 6: freedom in Christ, life by the Spirit, the fruit of the Spirit, and bearing one another's burdens.

## What to look for
Paul is blunt: anyone who preaches another gospel should be cursed. The center is 2:20, "I have been crucified with Christ, and I no longer live, but Christ lives in me." The letter says that in Christ there is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female, and it ends with the fruit of the Spirit, which is the life of freedom.
