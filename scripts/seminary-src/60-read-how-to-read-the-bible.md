# Read it the way it was written
@ref NEH 8:8; ACT 8:30-31; ROM 15:4
@q What is one passage you have read in a way you now suspect the author did not intend?

When Philip met an Ethiopian official reading the prophet Isaiah in his chariot, he asked a simple question: "Do you understand what you are reading?" The man answered, "How can I," he said, "unless someone guides me?" (Acts 8:30-31). It is an honest answer, and it states the reason for a course on interpretation. The Bible is a collection of ancient writings, from other cultures and languages, and reading it well takes more than good intentions.

The basic principle is that we should read each part the way its author meant it to be read. A recipe, a love poem, a legal contract, a newspaper report and a work of science fiction all use words, and all are read in different ways. If someone read a poem as if it were a lab report, or a joke as if it were a law, they would get nonsense. The Bible has many kinds of writing, which scholars call genres, and the first question to ask of any passage is what kind of writing it is.

The Bible's own genres include narrative, law, poetry, wisdom, prophecy, gospel, letters and apocalyptic. Each has its own rules. For example, Proverbs gives general wisdom and not guarantees, the prophets often used vivid, symbolic language, and the Gospels are accounts of Jesus written to persuade as well as to inform. The next lessons take each in turn.

The second principle is to ask what the passage meant to its first hearers before asking what it means to us. When Nehemiah's people read the Law, the Levites "explained" it "so that the people could understand what was being read" (Nehemiah 8:8). That is the work of interpretation. The aim is not to find a hidden meaning, but to hear what is there. Paul says that "everything that was written in the past was written for our instruction" (Romans 15:4), and that is why it is worth the effort.

# Reading the stories
@ref GEN 12:10-20; JDG 19:1-30; 1CO 10:6-11
@conf scholars

About forty percent of the Old Testament is narrative, and some of the most beloved parts of the Bible are stories: Noah, Joseph, David and Goliath, Esther. Stories are a powerful way to teach, but they are easy to misread, and the first rule is that a story is not always an example.

Many stories report what happened, and are not meant as a model for what should happen. The Bible tells of Abraham lying about his wife, Jacob deceiving his father, David taking Bathsheba, and a Levite's terrible story at the end of Judges. The text does not approve of these, and often it makes its judgment clear through the way it tells the story or by what follows. The scholarly terms are descriptive, what the passage tells us occurred, versus prescriptive, what it tells us to do. Gideon laying out a fleece to find out God's will is something he did, and it is not a rule for decision-making.

A better way to read a Bible story is to ask what it tells us about God, and about people. Storytellers in the Bible work with great economy. They give a few details, repeat key words, set scenes against one another, and sometimes let the dialogue speak. A reader should notice what is stressed, where the story turns, and who the characters are.

Every story also sits in a larger one. The story of David and Goliath is part of the account of how God established David's kingship, and that is part of the covenant story that leads to Christ. A single episode may teach a moral lesson, but its main role is in the whole narrative. Paul said that the stories of Israel's failures in the wilderness "happened as examples" and "were written down as warnings for us" (1 Corinthians 10:11). That is how to read a story well: not as a collection of morals, but as part of what God has done.

# Reading the Law
@ref LEV 19:1-18; DEU 22:8; MAT 5:17-20; GAL 3:23-25
@conf debated

For many readers, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy are the hardest parts of the Bible, because they contain hundreds of laws, many of which seem strange or irrelevant. A few principles help.

First, the laws were given to Israel at a particular time, in a covenant between God and a nation. They came after the exodus, as the response of a people who had been rescued, and they formed the constitution of that nation: its worship, its courts, its economy and its family life. They were meant to separate Israel from its neighbors.

Second, the laws include different kinds. Some state basic moral principles, such as the Ten Commandments. Some are case laws, which give examples of how to apply a principle in a specific situation. A famous example is the law that says to put a railing around a flat roof, so that no one falls off and brings guilt on the house (Deuteronomy 22:8). The point is not that all Christians must build a parapet, but that human life is precious and we are responsible for others' safety. Some laws deal with the sacrificial system, the priesthood and clean and unclean things. And some deal with civil matters in ancient Israel.

Third, Christians have to ask how the law relates to Christ. Jesus said he had come "to fulfill" the Law (Matthew 5:17), and Paul said that the law was a guardian "to lead us to Christ" (Galatians 3:24). The church has generally understood that the sacrificial and ceremonial laws were fulfilled in Christ, and no longer need to be kept, and that the moral principles of the law continue. Some traditions, such as the Reformed, divide the law into moral, civil and ceremonial parts. Others are more cautious about that division.

Even where we no longer keep a law, we can learn from it. It shows what God values: justice, mercy, honesty, care for the poor and the stranger, and the separation of what is holy from what is not.

# Reading Hebrew poetry
@ref PSA 1:1-3; PSA 136:1-9; PSA 51:1-2; PRO 10:1
@conf scholars

About a third of the Old Testament is poetry, not just the Psalms but much of Job, Proverbs, the Song of Songs, Lamentations and most of the prophets. Hebrew poetry has its own way of working, and it is quite different from English poetry, since it does not rely on rhyme. Its main device is something called parallelism, the way a line is echoed or answered by a second line.

In 1753 an English bishop, Robert Lowth, described the main types. In synonymous parallelism, the second line says the same thing in other words: "Have mercy on me, O God, according to Your loving devotion; according to Your great compassion, blot out my transgressions" (Psalm 51:1). In antithetic parallelism, the second line contrasts with the first, which is common in Proverbs: "A wise son brings joy to his father, but a foolish son grief to his mother." In synthetic parallelism, the second line builds on the first and carries the thought further.

Reading poetry requires a different approach from reading a legal text. Poets use images and metaphors, and they expect the reader to feel them: God is a rock, a shepherd, a shield, and his people are sheep, wheat, a vine. The Bible's poetry is also very emotional, so the Psalms are full of joy, anger, despair, awe and doubt. Many are laments, and a lament often follows a pattern: a cry to God, a complaint, a request, and a turn to trust.

Nine psalms are acrostics, in which each line starts with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, and this is the form of Psalm 119. The device is lost in English, but it signals completeness, as if to say everything from A to Z is being brought before God.

The right way to read a psalm is slowly, and aloud if possible, paying attention to its images and its movement. The psalms are not so much teaching about prayer as the prayer itself.

# Reading wisdom: Proverbs, Job and Ecclesiastes
@ref PRO 26:4-5; PRO 22:6; JOB 42:7-8; ECC 3:1-8
@conf scholars

The wisdom books ask different questions from the rest of the Bible. They do not tell the story of Israel or record the law. They ask how to live well in God's world, and they handle the question in three different ways.

Proverbs gives short sayings that state general truths. The mistake with a proverb is to read it as a promise. "Train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old he will not depart from it" (Proverbs 22:6) describes how things normally work, and it has been a source of great pain to parents whose children have strayed. A proverb is a rule of thumb, which is true most of the time and usually requires wisdom to apply. This is why Proverbs can say, back to back, "Do not answer a fool according to his folly" and "Answer a fool according to his folly" (Proverbs 26:4-5). Which is right depends on the situation, and knowing which is the skill of wisdom.

Job and Ecclesiastes push against the neat rules. Job asks what happens when a good person suffers, and the book shows that the rule that suffering always follows sin is false. His friends use it, and God says that they were wrong. Ecclesiastes asks what is the use of life if death makes it all vapor, and answers that we should receive life as God's gift, and fear him.

These books are balancing each other. Proverbs says that wise living usually leads to blessing, Job says that sometimes it does not, and Ecclesiastes says that there are things we cannot figure out. The reader needs all three. Wisdom in the Bible starts with the fear of the LORD, and it does not promise to explain everything, but to teach us how to trust him in a world we do not control.

# Reading the prophets
@ref JER 18:7-10; JON 3:4-10; MIC 6:6-8; ISA 7:14
@conf scholars

The prophets were messengers who spoke for God to their own time, and the first rule for reading them is to remember that. The words, "thus says the LORD," were addressed to real people in a particular setting, such as the Assyrian crisis or the Babylonian exile. A reader should always ask first what the message meant to those people.

Predicting the future was only a small part of what they did. Most of their work was forthtelling, calling the people back to the covenant, and denouncing idolatry, injustice and empty worship. When they did speak about the future, it was often a conditional warning. Jonah said, "Forty more days and Nineveh will be overturned!" but Nineveh repented and God "relented from the disaster He had threatened" (Jonah 3:4,10). God explains the principle through Jeremiah: if a nation he has warned turns from evil, he will relent (Jeremiah 18:7-8).

The prophets also used vivid, poetic language: sun and moon darkened, mountains melting, beasts and nations as symbols. Much of this is the language of metaphor, and a literal reading would miss the point. The prophets often saw several layers of fulfillment. Isaiah told King Ahaz in about 735 BC that a young woman would bear a child named Immanuel as a sign, and Matthew sees the final fulfillment in the virgin birth of Jesus (Isaiah 7:14; Matthew 1:22-23).

A useful way to read the prophets is to look for the three steps they follow: the charge, the warning of judgment, and the promise of restoration. They are rarely arranged in order, and an oracle may jump from one to another without notice. And remember that the promises of restoration often reach beyond the immediate situation, to the Messiah and the new creation.

Reading the prophets well means listening for their central cry, which is that God is holy, that his people have turned away, and that he will not let it end there.

# Reading the Gospels
@ref LUK 1:1-4; JHN 20:30-31; MRK 1:1; MAT 28:19-20
@conf scholars

The Gospels are neither modern biographies nor modern histories, and it helps to know what they are. Scholars today generally compare them to ancient biographies, which told the life of a great person in order to praise, to teach and to move readers to follow. They are selective. John says that Jesus did many other things that are not recorded, and that he wrote what he did "so that you may believe" (John 20:30-31).

Several habits help in reading them. Read each Gospel as a whole, and notice what its author wants to say. The details of arrangement are often thematic more than chronological, so when Matthew gathers Jesus' teaching into the Sermon on the Mount, he may be collecting sayings that Jesus said at different times, in the way a teacher gives a summary. Compare the parallel accounts. Matthew, Mark and Luke tell many of the same stories in different words, and the differences are often a clue to each writer's emphasis. They may seem like contradictions, but most can be explained as selection and paraphrase, as in any set of eyewitness accounts.

Read the stories in the context of the first century. The Gospels are full of references to the temple, the Pharisees, the Romans, and the Old Testament, and they only make full sense when these are known. And read them with the end in view. Every Gospel is written looking back from the cross and resurrection, and each of them gives a large part of its space to the last week.

The most important point is that the Gospels are about a person. They are not a rulebook or a collection of inspiring sayings. Jesus himself said to the Scripture experts of his day, "You pore over the Scriptures because you presume that by them you possess eternal life. These are the very words that testify about Me, yet you refuse to come to Me to have life" (John 5:39-40). The aim of reading is to know him.

# Reading the letters
@ref ROM 1:1-7; 1CO 7:1; 2PE 3:15-16; COL 4:16
@conf scholars

Twenty-one books of the New Testament are letters, and reading them well starts with remembering that they were letters. They were written by real people, to specific groups, for particular reasons, and when we read them we are reading other people's mail. Paul knew his congregations, and they knew him. We are listening to one side of a conversation, and sometimes we have to work out what the other side was.

Nearly all the letters were occasional, which means they were prompted by a situation. Paul wrote to Corinth because they were dividing into factions, and because they had sent him a list of questions ("Now for the matters you wrote about," 1 Corinthians 7:1). He wrote to Galatia because teachers were saying that Gentiles had to keep the Law. Knowing the occasion is often the key to the meaning. Some passages that seem baffling today, such as the one about head coverings or baptism for the dead, were evidently clearer to the original readers.

A Greek letter had a standard form: a greeting naming the sender and the recipients, a prayer or thanksgiving, the main body, practical exhortations, and a closing with greetings. Paul adapts it, and in the greeting he regularly states the gospel. Reading the opening lines of a letter often tells what its main themes will be.

Letters are argued, not just stated. Paul often reasons from point to point, and he uses words like "therefore" and "for." The reader should follow the argument, asking what each paragraph is doing, and notice how it moves from doctrine to practice, since he almost always supports commands with theology.

They also have both a first and a larger audience. Paul tells the Colossians to have his letter read in the church at Laodicea, and Peter says that Paul's letters contain some things hard to understand, which the ignorant and unstable twist to their own destruction. They were written to a few, and the church has always held that they speak to everyone.

# Reading apocalyptic: Daniel and Revelation
@ref DAN 7:1-14; REV 1:1-3; REV 1:12-20
@conf debated

Two books of the Bible, Daniel and Revelation, belong to a genre called apocalyptic, from the Greek apokalypsis, "unveiling." It was a type of literature popular in Judaism between about 200 BC and AD 100, written in times of oppression to encourage the faithful. It is full of visions, symbols, numbers and strange beasts, and it can be baffling to a modern reader.

The key to reading it is to recognize that it is written in symbols, and that the symbols are meant to be decoded and not taken literally. When John sees a lamb with seven horns and seven eyes, he does not mean that Jesus looks like a mutant sheep. The picture combines the Lamb who was slain with the fullness of power and sight. Often the book tells us what the symbols mean. "The seven stars are the angels of the seven churches, and the seven lampstands are the seven churches" (Revelation 1:20). Most of the images come from the Old Testament, from Daniel, Ezekiel, Isaiah and Exodus, and one of the best ways to understand Revelation is to learn the Old Testament.

Numbers have a symbolic role. Seven suggests completeness, twelve the people of God, a thousand a vast number. Colors, beasts and cities do too. Babylon in Revelation stands for the world system opposed to God, as Babylon did for the exiles, and it was a code that first-century readers would recognize as Rome.

Apocalyptic is meant to comfort. It tells persecuted people that the powers that seem to be winning are not, and that God is in control and will soon act. It was never intended as a timetable for the end of the world. Christians have read Revelation in four main ways, as describing events of the first century, the whole sweep of church history, the final days, or as timeless symbols of good against evil, and the next sections of this library note this difference. Whichever view one takes, its central message is clear: the Lamb wins.

# Three kinds of context
@ref 2TI 2:15; JHN 7:37-39; PSA 119:105
@q Think of a verse you know well: what comes just before and after it?

If you ask the question "What does this verse mean?" in the wrong way, you can come up with the wrong answer. A better question is, "What did the author mean by saying this, here?" That requires looking at context, and it is helpful to think of three circles of it.

The first circle is the literary context, which is the words around the passage. A verse belongs to a paragraph, the paragraph to a chapter, the chapter to a book. Almost every common mistake in reading the Bible comes from lifting a sentence out of its setting. When we look at the lines before and after it, and at the argument of the whole book, the meaning often becomes clear. The old rule of thumb is, "A text without a context is a pretext."

The second circle is the historical and cultural context: who wrote this, to whom, when, and in what circumstances? What did the images and customs mean at the time? A passage about "the salt of the earth" or "the veil of the temple" or a farmer sowing seed on a path was written for people who knew what those meant. We often need to learn it, from a good study Bible or a commentary.

The third circle is the canonical context, the context of the whole Bible. Scripture is a unity, and it builds from Genesis to Revelation. A passage in Leviticus is read in light of the cross, and a statement in Proverbs in light of the book of Job. The principle is called the analogy of faith, which says that Scripture interprets Scripture, and that no passage should be read in a way that contradicts the clear teaching of the rest.

None of this makes Bible reading harder than it needs to be. A plain reading of most passages is plain enough, and the Spirit helps. The point of paying attention to context is that we want to hear the author, and not our own voice, in the text. The psalmist's prayer is a good one to make before reading: "Open my eyes that I may see wondrous things from Your law" (Psalm 119:18).

# Words and their traps
@ref JHN 21:15-17; MAT 16:18; 1CO 13:1
@conf scholars

It is tempting to dig into the original Hebrew or Greek of a passage and to think that every word has some secret hidden meaning. Knowing the original languages is useful, but a few well-known traps can lead a reader astray. Scholars call them word-study fallacies.

One is the root fallacy, the idea that the meaning of a word is its root. In English, "nice" once meant "foolish," and "butterfly" has nothing to do with butter. Words mean what they are used to mean in a given context. The Greek word for church, ekklesia, does come from a verb meaning "to call out," but in ordinary use it meant an assembly, and it does not mean "the called-out ones" in the way sermons sometimes say.

Another is the habit of reading all possible meanings into a word at once, as if every sense it could have is meant every time. A word has a range of meanings, but in any one sentence the author means one. A third is making a doctrine turn on a distinction that is not there. In John 21, Jesus asks Peter "Do you love me?" using one Greek word for love, agapao, and Peter answers with another, phileo. Many sermons build a lot on this difference, but John uses the two words interchangeably elsewhere, and it is likely that he is just varying his vocabulary.

A fourth is overlooking the ordinary meaning in favor of the unusual one. Most of the time a word means what it normally means.

None of this is a reason to avoid word studies. They are one useful tool, and the course on the words of the Bible in this library looks at some key terms. But a word study is best done with a good dictionary, and with attention to how the word is used by the same author and in the same genre. The rule is to study the word in its sentence, and not the sentence in its word.

# The Old Testament in the New
@ref LUK 24:25-27; LUK 24:44-45; HOS 11:1; MAT 2:15; GAL 4:21-26
@conf scholars

A reader of the New Testament soon notices how often it quotes the Old, and sometimes in ways that raise questions. Matthew says that Joseph took the child Jesus to Egypt to fulfill what the prophet said: "Out of Egypt I called My son" (Matthew 2:15). But in Hosea 11:1 the verse refers to the exodus, and it is in the past tense. How can it be a prophecy of Jesus?

The New Testament writers used several kinds of connection. Sometimes they quote a direct prediction, as when Micah says that the ruler would come from Bethlehem. Sometimes they use typology, the idea that persons, events and institutions in the Old Testament are patterns pointing forward to something greater. Israel, God's son, is called out of Egypt, and Jesus, the true Son, is also called out of Egypt, so Matthew sees a pattern fulfilled. Adam is a type of Christ (Romans 5), the Passover lamb is a type of Christ, and the tabernacle is a copy and shadow of heavenly realities (Hebrews 8:5). Sometimes they draw an analogy or an illustration, as when Paul takes the story of Hagar and Sarah as an allegory about two covenants (Galatians 4:21-26).

The basis is a conviction that Jesus himself gave. On the road to Emmaus, "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, He explained to them what was written in all the Scriptures about Himself" (Luke 24:27), and later he told the disciples that everything written about him in the Law, the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled. The apostles read the Old Testament as a story that finds its climax in Christ.

Modern readers should be cautious about copying every method of the apostles, who were inspired and we are not. But we can follow their central insight, which is that the Old Testament points forward, that it makes full sense only when read in light of Christ, and that the same God is working throughout.

# From then to now: application
@ref JAS 1:22-25; 2TI 3:16-17; 1CO 9:8-10
@conf scholars

After working out what a passage meant to its first readers, the next step is to ask what it means for us. That is called application, and there is a simple way to approach it. First, find the principle behind the text. Second, ask how that principle applies in our setting.

Take the law about putting a railing around a flat roof. The principle behind it is that we must protect the safety of others, and so we apply it today with seat belts, fences around swimming pools and safe workplaces. The law itself is tied to ancient houses, but the principle carries across. Paul uses the same method when he cites the law about not muzzling an ox while it treads grain, and says that it shows that those who preach the gospel should be supported (1 Corinthians 9:8-10).

Some commands are tied to particular cultures. Greeting one another with a holy kiss, or washing one another's feet, show a principle, which is affection and humble service, expressed in a particular form. Other commands are not tied to culture, such as the prohibition of murder, which is rooted in the nature of God and the creation. Telling the difference takes care, and the guide is to look at whether the reason the Bible gives for the command is cultural or universal.

A good question to ask is "What does this passage show me about God, about people, and about how I should respond?" Application is not just about getting a rule for the week. It is also about worship, wonder, repentance and trust. James says that we should not merely listen to the word, deceiving ourselves, but do what it says, and compares those who only hear to someone who looks in a mirror and then forgets what he looks like (James 1:22-24).

The aim of all reading is a changed life. The most thorough knowledge of the Bible is wasted if it is not lived.

# Verses we get wrong
@ref JER 29:4-11; PHP 4:11-13; MAT 18:15-20; 1TI 6:10
@conf scholars

Some of the best-loved verses are often used in ways their authors never meant. A look at a few is a good lesson in why context matters.

Jeremiah 29:11 says, "For I know the plans I have for you," and "plans to prosper you and not to harm you, to give you a future and a hope." It is often given to individuals as a promise of personal success. In context it is part of a letter to the exiles in Babylon, telling them to settle down, build houses and seek the welfare of the city, because the exile would last seventy years. The promise was to the nation, and it came after a long wait. It is still a real encouragement, because it shows God's faithfulness in hard times, but it is not a guarantee of an easy life.

Philippians 4:13 says, "I can do all things through Christ who gives me strength." It is quoted on athletic fields as a promise of victory. In context, Paul has just said that he has learned to be content in plenty and in need. The "all things" is about enduring every circumstance, from abundance to hunger, with Christ's strength.

Matthew 18:20 says, "where two or three gather together in My name, there am I with them." It is often cited for small prayer meetings. The context is church discipline, in which Jesus is describing how a church should deal with a member who sins and refuses to listen. The promise is that Christ stands behind the church's decision. He is also present with those who gather in his name, but this is not the main point there.

Some sayings people think are in the Bible are not. "God helps those who help themselves" is not in Scripture, and it contradicts its teaching about grace. It is an old proverb, popularized by Benjamin Franklin. "Money is the root of all evil" is a misquotation: Paul says that "the love of money is the root of all kinds of evil" (1 Timothy 6:10). And "cleanliness is next to godliness" is not in the Bible either.

The cure is to read the verse in its place, and to ask what it meant.
