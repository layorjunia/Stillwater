# One story in sixty-six books
@ref GEN 1:1; REV 22:1-5; LUK 24:25-27
@q If someone asked you what the whole Bible is about, how would you answer in two sentences?

The Bible is a library, not a single book. It has sixty-six books written over roughly 1,500 years by many different authors: shepherds, kings, fishermen, a tax collector, a physician, a tentmaker. They wrote in Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek, in laws and poems, in letters and battle reports and visions. Yet Christians have always read it as one continuous story with a single author behind the human ones.

The story moves in four great movements. It begins with creation, a good world made by a good God, with people made to know him and to look after what he made. Then comes the fall, when the first humans turn away from God and everything is damaged: relationships, work, bodies, the ground itself. The longest part of the Bible is redemption, the account of how God sets out to rescue what was lost, first through a family, then a nation, then a single person, Jesus. The story ends with new creation, where the damage is undone and God lives with his people again.

Jesus himself taught that the Old Testament was pointing to him. On the road to Emmaus, after his resurrection, he took two confused disciples through "Moses and all the Prophets" and explained what the Scriptures said about himself (Luke 24:27). That is the key for reading everything else. A single psalm or a single battle may look like an isolated scene, but each one sits somewhere on this map, and learning to find the place on the map is most of what a seminary course in the Bible teaches.

The lessons in this course walk the story in order, from the first page to the last, and then pull out the threads that run through the whole thing.

# In the beginning: a world made on purpose
@ref GEN 1:1; GEN 1:26-28; GEN 1:31; PSA 19:1
@q What difference does it make to say the world was made on purpose and called good?

The Bible opens with a claim that was bold in its own day: one God made everything, and he did it by speaking. Genesis 1:1 gives the heading, "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth." The rest of the chapter unpacks it, day by day, in a careful and almost liturgical pattern. God separates light from darkness, sea from sky, sea from dry land, then fills each realm: lights in the sky, fish and birds, land animals, and finally human beings.

Other ancient peoples told stories of creation too, and often the world came out of a battle between gods, or from the body of a slain deity. Genesis tells it differently. There is no rival. The sun and moon, which many nations worshiped, are simply lamps that God hangs in the sky. Nothing in the created order is divine, and nothing is an accident.

At the end of each stage God calls what he has made good, and after the people appear the text says, "indeed, it was very good" (Genesis 1:31). Matter is not evil. Bodies, food, work, marriage, rest and beauty are all part of the original design. Human beings are made in the image of God and given a task: to rule over the creation, which in the Bible means to look after it as a king looks after a land he loves (Genesis 1:26-28).

Christians have long argued about how to read the days of Genesis 1: as six ordinary days, as long ages, as a literary frame, or in other ways. Those debates are real, but they should not hide what every reading holds in common. The world has a Maker, the Maker is good, and people have a purpose in it.

# The garden and the two trees
@ref GEN 2:7-9; GEN 2:15-17; GEN 2:18-25; REV 22:2
@conf debated

Genesis 2 retells the sixth day from close up. God shapes a man out of the dust of the ground and breathes life into him. The Hebrew sounds almost like a pun: the man is adam, and the ground is adamah. He is earth and he is breath, both at once. God plants a garden in Eden and puts the man there "to cultivate and keep it" (Genesis 2:15). Work is therefore not a punishment. It is part of paradise.

In the middle of the garden stand two trees: the tree of life, and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. God gives one command with one warning. The man may eat from every tree except the second, "for in the day that you eat of it, you will surely die" (Genesis 2:17). The command is simple, and that is the point. It gives the man something to trust God about, a way to say yes to being a creature who depends on his Maker.

Then God makes an observation that surprises the reader: "It is not good for the man to be alone" (Genesis 2:18). Everything so far has been good, and now something is lacking. God brings the woman as a partner, and the man answers with the first poem in the Bible. The chapter closes with a picture of a man and his wife who are naked and feel no shame, at ease with each other and with God.

Readers differ on how to take the details of Genesis 2 and 3. Some read the garden, the trees and the serpent as plain history, some as rich symbols of real events, and some as a story that teaches truth in a literary form. What the whole church has held in common is that this is the starting point for everything that follows: the first people were made good, placed in a home with God, and given a choice.

# The fall: what went wrong in Eden
@ref GEN 3:1-7; GEN 3:8-13; ROM 5:12; ROM 8:20-21
@conf debated

Genesis 3 is the shortest and most consequential chapter in the Bible. A serpent asks the woman a question designed to make God look stingy: "Did God really say, 'You must not eat from any tree in the garden?'" (Genesis 3:1). It twists what God said, then contradicts it outright, then offers the thing that has tempted every human since: you can be like God. The woman looks at the fruit, sees that it is good for food and pleasing to the eye, takes it, and gives some to her husband, who is with her.

Notice what happens at once. They discover they are naked and hide. For the first time they feel shame. When God comes walking in the garden, they hide from him, and when he asks what happened, the man blames the woman, and the woman blames the serpent. In a few verses the first marriage and the first friendship with God have both cracked.

The rest of the Bible reads this as the original wound. Paul writes that "sin entered the world through one man, and death through sin" (Romans 5:12). Creation itself "was subjected to futility" and waits to be set free (Romans 8:20-21). Christians call this the fall, and the doctrine of sin grows out of it: we are born into a world and a nature already bent away from God.

Traditions differ on exactly how the effects of that first sin pass down to everyone. Some speak of inherited guilt, some of an inherited corruption and a deadly inheritance, and the Eastern churches stress mortality more than guilt. All of them agree on the main fact. Something went badly wrong at the start, and people cannot repair it by themselves. That is why the rest of the story is a rescue.

# The first promise
@ref GEN 3:14-15; GEN 3:21; GAL 4:4; 1JN 3:8
@conf debated

When God finds the man and the woman hiding, he does not simply condemn them. He first judges the serpent, and in doing so he makes a promise: "I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your seed and her seed. He will crush your head, and you will strike his heel" (Genesis 3:15). The woman's descendants will fight the serpent's, and one of them will strike a fatal blow at the head while suffering a wound at the heel.

Christians have long read this verse as the first announcement of the gospel, and the Latin name protoevangelium means exactly that, the first gospel. Irenaeus, a bishop in Gaul writing about AD 180, read the promised offspring as Christ, and many later writers followed him. Others hold that the verse speaks first of the ongoing conflict between humanity and evil, and that the New Testament shows that conflict reaching its climax in Jesus. Either way, the shape fits. At the cross the serpent's seed strikes the heel of the woman's offspring, and the resurrection crushes the head of death. As John puts it, "This is why the Son of God was revealed, to destroy the works of the devil" (1 John 3:8).

A smaller sign comes a few verses later. God makes garments of skin for the man and the woman and clothes them himself (Genesis 3:21). Something had to die to cover their shame. The tone is gentle, but the pattern is already there and will grow: sin is serious, life is costly, and God himself provides the covering.

The point to carry forward is that the Bible's story of rescue starts in the same chapter as the story of the fall. God did not wait for people to ask for help. He spoke the promise first.

# Cain and Abel, and a world gone wrong
@ref GEN 4:1-16; GEN 4:23-24; GEN 6:5-8; 1JN 3:12
@conf scholars

Within a single generation, the fall bears its first terrible fruit. Cain and Abel bring offerings to God, and God accepts Abel's but not Cain's. The text does not say precisely why, though the New Testament says Abel offered "by faith" (Hebrews 11:4), and John says Cain's deeds were evil (1 John 3:12). Cain is furious, and God speaks to him with unusual directness: "sin is crouching at your door; it desires you, but you must master it" (Genesis 4:7). Cain does not listen. He lures his brother into a field and kills him.

When God asks where Abel is, Cain answers with one of the most famous lines in Scripture: "Am I my brother’s keeper?" (Genesis 4:9). God answers that Abel's blood is crying out from the ground. Cain is driven away, yet God puts a mark on him so that no one will kill him. Judgment and mercy again arrive together.

The chapter then follows Cain's line. They build a city, develop cattle farming, music and metalwork, so there is real human achievement here. But Lamech, a descendant, boasts to his wives that he has killed a man for wounding him, and that if Cain is avenged sevenfold, then Lamech will be avenged seventy-sevenfold (Genesis 4:23-24). Civilization advances and so does violence.

By Genesis 6 the Lord sees that "the wickedness of man was great upon the earth" (Genesis 6:5). But even there the text adds a short sentence that points forward: Noah "found favor in the eyes of the LORD" (Genesis 6:8). The first eleven chapters of Genesis keep repeating this pattern. Sin spreads, God judges, God shows grace to someone, and the story continues.

# Noah, the flood, and the first covenant
@ref GEN 6:13-22; GEN 8:20-22; GEN 9:8-17; 1PE 3:20-21
@conf scholars

God sees that the earth is filled with violence, and he tells Noah that he will end it. At the same time he tells him how to be saved: build a large vessel, an ark, and bring in his family and pairs of the animals. Noah does exactly as he is told, which the author notes more than once. When the flood has come and gone, and the ark has settled, Noah steps out onto dry ground and builds an altar. God smells the pleasing aroma and says in his heart, "Never again will I curse the ground because of man, even though every inclination of his heart is evil from his youth" (Genesis 8:21).

That is a remarkable verse. The reason God gives for sparing the world is the same reason he gave for destroying it. The flood has not fixed human hearts, and God knows that. So he makes a promise instead: he establishes a covenant with Noah, with his descendants and with every living creature, that never again will a flood destroy the earth. The sign is the rainbow, set in the clouds. The Hebrew word for the rainbow is the ordinary word for a warrior's bow, as if God had hung up his weapon in the clouds.

Readers disagree about the extent of the flood. Some hold it was global, some regional to the area known to the early writers, and the scholarly literature on ancient flood stories (such as the Mesopotamian Epic of Gilgamesh and the Atrahasis story) shows both parallels and sharp differences. What stands out in Genesis is how the story is told: a moral holiness at the center, a God who judges and saves, and the first formal covenant of the Bible. Peter later sees the water as a picture of baptism, through which believers are saved (1 Peter 3:20-21).

# Babel and the scattering of the nations
@ref GEN 11:1-9; GEN 10:32; ACT 2:5-11; REV 7:9
@conf scholars

Genesis 10 lists the descendants of Noah's sons as the family of nations, in a table of seventy names. Genesis 11 then steps back to tell how they came to be spread out and speaking different languages. The people settle on a plain in Shinar, which is the region of Babylonia, and decide to build a city and a tower with its top in the heavens, "that we may make a name for ourselves and not be scattered over the face of all the earth" (Genesis 11:4).

The two motives are the whole story. They want a name for themselves, which is pride, and they want to avoid being scattered, which is a refusal of God's command to fill the earth. The tower is probably a ziggurat, the stepped temple-tower common in Mesopotamia. Archaeologists have found the remains of many of them. The narrator has some fun with the scene: the people are building a tower to reach the heavens, and God has to come down to see it (Genesis 11:5).

God confuses their speech, and they stop building. The city is called Babel, which sounds like the Hebrew word for confused, though in the Akkadian language of Babylon the same word, Babilu, meant "gate of God." The great monument to human greatness becomes a symbol of human failure.

The story also sets up everything that follows. The nations are now divided and drifting, and the next chapter introduces a man whom God will call out of that world to bless "all the families of the earth." The reversal comes at Pentecost, when people from every nation hear the apostles' message in their own languages (Acts 2:5-11), and it is completed in the vision of Revelation 7:9, where a crowd from every nation, tribe, people and language stands before the throne.

# Abraham: the call and the promise
@ref GEN 12:1-3; GEN 15:1-6; GEN 17:1-8; ROM 4:1-5; GAL 3:8
@conf scholars

After eleven chapters about the whole world, the Bible narrows to one man. Abram is living in Haran, having come from Ur with his father Terah, when the LORD says to him: "Leave your country, your kindred, and your father’s household, and go to the land I will show you" (Genesis 12:1). With the command come three promises: a great nation, a land, and a blessing that will reach "all the families of the earth" (Genesis 12:3). He is seventy-five, his wife Sarai is barren, and he is told to leave everything behind. Abram goes.

The promise is repeated and deepened. In Genesis 15 God takes him outside, tells him to count the stars, and says his descendants will be as many. Abram "believed the LORD, and it was credited to him as righteousness" (Genesis 15:6). That one verse becomes the center of Paul's argument in Romans 4 and Galatians 3: Abraham was put right with God by trusting, long before the law of Moses, and so all who trust God as he did are his children.

In Genesis 17 God makes the covenant formal. He changes the names to Abraham ("father of many") and Sarah, promises to be God to him and to his offspring, and gives circumcision as the sign. A covenant in the ancient world was a binding relationship with promises and obligations. This one is mostly promise: God does the main work.

The date of Abraham is debated, but most place him early in the second millennium BC, perhaps between 2000 and 1800 BC. What matters for the story is the shape. After Babel, God does not give up on the nations. He chooses one family so that through them the blessing may reach all the others.

# The test on the mountain
@ref GEN 22:1-14; HEB 11:17-19; JHN 3:16; ROM 8:32
@conf scholars

Genesis 22 is one of the most difficult chapters in the Old Testament, and it opens with a sentence that tells the reader what the characters do not know: "God tested Abraham." He tells him to take Isaac, "your only son Isaac, whom you love," to the land of Moriah and offer him there as a burnt offering. Abraham gets up early, saddles the donkey, and goes. Isaac carries the wood. He asks where the lamb is, and Abraham answers, "God Himself will provide the lamb for the burnt offering, my son" (Genesis 22:8).

Just as Abraham raises the knife, the angel of the Lord calls out, and Abraham sees a ram caught in a thicket. He offers the ram in place of his son and names the place "The LORD will provide." The angel then repeats the covenant promise with an oath.

The New Testament helps with what this means. The letter to the Hebrews says Abraham reasoned that God could raise the dead, and in a sense he received Isaac back "from the dead" (Hebrews 11:19). The story also tells the reader that God does not desire child sacrifice, which Canaan's neighbors practiced. He stops the knife and provides a substitute.

Christians have also seen a picture here that points forward. A father gives up his beloved son; the son carries the wood; a substitute dies in his place; and the place is traditionally associated with the hill where Jerusalem later stood. Paul writes that God "did not spare His own Son but gave Him up for us all" (Romans 8:32), and the contrast is striking. Abraham was spared from giving up his son. God did not spare his own.

# Jacob and the twelve tribes
@ref GEN 25:23; GEN 28:10-17; GEN 32:22-32; GEN 35:9-12
@conf scholars

Isaac and Rebekah have twin sons, Esau and Jacob, and before they are born God tells Rebekah that the older will serve the younger. Jacob's name means "heel-grabber" or "deceiver," and he lives up to it. He buys Esau's birthright for a bowl of stew and with his mother's help tricks his father into giving him the blessing. He flees from his furious brother, and the first night alone he dreams of a ladder reaching to heaven, with angels going up and down and the LORD standing at the top, repeating to him the promise made to Abraham (Genesis 28:10-17).

Jacob then spends twenty years in Haran, where his uncle Laban deceives him in turn. He marries Leah and Rachel, and the two women and their maids bear him twelve sons and a daughter. Returning home, he is afraid to meet Esau, and the night before the meeting he wrestles with a man until daybreak. He refuses to let go until the man blesses him, and he leaves with a limp and a new name: Israel, which the text explains this way: "you have struggled with God and with men, and you have prevailed" (Genesis 32:28). He had spent his whole life grasping, and now God would give him what he could not grasp.

The twelve sons are Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, Dan, Naphtali, Gad, Asher, Issachar, Zebulun, Joseph and Benjamin, and their families become the tribes of Israel. Joseph's share is later divided between his two sons, Ephraim and Manasseh. The whole people take their name from Jacob, so Israel is the family of a man who struggled with God.

The patriarchs are no saints, and the Bible does not clean them up. Abraham lies, Isaac favors one son, Jacob schemes. The promise is carried by a God who keeps his word, not by the goodness of the people who receive it.

# Joseph, and why Israel ended up in Egypt
@ref GEN 37:3-28; GEN 41:37-45; GEN 45:4-8; GEN 50:19-20; ROM 8:28
@conf scholars

The last quarter of Genesis follows Jacob's favorite son. Joseph is loved by his father, given a richly ornamented robe, and has dreams that his family will bow down to him. His brothers, burning with jealousy, sell him to traders heading for Egypt and tell their father he has been killed. Joseph becomes a slave in the house of Potiphar, is falsely accused by Potiphar's wife, and ends up in prison.

In prison he correctly interprets the dreams of two of Pharaoh's officials, and two years later, when Pharaoh himself has a troubling dream, he is called out. Joseph explains that seven good years will be followed by seven years of famine and recommends storing grain. Pharaoh makes him second in command of Egypt. When the famine comes, it reaches Canaan, and Joseph's brothers arrive to buy grain. They do not recognize him. After testing them, Joseph weeps and tells them who he is.

The center of the story is his statement to them after their father dies and they fear revenge: "what you intended against me for evil, God intended for good, in order to accomplish a day like this," which was "to preserve the lives of many people" (Genesis 50:20). It does not say that the evil was good. It says that God was working through it for a larger purpose, and that is the idea behind Paul's later words in Romans 8:28.

The story also answers a plain question: how did the family of Abraham, living in Canaan, end up in Egypt? Famine drove them there, and Joseph's position made it possible. Jacob's family settles in the region of Goshen and grows into a nation. The book ends with Joseph's coffin in Egypt and a promise that God will bring them back to the land.

# Moses and the exodus
@ref EXO 1:8-14; EXO 3:1-14; EXO 12:1-13; EXO 14:21-31; 1CO 5:7
@conf scholars

Exodus begins several centuries after Genesis ends. The family of Jacob has grown into a large people, and a new king "who did not know Joseph" fears them and turns them into slaves, working on brick and mortar. When that does not stop their growth, he orders every Hebrew baby boy thrown into the Nile. One mother hides her son in a basket among the reeds, and Pharaoh's own daughter finds and raises him. He is named Moses.

At eighty Moses is a shepherd in the wilderness of Midian when he sees a bush that burns without burning up. God speaks from it, tells him he has seen the misery of his people, and sends him to Pharaoh. Moses objects, and asks God's name. The answer is "I AM WHO I AM" (Exodus 3:14), which is tied to the Hebrew four-letter name YHWH, usually written "the LORD" in English Bibles.

Ten plagues fall on Egypt, and the last, the death of the firstborn, is the one that breaks Pharaoh. Before it, God tells each Hebrew household to kill a lamb, put its blood on the doorposts, and eat a meal in haste. When the destroyer passes through, he will pass over every house with the blood. That night is the first Passover. Israel leaves, Pharaoh chases them, and at the sea the water divides so that they cross on dry ground and the pursuing army is drowned.

When the date is considered, scholars divide between an early exodus around 1446 BC, based on 1 Kings 6:1, and a later one around 1260 BC, based on archaeological evidence from the Egyptian capital Pi-Ramesses. The Bible's own emphasis is not on the date. It is on the fact that God hears, comes down, and saves. Paul says that "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Corinthians 5:7), which is why Christians keep reading the exodus as the pattern of their own deliverance.

# Sinai: the covenant and the law
@ref EXO 19:3-6; EXO 20:1-17; EXO 24:3-8; GAL 3:19-25; MAT 5:17
@conf debated

Three months after leaving Egypt, the Israelites camp at the foot of Mount Sinai. God tells Moses what to say. He reminds them what he did to Egypt, and then adds: "if you will indeed obey My voice and keep My covenant, you will be My treasured possession out of all the nations." They are to be "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exodus 19:5-6). Notice the order. God saves first. The law comes after the rescue, not before it.

Then, amid thunder, fire and a trumpet blast, God speaks the Ten Commandments to the whole assembly. They start with the relationship (no other gods, no images, no misuse of the name, the Sabbath) and move to the community (honor parents, no murder, adultery, theft, false witness, coveting). Moses then gives them a longer set of laws, and the people reply, "All that the LORD has spoken we will do, and we will be obedient." Moses seals the covenant by sprinkling blood on the altar and on the people (Exodus 24:3-8).

The Mosaic covenant gave Israel a constitution, a worship, and a way of life that set them apart from their neighbors. Christians differ on how the law applies today. A common view in the Reformed tradition divides it into moral, civil and ceremonial parts and holds that only the moral law still binds. Others stress that the whole law is fulfilled in Christ and that believers are under the law of Christ. Jesus said, "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them" (Matthew 5:17).

Paul adds that the law acted as a guardian until Christ came (Galatians 3:24). It showed what holiness looked like, exposed sin, and pointed to a need that only a savior could meet.

# The tabernacle: God moves in
@ref EXO 25:8-9; EXO 40:34-38; JHN 1:14; HEB 9:1-14
@conf scholars

Thirteen of the last sixteen chapters of Exodus are about a tent. At Sinai God gives Moses detailed plans for a portable sanctuary: a courtyard with an altar for sacrifice, a bronze basin for washing, and a tent divided into two rooms. The first room, the Holy Place, has a lampstand, a table with bread and an altar for incense. The inner room, the Most Holy Place, holds the ark of the covenant, a gold-covered chest containing the stone tablets with the covering called the mercy seat.

The reason is given in a single sentence: "And they are to make a sanctuary for Me, so that I may dwell among them" (Exodus 25:8). The tabernacle is the answer to Eden. God had walked in the garden with the first couple, and since they were driven out there has been a barrier. Now he sets up a tent in the middle of the camp. Every detail of its materials, its curtains and its layout is designed to say that God can live among people, but that he is holy and must be approached the right way.

The book ends with one of its most moving scenes. Moses finishes the work, and "the cloud covered the tent of meeting, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle" (Exodus 40:34). So great is the glory that Moses cannot enter. The cloud then guides Israel on its travels, day and night.

The New Testament picks up the picture. John says that the Word "became flesh and made His dwelling among us" (John 1:14). The Greek verb for made his dwelling is related to the word for tent, and can be rendered "pitched his tent" or "tabernacled." Hebrews adds that Christ is the high priest of a greater and more perfect tent, and that his own blood opens the way into God's presence (Hebrews 9:11-12).
