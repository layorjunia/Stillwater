# Grace
@ref EPH 2:4-9; TIT 2:11-14; ROM 3:24; ROM 11:6
@q Where in your own life are you still trying to earn what God has said he gives freely?

If one word sums up the Christian message, it is grace. In the New Testament the Greek word is charis, a word that in ordinary use meant a favor, a gift or a kindness. Paul bends it to a special use: the free, undeserved kindness of God toward people who deserve the opposite.

The classic statement is in Ephesians 2. After describing human beings as dead in sin, Paul says, "But because of His great love for us, God, who is rich in mercy, made us alive with Christ even when we were dead in our trespasses. It is by grace you have been saved!" (Ephesians 2:4-5). A few verses later: "For it is by grace you have been saved through faith, and this not from yourselves; it is the gift of God, not by works, so that no one can boast" (Ephesians 2:8-9).

Three things stand out. First, grace is undeserved. It is not given because of any merit, and it cannot be earned. If it could, Paul says, "grace would no longer be grace" (Romans 11:6). Second, grace is free and it is costly. It costs the receiver nothing, but it cost the giver everything, since forgiveness was paid for on the cross. Third, grace is not only about forgiveness. It is also the power that changes people. Titus says that "the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation to everyone. It instructs us to renounce ungodliness and worldly passions" (Titus 2:11-12).

Christian traditions have different ways of describing how grace works. Some distinguish prevenient grace, which comes before we respond, saving grace, and sustaining grace. Catholic theology speaks of grace as a gift that can be channeled through the sacraments. Reformed theology stresses that grace is sovereign. But all insist that it is the foundation of everything, and that the proper response is gratitude.

# Chosen before the world began
@ref EPH 1:3-6; ROM 8:29-30; ROM 9:14-18; JHN 6:37
@conf debated

Few teachings have caused more argument among Christians than election and predestination, though the words are the Bible's own. Paul writes that God "chose us in Him before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless in His presence. In love He predestined us for adoption as His sons through Jesus Christ" (Ephesians 1:4-5). Romans 8 lays out a chain: those God foreknew, he predestined, called, justified and glorified.

The disagreement is about the basis of God's choice. One view, held by Augustine, Luther, Calvin and the Reformed tradition, says God's election is unconditional. He chose particular people for salvation purely out of his own mercy, not because of anything he foresaw in them, and his grace to them is effective. In the early seventeenth century a Dutch pastor named Jacobus Arminius questioned this, and after his death his followers presented their view, which was condemned at the Synod of Dort in 1618 and 1619. The Synod's answer, in five points, is where the five points of Calvinism come from, which later generations remembered by the acronym TULIP.

The other main view, held by Arminians and Wesleyans, says that God elects on the basis of foreknowledge of who will believe, or that election is corporate, belonging to the people of God in Christ, and that individuals enter by faith. They point to the verse saying that God our Savior "wants everyone to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth" (1 Timothy 2:3-4) and to the statement that God is "not wanting anyone to perish but everyone to come to repentance" (2 Peter 3:9).

Catholic and Orthodox traditions, while differing from each other, generally hold that God's grace is first and necessary but that people cooperate with it. Reformed readers answer that a human cooperation that decides the matter would make salvation depend on us after all.

Whatever one decides, the text's own aim is not to alarm but to comfort and to produce praise. Paul begins with a blessing: "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ." Jesus offers a promise that every believer can rest in: "the one who comes to Me I will never drive away" (John 6:37).

# The call and the new birth
@ref JHN 3:3-8; EZK 36:26-27; 1PE 1:3; TIT 3:4-7
@conf debated

When a person comes to faith, something happens that no one can bring about by willpower. Jesus tells Nicodemus: "Truly, truly, I tell you, no one can see the kingdom of God unless he is born again" (John 3:3). Nicodemus is puzzled, and Jesus explains that it is a birth "of water and the Spirit," and compares the Spirit to the wind: you hear it and see what it does, but you cannot control it.

Theologians call this regeneration. It is God's act of giving new spiritual life. Ezekiel had promised it centuries earlier: "I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit within you; I will remove your heart of stone and give you a heart of flesh" (Ezekiel 36:26). Peter says that God "has given us new birth into a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead" (1 Peter 1:3). Titus describes "the washing of new birth and renewal by the Holy Spirit."

Before regeneration comes the call. The gospel is preached, and the Spirit works through it to draw people to Christ. Reformed theology distinguishes an outward call, which goes to everyone who hears, from an inward or effectual call, in which the Spirit makes the message take root. Arminian theology holds that the Spirit's grace goes to all and enables a response. Christians differ on the order, whether new birth comes before faith or after it, but they agree that both are the work of God.

The Bible also uses the image of rebirth in connection with baptism. Churches that practice infant baptism, such as the Catholic, Orthodox and Lutheran, often link the two closely. Others hold that baptism is a sign of a new birth that has already happened. Whatever the view of the sign, the reality is that those who belong to Christ have been given a new life, and they are able to love what they once could not.

# Faith and repentance
@ref MRK 1:15; ACT 20:21; HEB 11:1-6; 2CO 7:10
@q What does repentance look like in your life this week: a turn, a confession, or a decision to do something differently?

When Jesus began to preach, he said two things: "Repent and believe in the gospel!" (Mark 1:15). Paul later summed up his own message as "repentance to God and faith in our Lord Jesus Christ" (Acts 20:21). They are two sides of one turning.

Repentance means a change of mind that leads to a change of direction. The Greek word, metanoia, means to think differently afterward. It includes knowing that one has sinned, feeling sorrow over it, and turning away from it toward God. Paul distinguishes two kinds of sorrow: "Godly sorrow brings repentance that leads to salvation without regret, but worldly sorrow brings death" (2 Corinthians 7:10). The difference is that godly sorrow turns toward God, while worldly sorrow only turns inward in regret or fear of punishment.

Faith is trust. The Bible does not mean by it a vague optimism or a belief in something with no evidence. Hebrews says, "Now faith is the assurance of what we hope for and the certainty of what we do not see" (Hebrews 11:1). Theologians divide faith into three parts. It involves knowledge, because we must know the message. It involves assent, agreement that it is true. And it involves trust, resting on Christ personally to save us. The last is the heart of it. A person can believe that a bridge is sound and still not walk across it.

Faith is also not a work that earns salvation. It is the empty hand that receives the gift. Paul says that salvation is by grace "through faith," and "this not from yourselves; it is the gift of God." Even the faith is a gift. Christians differ over how far it is a gift given directly by God to the chosen and how far it is given to all who hear, but in both views the praise goes to God.

Repentance and faith are not one-time events only. They mark the whole Christian life, since a believer goes on turning from sin and trusting Christ daily.

# Justification: declared right with God
@ref ROM 3:21-28; ROM 5:1; GAL 2:15-16; JAS 2:21-24
@conf debated

Justification is the word the New Testament uses for the verdict of God on the believer. It is a courtroom word. To justify is to declare someone righteous, to acquit. Paul builds the main argument of Romans on it: "we maintain that a man is justified by faith apart from works of the law" (Romans 3:28), and "since we have been justified through faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 5:1).

The reason it needs saying is that every person stands guilty before God. No one can be declared righteous by keeping the law, since no one keeps it. Paul says in Galatians that "a man is not justified by works of the law, but by faith in Jesus Christ." The ground of the verdict is not our own goodness but Christ's. In Romans 3 he says that people are "justified freely by His grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus." Christ's righteousness is credited to those who trust him, as Abraham's faith "was credited to him as righteousness."

This was the center of the Reformation. Martin Luther said that this teaching is the article on which the church stands or falls. The Reformers said that justification is by faith alone, that it is a legal declaration, and that the righteousness is Christ's, given from outside us. The Catholic Church at the Council of Trent in 1547 replied that justification is not only a declaration but also an inner renewal, in which grace makes a person actually righteous, with faith working through love. For centuries this was a dividing line. In 1999 the Lutheran World Federation and the Catholic Church signed a Joint Declaration saying that the two sides now share a common understanding of the basics, while some differences of emphasis remain.

James says, "a man is justified by his deeds and not by faith alone" (James 2:24). Most Christians reconcile this with Paul by noting that James is talking about the kind of faith that is real, and that it shows itself in works. Paul and James both agree that the fruit of true faith is a changed life.

# Adopted into the family
@ref ROM 8:14-17; GAL 4:4-7; 1JN 3:1-2; EPH 1:5
@q What changes if you believe that God relates to you as a child and not as a servant or a stranger?

Justification is a courtroom word. Adoption is a family word. It says that the person who was a rebel and a stranger is now welcomed as a child into the house. The Bible uses both pictures, and the two together are a fuller account of the gospel than either alone.

In the Roman world an adopted son received the name, the family and the inheritance of his new father, with full legal standing, and often chosen over natural born sons. Paul draws on that. He says that God sent his Son "to redeem those under the law, that we might receive our adoption as sons" (Galatians 4:5). Those who believe have "received the Spirit of adoption to sonship, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!'" (Romans 8:15).

The effects are immediate. A child has a new relationship, a new name and a new access. We may speak to God as Father, as Jesus taught. A child also has a new security: "The Spirit Himself testifies with our spirit that we are God's children. And if we are children, then we are heirs: heirs of God and co-heirs with Christ" (Romans 8:16-17). Everything that belongs to Christ is shared with those who are in him. And a child has a family. Adopted people are brothers and sisters, and the church is the household of God.

John's wonder at this is plain. "Behold what manner of love the Father has given to us, that we should be called children of God. And that is what we are!" (1 John 3:1). He adds that we do not yet know what we will be, but we know that when Christ appears, we will be like him.

The adoption of believers is not a legal fiction. It is a real change of status and relationship, accomplished by God at great cost, and its goal is that those who were far off may be at home with him.

# United with Christ
@ref JHN 15:4-5; ROM 6:3-5; GAL 2:20; EPH 1:3-4
@conf scholars

If you read the letters of Paul looking for a phrase that sums up his view of the Christian life, you might choose "in Christ." It appears in some form well over a hundred times. Many theologians, including Calvin, have argued that union with Christ is the master idea behind salvation: not a separate step along with justification and sanctification, but the reality that holds them all together.

The Bible speaks of this union in many pictures. Jesus says, "I am the vine and you are the branches. The one who remains in Me, and I in him, will bear much fruit" (John 15:5). Paul speaks of the church as the body of Christ, with Christ as the head. He speaks of a marriage between Christ and the church. He says believers are in Christ and Christ is in them, and that the Holy Spirit is the bond.

Union means that what happened to Christ is counted as having happened to us. Paul says, "all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into His death. We were therefore buried with Him through baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, we too may walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:3-4). To be united with Christ is to die with him to sin and rise with him to a new life. In Galatians Paul says, "I have been crucified with Christ, and I no longer live, but Christ lives in me."

The doctrine also explains why believers receive every benefit of salvation together. Forgiveness, adoption, the Spirit, the hope of resurrection and the promise of glory all come with Christ, since he cannot be divided. The Orthodox tradition speaks of theosis, becoming partakers of the divine nature (2 Peter 1:4), and means something similar. Whatever the vocabulary, the heart of the Christian life is not a set of rules, but a person, and a relationship with him.

# Becoming holy
@ref 1TH 4:3-7; PHP 2:12-13; ROM 6:11-14; ROM 8:13
@conf debated

Justification declares a person right with God once for all. Sanctification is the lifelong process in which that person is made holy. The word means to be set apart, and it covers both a position and a growth. In one sense, believers are already sanctified, set apart for God by Christ. In another sense, they are being sanctified, becoming in practice what they already are in standing.

Paul gives the aim bluntly: "It is God's will that you should be holy" (1 Thessalonians 4:3). He also describes how it works, with both halves of the truth in one sentence. "continue to work out your salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God who works in you to will and to act on behalf of His good purpose" (Philippians 2:12-13). Believers must make an effort, and the effort is empowered by God. In another passage he says that "if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live" (Romans 8:13).

The means are ordinary. They include prayer, the reading and hearing of Scripture, worship, the Lord's Supper, fellowship with other believers, service, and the discipline of obedience. They are called means of grace, because God uses them to give what he promises. Suffering is another, since Peter says that trials refine faith as fire refines gold.

Traditions differ on how far sanctification goes in this life. The Reformed tradition teaches progressive sanctification: a real growth that is never completed before death, and in which the struggle with sin continues. John Wesley taught that Christians could aim at "entire sanctification" or "Christian perfection," which he defined as a heart filled with love for God and neighbor, not a state free from mistakes. Others, in the Keswick tradition, taught a decisive "higher life" of surrender. The Orthodox Church speaks of a journey of theosis. John writes plainly that if we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves (1 John 1:8), and that everyone with the hope of seeing Christ purifies himself.

# Can you lose it? Perseverance and assurance
@ref JHN 10:27-29; ROM 8:35-39; HEB 6:4-6; 1JN 5:13
@conf debated
@q What gives you assurance that you belong to Christ, and what threatens it?

Does a Christian ever fall away? The question has a pastoral side: how can someone be sure of salvation? And a doctrinal side: what is the relationship between God's grace and human faithfulness? The Bible has comforting promises and also solemn warnings, and Christians have held different views of how they fit together.

The promises are strong. Jesus says of his sheep, "I give them eternal life, and they will never perish. No one can snatch them out of My hand" (John 10:28). Paul asks who can separate us from the love of Christ and concludes that "neither death nor life, neither angels nor principalities, neither the present nor the future, nor any powers, neither height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God that is in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:38-39). John says that he writes "so that you may know that you have eternal life" (1 John 5:13).

The warnings are strong as well. Hebrews speaks of those who have been enlightened and tasted the heavenly gift and have fallen away, and it says that it is impossible to restore them again to repentance (Hebrews 6:4-6). Jesus tells his disciples to remain in him.

Reformed and Baptist traditions teach the perseverance of the saints: those whom God has truly saved will be kept by him to the end, and those who fall away show that their faith was not real. This is sometimes loosely called "once saved, always saved," though the phrase suggests that a decision is enough without a changed life. Arminian and Wesleyan traditions teach that believers are secure in Christ but may through persistent unbelief or deliberate sin forsake the faith. Catholic teaching holds that grace can be lost by mortal sin and restored by confession. Orthodox teaching speaks of an ongoing journey, with real dangers.

On one thing all agree: the believer's security rests on Christ's faithfulness and not on his own grip. And the Bible expects those who belong to Christ to know it, through the Spirit, through the fruit of a changed life, and through the promises of the gospel.

# Glorification: the end of the road
@ref ROM 8:29-30; 1JN 3:2; PHP 3:20-21; 1CO 15:51-53

Romans 8 gives a chain of five links that stretches from before time to after it: "those He predestined, He also called; those He called, He also justified; those He justified, He also glorified" (Romans 8:30). The last link is stated in the past tense, as if it were already done, because for those who belong to Christ the outcome is certain.

Glorification means that believers will be completely freed from sin and its effects, and will share in the glory of Christ. It will take place when he returns. John says, "we know that when Christ appears, we will be like Him, for we will see Him as He is" (1 John 3:2). Paul says that the Lord Jesus Christ "will transform our lowly bodies to be like His glorious body" (Philippians 3:21).

Glorification has several parts. The soul will be made perfect, with no remaining sin or longing for it. The body will be raised, no longer subject to decay, weakness and death. Paul says, "we will not all sleep, but we will all be changed, in an instant, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet" (1 Corinthians 15:51-52). The creation will be renewed, so that the whole world is set free. And the people of God will live in his presence forever.

This is the final answer to the problem set out in the earlier lessons of this course. The fall damaged the whole of humanity, and the cross and resurrection begin the healing, and glorification completes it. Christians are not told to expect a perfect life now, but to hold on with hope. "Our citizenship is in heaven," Paul says, and "we eagerly await a Savior from there, the Lord Jesus Christ" (Philippians 3:20).

# Faith and works: Paul and James together
@ref ROM 4:1-5; JAS 2:14-26; EPH 2:8-10; GAL 5:6
@conf scholars

Many readers meet a puzzle when they set two statements side by side. Paul says that a man is justified by faith apart from works of the law (Romans 3:28). James says that a man is justified by his deeds and not by faith alone (James 2:24). Martin Luther, in his preface to the New Testament in 1522, called James "an epistle of straw," though he later softened his view. Are the two in conflict?

Most Christian interpreters say no, and the reason is in the words. Paul is dealing with a particular question: how can a sinner be put right with God? His answer is that the basis is not the works of the law, meaning the observances that marked out Israel, such as circumcision, but faith in Christ. James is dealing with a different question: how do you know a profession of faith is genuine? His answer is that real faith produces action. He says, "faith without deeds is dead" (James 2:26), and gives the examples of Abraham and Rahab.

The two also use the words in different senses. Paul uses "justify" to mean a declaration that someone is righteous in God's sight. James is using it in a way that means a faith is shown to be true, vindicated by what it does. They also use "faith" differently. For James, a faith that is merely agreeing to facts, which "even the demons believe" (James 2:19), is not real faith at all.

Paul himself insists on the same thing. In the same chapter of Ephesians that says we are saved by grace, through faith, and not by works, he goes on to say, "For we are God’s workmanship, created in Christ Jesus to do good works" (Ephesians 2:10). In Galatians he says that what matters is "faith expressing itself through love" (Galatians 5:6).

A short summary is that we are saved by faith alone, but the faith that saves is never alone. It is the root, and works are the fruit.
