# Genesis
@id book-gen
@ref GEN 1:1; GEN 12:1-3; GEN 50:20
@conf scholars

Genesis, the first book of the Bible, has fifty chapters. The English name comes from the Greek word for "origin," while Jews call it Bereshit, "In the beginning," after its first word. It is a book of beginnings: of the world, of marriage, of sin and death, of nations and languages, and of the family through which God chose to bless them.

Jewish and Christian tradition has held that Moses wrote Genesis along with the next four books, probably during the wilderness years, which would place it in the fifteenth or thirteenth century BC depending on the date of the exodus. Many modern scholars think the Pentateuch grew over centuries from older sources and was given its final shape around or after the exile. Whichever view you take, the book presents itself as a single story, and the church has read it that way.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 11: the beginnings of the whole human family. Creation, the fall, Cain and Abel, the flood, and Babel.
- Chapters 12 to 25: Abraham, called to leave his home and trust God's promise of a land, a nation and a blessing.
- Chapters 25 to 36: Isaac and Jacob, whose family gives the nation its name, Israel.
- Chapters 37 to 50: Joseph, sold into Egypt, who rises to save the family from famine.

## What to look for
Watch the promise. God speaks it to Abraham in chapter 12, repeats it to Isaac and Jacob, and keeps it alive through barren wives, family feuds and famine. The book is honest about its heroes, and the credit goes to God. It also plants seeds that the rest of Scripture harvests: the promise of a seed who will crush the serpent, the blessing to all nations, and a ruler from Judah. Genesis ends with Joseph's coffin in Egypt and a hope of going home.

# Exodus
@id book-exo
@ref EXO 3:13-15; EXO 12:12-13; EXO 20:1-3

Exodus, the second book, has forty chapters. The English title is the Greek word for "departure," and it names the event at the center of the Old Testament, the rescue of Israel from slavery in Egypt. The Hebrew title, Shemot, means "Names," from its opening words listing the sons of Jacob who came down to Egypt.

By tradition Moses is the author, and the book itself says he wrote down some of what happened (Exodus 24:4). The date of the exodus is debated. The early view puts it about 1446 BC, based on 1 Kings 6:1, and the later one about 1260 BC, based on the Egyptian city Rameses mentioned in Exodus 1:11.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 15: slavery, the call of Moses at the burning bush, the ten plagues, the first Passover and the crossing of the sea.
- Chapters 15 to 18: the first weeks in the wilderness, with bitter water, manna and quail, and water from the rock.
- Chapters 19 to 24: the covenant at Sinai, with the Ten Commandments and the laws that follow.
- Chapters 25 to 40: the tabernacle, the golden calf and its aftermath, and the glory of the LORD filling the tent.

## What to look for
The book is about redemption and presence. God saves first and then gives the law, and he saves in order to live among his people. The name he reveals at the bush, "I AM WHO I AM," is the name the rest of the Bible keeps returning to. Look for the Passover lamb, the mediator who goes up the mountain, the bread from heaven and the tent of meeting. The New Testament reads every one of them as pointing to Jesus.

# Leviticus
@id book-lev
@ref LEV 16:29-30; LEV 19:1-2; LEV 19:18

Leviticus has twenty-seven chapters, and it is the instruction manual for life with God in the middle of the camp. Its English name comes from the Greek and Latin titles, which refer to the tribe of Levi, whose men served as priests and helpers in the sanctuary. Jews call it Vayikra, "And he called," from the first words.

The book is set at Sinai, at the foot of the mountain, between the building of the tabernacle at the end of Exodus and the departure at the beginning of Numbers. Tradition assigns it to Moses. Most of it is spoken by God directly, and the phrase "the LORD spoke to Moses" comes up over and over.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 7: the five main offerings (the burnt offering, grain offering, peace offering, sin offering and guilt offering) and how priests handle them.
- Chapters 8 to 10: the ordination of Aaron and his sons, and the death of two priests who offered unauthorized fire.
- Chapters 11 to 15: what is clean and unclean, including food, childbirth, skin disease and bodily discharges.
- Chapter 16: the Day of Atonement.
- Chapters 17 to 27: the call to holiness in daily life, including love for neighbor, festivals, the Sabbath year and the year of Jubilee.

## What to look for
Two ideas hold the book together: God is holy, and sin is costly. Sacrifices let a sinner come near without being consumed, and they teach that forgiveness takes a life. Hebrews explains that these were shadows, and that Christ is the final sacrifice and the true high priest. Notice also how much of the book is about ordinary things such as honest scales and care for foreigners. Holiness is not only for priests.

# Numbers
@id book-num
@ref NUM 6:24-26; NUM 14:1-10; NUM 21:4-9

Numbers has thirty-six chapters. The English title, from the Greek, refers to the two censuses of Israel's men that open the book and begin its second half. The Hebrew name, Bemidbar, "In the wilderness," is closer to the story, which is about forty years of wandering between Sinai and the edge of the promised land.

Tradition names Moses as author. Its events fall in the years after the exodus, whether you date that to 1446 or 1260 BC. Some stories are told with plain frankness about the failures of Israel and also of Moses and Aaron.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 10: at Sinai. The census, the order of the camp, the work of the Levites, laws for purity, and the priestly blessing.
- Chapters 11 to 20: the journey and the rebellions. The spies at Kadesh, the refusal to enter the land, Korah's revolt, and Moses striking the rock.
- Chapters 21 to 25: the approach to Canaan, including the bronze serpent, Balaam and his donkey, and trouble with Moab.
- Chapters 26 to 36: the second census, the new generation, Joshua named as successor, and instructions for settling the land.

## What to look for
The theme is the contrast between God's faithfulness and Israel's unbelief. People grumble about food, water and leaders, and at Kadesh they refuse to trust God's promise, so an entire generation dies in the desert. Yet God keeps feeding them and does not cancel the plan. Even Balaam, hired to curse Israel, ends up blessing them. Paul uses the wilderness years as a warning for Christians (1 Corinthians 10), and Jesus uses the bronze serpent as a picture of his own cross.

# Deuteronomy
@id book-deu
@ref DEU 6:4-9; DEU 30:19-20; DEU 34:10-12
@conf scholars

Deuteronomy has thirty-four chapters. Its name means "second law" in Greek, a mistranslation of a phrase meaning a copy of this instruction, but the title fits, since Moses restates the law to a new generation on the plains of Moab. The Hebrew title, Devarim, simply means "Words."

Tradition holds that Moses spoke and wrote it just before his death, with a later hand adding the account of his burial in chapter 34. Many scholars connect the book with the reforms of King Josiah in the seventh century BC, in which "the Book of the Law" was found in the temple. Its form closely follows that of ancient treaties between kings and their subjects, which suggests that it is deliberately a covenant document.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 4: Moses looks back on the journey from Sinai and urges obedience.
- Chapters 5 to 11: the Ten Commandments repeated, the Shema, and the call to love God wholeheartedly.
- Chapters 12 to 26: detailed laws about worship, justice, family, and care for the poor.
- Chapters 27 to 30: blessings and curses, and the covenant renewal.
- Chapters 31 to 34: Joshua commissioned, Moses' song and blessing, and his death on Mount Nebo.

## What to look for
Deuteronomy asks for love, not just rule keeping: "Love the LORD your God with all your heart." It stresses remembering, teaching children and choosing life. It also predicts that Israel will fail and be scattered, and then be brought back, which is why later prophets keep quoting it. Jesus quotes Deuteronomy three times to answer the devil in the wilderness (Matthew 4), and he calls the Shema's command the greatest of all.

# Joshua
@id book-jos
@ref JOS 1:7-9; JOS 6:20; JOS 24:14-15

Joshua has twenty-four chapters. It is named for the man who succeeded Moses and led Israel into Canaan. His name in Hebrew, Yehoshua, means "the LORD saves," and the Greek form of it is Jesus. The book picks up exactly where Deuteronomy leaves off, and together with Judges, Samuel and Kings it forms what Jewish tradition calls the Former Prophets.

The book does not name its author. Jewish tradition says Joshua wrote most of it, and the text itself says he wrote down the covenant renewal at Shechem (Joshua 24:26). The events would date to about 1400 BC on the early chronology or about 1250 to 1200 BC on the later one. The book was probably put into its present form somewhat later.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 5: preparations. Crossing the Jordan, circumcision of the new generation, and the Passover at Gilgal.
- Chapters 6 to 12: the conquest. Jericho, Ai, the Gibeonite treaty, and campaigns in the south and the north.
- Chapters 13 to 21: the land divided among the tribes, with cities of refuge and towns for the Levites.
- Chapters 22 to 24: the eastern tribes go home, and Joshua's farewell speeches and the covenant at Shechem.

## What to look for
The theme is that God keeps his word: "Not one of all the LORD’s good promises to the house of Israel had failed; everything was fulfilled" (Joshua 21:45). The book is also demanding. It requires faith in battle and loyalty in daily life, and the conquest accounts raise hard moral questions that Christians read carefully. Notice Rahab, a Canaanite who is saved by faith, and the "Commander of the LORD’s army" whom Joshua meets before Jericho.

# Judges
@id book-jdg
@ref JDG 2:16-19; JDG 6:11-16; JDG 21:25

Judges has twenty-one chapters and tells of Israel's life between the death of Joshua and the rise of the monarchy. A judge in this book is less a courtroom official than a military and moral leader raised up by God for a time of crisis. The book is the darkest in the Former Prophets, and it ends on a note of ruin.

Its author is unknown. Jewish tradition suggests Samuel. The repeated line that "in those days there was no king in Israel" suggests it was written once the monarchy had begun, perhaps in the time of David or later. The events fill the years between the entry into Canaan and about 1050 BC, when Saul became king.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 2: the incomplete conquest and an explanation of why the people fell into trouble.
- Chapters 3 to 16: the stories of the judges. Othniel, Ehud, Deborah and Barak, Gideon, Abimelech, Jephthah and Samson.
- Chapters 17 to 21: two appalling stories of idolatry and violence, which show how far Israel has sunk.

## What to look for
The pattern repeats in cycles of sin, oppression, a cry for help, rescue and rest, and each loop sinks lower. The leaders also decline, from faithful Othniel to flawed Gideon and then to Samson, who lives for himself. The book is meant to make the reader hungry for something better. By the end you are asking for a king who will do right, and then, as the Bible goes on, for a king who will do more than any human king could.

# Ruth
@id book-rut
@ref RUT 1:16-17; RUT 3:9-11; RUT 4:13-17

Ruth has four chapters and is a short story, almost a novella, of exceptional beauty. It is set "in the days when the judges ruled," which makes it a gleam of light against the dark background of Judges. In the Christian Old Testament it follows Judges. The Hebrew Bible places it among the Writings and reads it at the feast of Weeks.

The author is unnamed, though Jewish tradition says Samuel. The setting is the period of the judges, and the genealogy at the end, which reaches David, suggests it was written in or after his time. It was meant to be read as a quiet answer to the question of how God works in ordinary lives during hard years.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: famine, a move to Moab, three deaths, and the return of Naomi with Ruth.
- Chapter 2: Ruth gleans in the field of Boaz and finds his kindness.
- Chapter 3: Naomi's plan, and Ruth's request to Boaz on the threshing floor.
- Chapter 4: Boaz acts as kinsman-redeemer at the town gate, marries Ruth, and a son is born.

## What to look for
Look for the quiet providence in a story where God rarely acts openly. Look for loyalty, shown by Ruth to Naomi and by Boaz to Ruth. Above all, look for the redeemer. Boaz is a relative who buys back what was lost, and the Bible's longer story uses that picture for Christ. The last verses place a foreign woman in the family tree of David, and therefore in the family tree of Jesus (Matthew 1:5).

# 1 and 2 Samuel
@id book-1sa
@ref 1SA 16:7; 2SA 7:12-16; 2SA 12:7-13

First and Second Samuel were originally a single scroll, divided in the Greek translation because of its length. Together they have fifty-five chapters. They are named for Samuel, the last judge and the prophet who anointed Israel's first two kings, though he dies in 1 Samuel 25 and is not the author of the later chapters.

The books do not say who wrote them. The writer used earlier records, and the work seems to have been put together some time after the kingdom split in 931 BC. The story covers about a century, from the birth of Samuel around 1100 BC to the end of David's reign around 970 BC.

## How it is arranged
- 1 Samuel 1 to 7: Samuel's birth and ministry, and the loss and return of the ark.
- 1 Samuel 8 to 15: Israel demands a king, and Saul is anointed, then rejected.
- 1 Samuel 16 to 31: David is anointed, kills Goliath, serves Saul, flees from him, and Saul dies.
- 2 Samuel 1 to 10: David becomes king, takes Jerusalem, and receives God's covenant promise.
- 2 Samuel 11 to 20: David's sin with Bathsheba and the family disasters that follow.
- 2 Samuel 21 to 24: appendices, including David's last songs and the census.

## What to look for
These books ask what a king should be. Saul looks the part but will not obey. David is flawed but repentant, and God makes the promise of an eternal throne to him in 2 Samuel 7, which is the hinge of the whole Old Testament. Watch how the LORD judges the heart and not the outward appearance. Hannah's song at the start and David's song near the end are meant to frame the books, and Mary's song in Luke 1 echoes the first.

# 1 and 2 Kings
@id book-1ki
@ref 1KI 8:27-30; 1KI 18:36-39; 2KI 25:27-30

First and Second Kings were also a single scroll, and they have forty-seven chapters between them. They carry the story of Israel from David's death to the fall of Jerusalem, about four centuries from about 970 BC to 586 BC. They pass judgment on each king by one measure: did he lead the people to worship the LORD alone?

The author is unnamed. Jewish tradition says Jeremiah, which is possible because his ministry overlaps the last events. The last event mentioned, the release of King Jehoiachin from prison in Babylon, took place about 561 BC (2 Kings 25:27-30), so the final form dates to the exile or soon after.

## How it is arranged
- 1 Kings 1 to 11: Solomon's reign, the building of the temple, and his decline.
- 1 Kings 12 to 2 Kings 17: the divided kingdom. The north falls to Assyria in 722 BC. The prophets Elijah and Elisha appear.
- 2 Kings 18 to 25: Judah alone. The reforms of Hezekiah and Josiah, then the Babylonian invasion and the destruction of Jerusalem.

## What to look for
Kings explains why the exile happened, in the language of the covenant at Sinai and the warnings in Deuteronomy. It is not a neutral chronicle. It selects what matters for faith, which is why Omri, an important king politically, gets only a few verses. Yet the last scene is a flicker of hope. The line of David is not extinguished. A descendant is alive and honored in Babylon, which is the Bible's way of saying that God has not finished.

# 1 and 2 Chronicles
@id book-1ch
@ref 1CH 17:11-14; 2CH 7:14; 2CH 36:22-23
@conf scholars

First and Second Chronicles are another pair that began as one book, with sixty-five chapters in all. They retell the story from Adam to the return from Babylon, but with a different aim from Samuel and Kings. In the Hebrew Bible they come last of all, and the Hebrew title means "the events of the days."

The author is unnamed, though Jewish tradition says Ezra. The closing verses refer to the decree of Cyrus in 538 BC, so the book was written after the return, probably in the fifth or fourth century BC. It was meant for a small, discouraged community living under Persian rule in a rebuilt Jerusalem.

## How it is arranged
- 1 Chronicles 1 to 9: family trees from Adam to those who returned from exile.
- 1 Chronicles 10 to 29: the reign of David, with the ark, the temple plans and his last charge to Solomon.
- 2 Chronicles 1 to 9: Solomon and the temple.
- 2 Chronicles 10 to 36: the kings of Judah, ending with the fall of Jerusalem and Cyrus's decree.

## What to look for
Chronicles focuses on temple worship, the line of David and the people's identity. It leaves out David's sin with Bathsheba and concentrates on the promises. The message to the returned exiles is that they are still the people of the promise, that worship in the temple still matters, and that God answers when his people humble themselves and seek him (2 Chronicles 7:14). The last words of the Hebrew Bible are an invitation to go up to Jerusalem.

# Ezra and Nehemiah
@id book-ezr
@ref EZR 1:1-4; NEH 2:17-18; NEH 8:1-8

Ezra and Nehemiah are two books, with ten and thirteen chapters, that in the Hebrew Bible form one scroll. They tell what happened when the Jews came home from Babylon, and they are the last historical books of the Old Testament in time.

Tradition credits Ezra with writing the book that bears his name, and Nehemiah's own memoirs are quoted in the second book, in the first person. Together they cover roughly a century, from the decree of Cyrus in 538 BC to Nehemiah's second term as governor after 433 BC.

## How it is arranged
- Ezra 1 to 6: the return under Zerubbabel and the rebuilding of the temple, completed in 516 BC.
- Ezra 7 to 10: Ezra arrives in 458 BC and addresses marriages with foreign women.
- Nehemiah 1 to 7: Nehemiah, the king's cupbearer, rebuilds the wall of Jerusalem in fifty-two days, despite opposition.
- Nehemiah 8 to 10: Ezra reads the Law to the people, and they renew the covenant.
- Nehemiah 11 to 13: the city is repopulated and the wall dedicated, followed by Nehemiah's reforms.

## What to look for
The books show how to rebuild when everything has been lost. They are full of prayer. Nehemiah prays before speaking to the king and again under attack, and Ezra prays over the people's sin. They also stress the Word of God: when the law is read, the people weep, and then Nehemiah tells them that the joy of the LORD is their strength. The final lesson is that reform is never finished, which is why Nehemiah ends with a prayer.

# Esther
@id book-est
@ref EST 4:12-16; EST 7:1-6; EST 9:20-22

Esther has ten chapters and is the story of a Jewish orphan who becomes queen of Persia and saves her people. It is set in the capital at Susa during the reign of Ahasuerus, who is identified with Xerxes I, around 483 to 473 BC. It is the book of the Bible in which God is never named, and that is deliberate.

The author is unknown. Jewish tradition has suggested Mordecai or Ezra. The details of Persian court life are accurate and the writer was clearly familiar with the empire, so the book was probably written not long after the events. The Jews still read it aloud each year at the feast of Purim.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 and 2: the queen Vashti is dismissed, and Esther is chosen as queen.
- Chapters 3 to 5: Haman, the king's chief minister, plots to destroy all the Jews. Esther decides to approach the king uninvited.
- Chapters 6 to 8: the tables turn. Haman is hanged on the gallows he built for Mordecai, and the Jews are allowed to defend themselves.
- Chapters 9 and 10: the Jews are saved, and Purim is established.

## What to look for
The hidden hand of God is the point. Coincidences stack up, from the queen's selection to the king's sleepless night, and Mordecai's challenge gives the book its center: "who knows if perhaps you have come to the kingdom for such a time as this?" Esther is a reminder that even when God seems absent, he is at work, and that faith sometimes means taking a risk.
