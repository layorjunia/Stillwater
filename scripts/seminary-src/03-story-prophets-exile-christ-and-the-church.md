# What the prophets were for
@ref AMO 5:21-24; MIC 6:6-8; JER 7:1-7; HEB 1:1-2
@conf scholars
@q Which of the prophets' charges, about worship without justice, would be hardest for people today to hear?

From the ninth century BC to about 400 BC, God sent a long line of men and a few women to speak to Israel and Judah. We call them the prophets. The popular idea is that a prophet was a fortune teller. In the Bible a prophet is first of all a spokesman, someone who stands in the council of God and then tells the people what has been said. Sometimes that included the future, but most of the time it was about the present: how to live now, given the covenant God made at Sinai.

The prophets were, in a sense, covenant lawyers. At Sinai God had set out blessings for faithfulness and curses for betrayal (Deuteronomy 27 to 30). When Israel turned to other gods, trampled the poor and treated worship as a lucky charm, the prophets brought the charge. Amos puts God's words about their festivals plainly: "But let justice roll on like a river, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24). Micah says that what God wants is "to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God" (Micah 6:8).

Their message had a pattern. First came a charge of idolatry and injustice. Then came a warning of judgment, often by foreign armies and exile. Then, and this is easy to miss, came a promise of restoration: a new covenant, a new king from David's line, a new heart, even a new creation. The same book that threatens ruin also holds out the most extravagant hope in the Old Testament.

The prophets are divided into the "major" ones (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel), named for the length of their books, and the twelve "minor" ones. The letter to the Hebrews summarizes the whole line: "On many past occasions and in many different ways, God spoke to our fathers through the prophets," but "in these last days He has spoken to us by His Son" (Hebrews 1:1-2).

# Isaiah: holy, holy, holy
@ref ISA 6:1-8; ISA 9:6; ISA 53:5-6; ISA 2:2-4
@conf scholars

Isaiah of Jerusalem served as a prophet for roughly forty years from about 740 BC, in the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah. His call comes in one of the great scenes of Scripture. In the year King Uzziah dies, he sees the Lord seated on a high throne, with the train of his robe filling the temple, and seraphim calling to one another, "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of Hosts; all the earth is full of His glory" (Isaiah 6:3). The doorposts shake. Isaiah says, "Woe is me, for I am ruined." A seraph touches his lips with a glowing coal from the altar, and then he hears the Lord asking, "Whom shall I send?" and answers, "Here am I. Send me!"

The sixty-six chapters of the book move between judgment on Judah and its neighbors and promises of a future king and a future world. Isaiah says that "the virgin will be with child and give birth to a son" named Immanuel, God with us (Isaiah 7:14), and that a child will be born whose names are "Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace" (Isaiah 9:6). He describes a time when the nations will stream to Zion to learn God's ways, and they "will beat their swords into plowshares" (Isaiah 2:2-4).

From chapter 40 the tone turns to comfort, and a figure called the Servant of the LORD appears. In chapter 53 the Servant is "pierced for our transgressions" and "the LORD has laid upon Him the iniquity of us all." The New Testament quotes Isaiah more than any other prophet, and Philip uses this chapter to explain Jesus to an Ethiopian official (Acts 8:26-35).

Most scholars of earlier generations took the whole book as the work of one prophet, and many Christians still do. Many modern scholars think chapters 40 to 66 were written by a later author, or authors, during or after the exile. Both camps agree on the book's final shape and on its message: God is holy, he judges, and he will save.

# Israel falls to Assyria
@ref 2KI 17:6-8; 2KI 17:13-18; HOS 11:1-4; AMO 7:10-15
@conf scholars

The northern kingdom of Israel lasted about two hundred years, from the split around 931 BC to its fall in 722 BC. None of its nineteen kings is said to have done what was right. During its last decades the great power of the region was Assyria, a brutal empire based in Nineveh, which collected tribute from smaller nations and deported those who rebelled.

God sent prophets to the north in these years. Amos, a shepherd and fig farmer from Tekoa in Judah, went to Bethel and denounced the rich for selling the needy for a pair of sandals. The priest there told him to go home and prophesy in his own country, and Amos replied that he was no professional prophet, just a herdsman, and that the LORD had taken him and told him to go. Hosea, whose wife left him for other lovers, was told to take her back as a picture of God's faithfulness to unfaithful Israel. In a verse Matthew later applies to the child Jesus, God says through Hosea, "When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called My son" (Hosea 11:1). Jonah, active in the first half of the same century, was sent to Nineveh itself.

The end came in 722 BC. After a siege of about three years, Samaria, the capital, fell to the Assyrian king, and large numbers of its people were deported to other parts of the empire. Others were brought in to settle the land, and their descendants mixed with those who remained, which is part of the background to the later tensions between Jews and Samaritans.

The writer of 2 Kings gives the cause in one long, sorrowful paragraph. The people had "rejected His statutes and the covenant He had made with their fathers," despite the warnings of every prophet (2 Kings 17:13-18). The ten tribes of the north never returned as a nation. Only Judah, and Jerusalem with David's house, remained.

# Hezekiah, Josiah and the last good kings
@ref 2KI 18:1-7; 2KI 19:14-19; 2KI 22:8-13; 2KI 23:25
@conf scholars

Judah outlasted its northern neighbor by about a century and a half, and a few of its kings tried to turn the nation back. Hezekiah, who became king around 715 BC, removed the high places, broke the bronze serpent Moses had made, which people had begun to burn incense to, and reopened the temple. The text says that there was no one like him among the kings of Judah, "either before him or after him" (2 Kings 18:5).

In 701 BC the Assyrian king Sennacherib invaded Judah, captured dozens of its fortified cities and surrounded Jerusalem. Hezekiah took the threatening letter into the temple, spread it out before the LORD and prayed. According to Kings, the angel of the LORD struck down the Assyrian camp in one night, and Sennacherib went home. Sennacherib's own prism records that he shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem "like a bird in a cage" but does not say he took the city. In preparation for the siege Hezekiah had dug a tunnel to bring water from the Gihon spring inside the walls, and an inscription from it was found in 1880.

The other great reformer was Josiah, who became king at eight years old and was about twenty-six when workmen repairing the temple found "the Book of the Law" (2 Kings 22:8). Most scholars think it was Deuteronomy or part of it. When it was read to him, he tore his clothes, led a nationwide reform, destroyed idols, and kept a Passover such as had not been held since the time of the judges. The writer says that no king before or after "turned to the LORD with all his heart and with all his soul and with all his strength" (2 Kings 23:25).

Josiah died at Megiddo in 609 BC, fighting Pharaoh Neco. The reforms had not reached the heart of the people. Within a generation Judah was finished.

# Jeremiah and the new covenant
@ref JER 1:4-10; JER 29:10-11; JER 31:31-34; LUK 22:20; HEB 8:7-13
@conf scholars

Jeremiah was a priest's son from Anathoth, near Jerusalem. He was called young, around 627 BC, and told that God had known him before he was formed in the womb. He protested that he was only a boy, and God replied that he would put his words in Jeremiah's mouth. The task was hard: "to uproot and tear down, to destroy and overthrow, to build and plant" (Jeremiah 1:10). For about forty years he warned that Babylon would come, and he was beaten, put in stocks, thrown into a cistern and accused of treason.

He is sometimes called the weeping prophet, and the title fits. He loved the people he condemned. When the first Judeans were taken to Babylon in 597 BC he wrote them a letter telling them to build houses, plant gardens, seek the welfare of the city where they now lived and wait. "When Babylon's seventy years are complete, I will attend to you and confirm My promise to restore you to this place" (Jeremiah 29:10). The verse that follows, "For I know the plans I have for you," was spoken to exiles, and its promise is of a future after long waiting.

His great word of hope is in chapter 31. The old covenant, made at Sinai, was one "they broke." God will make a new one: "I will put My law in their minds and inscribe it on their hearts. And I will be their God, and they will be My people." Everyone will know him, "from the least of them to the greatest," and he will "forgive their iniquities and will remember their sins no more" (Jeremiah 31:31-34).

At the Last Supper Jesus lifted a cup and said, "This cup is the new covenant in My blood, which is poured out for you" (Luke 22:20). The letter to the Hebrews quotes the whole passage and says the new covenant has arrived (Hebrews 8:7-13).

# The fall of Jerusalem
@ref 2KI 24:10-17; 2KI 25:1-12; LAM 3:19-26; HAB 3:17-19
@conf scholars

In 605 BC Babylon defeated Egypt at Carchemish and became the dominant power of the Near East. Under Nebuchadnezzar it moved against Judah. In 597 BC, after a short siege, the young king Jehoiachin surrendered, and Nebuchadnezzar took him to Babylon along with thousands of the leading people, including the priest Ezekiel. He put Jehoiachin's uncle Zedekiah on the throne. Zedekiah rebelled, against Jeremiah's repeated warning, and the army came again.

The siege of Jerusalem lasted about eighteen months. In the summer of 586 BC the walls were breached. Zedekiah tried to escape at night, was caught, and was made to watch his sons killed before he was blinded and taken to Babylon in chains. Then Nebuzaradan, the captain of the guard, came to the city: "He burned down the house of the LORD, the royal palace, and all the houses of Jerusalem" (2 Kings 25:9). The walls were torn down, the temple's bronze and gold carried away, and most of the people who survived were deported. The poorest were left to farm the land.

The date is given as 586 BC in many books and 587 BC in others, depending on how the Babylonian and Judean calendars are reconciled. What matters is what ended. The land, the city, the temple, the kingdom of David and the whole structure through which Israel knew God's presence were gone.

The book of Lamentations is the poetry of that grief. In the middle of it the writer says, "Yet I call this to mind, and therefore I have hope: Because of the loving devotion of the LORD we are not consumed" (Lamentations 3:21-22). The prophet Habakkuk, who had seen it coming, said that even if the fig tree does not bloom and the fields produce no food, "yet I will exult in the LORD" (Habakkuk 3:17-18).

# By the rivers of Babylon
@ref PSA 137:1-6; EZK 37:1-14; DAN 6:10; ISA 40:1-2
@conf scholars

The Jews carried into Babylon did not become slaves in chains. They were settled in communities along the canals, such as the one at Tel Abib by the Kebar River, where Ezekiel lived, and many farmed, built houses and engaged in trade. Archaeologists have found cuneiform tablets from this period that name Judean families in a town called Al-Yahudu, which means "Judah-town." Still, they were exiles, far from Jerusalem and the temple, and they were not sure that God could be found anywhere else. Psalm 137 captures the mood: "By the rivers of Babylon we sat and wept when we remembered Zion."

God sent prophets to them. Ezekiel saw visions of the glory of the LORD leaving the temple and then, in later chapters, returning. In the valley of dry bones he is told to prophesy to a field of skeletons, and they stand up as an army. God explains: "these bones are the whole house of Israel" who say that their hope is lost, and he promises to open their graves and "put My Spirit in you and you will live" (Ezekiel 37:11-14). Daniel, taken to Babylon young, rose in the Babylonian and Persian courts while keeping his faith, and kept praying three times a day toward Jerusalem even when it was illegal (Daniel 6:10).

The exile also did something to the Jewish people. It cured them of open idolatry, which was never again their main problem. It produced the gathering of Scripture: scribes copied and edited the older books, and the synagogue, a place of reading and prayer that did not require a temple, probably grew up in this period. It also gave a new word to the vocabulary of hope. When Isaiah 40 opens, "Comfort, comfort My people," the exile is what it is speaking to.

# The return, and the second temple
@ref EZR 1:1-4; EZR 3:10-13; EZR 6:13-16; HAG 2:3-9
@conf scholars

In 539 BC the Persian king Cyrus captured Babylon. The Bible says that in his first year he issued a decree: "The LORD, the God of heaven, who has given me all the kingdoms of the earth, has appointed me to build a house for Him at Jerusalem in Judah." Anyone from God's people could go up and build it (Ezra 1:2-3). The book of Isaiah names Cyrus as God's shepherd and anointed (Isaiah 44:28; 45:1). The Cyrus Cylinder, a clay barrel in the British Museum, shows that Cyrus did have a policy of allowing deported peoples to return and restore their temples, though it does not mention the Jews by name.

Nearly fifty thousand people returned under Sheshbazzar and then Zerubbabel, a descendant of David, with the priest Joshua. They rebuilt the altar and laid the foundation of the temple in about 536 BC. At the ceremony the young shouted for joy, but some of the old priests who remembered the first temple wept aloud, and the sounds mingled so that no one could tell them apart (Ezra 3:12-13). Then opposition and discouragement stopped the work for about fifteen years.

In 520 BC the prophets Haggai and Zechariah urged the people to start again, and the temple was finished in the sixth year of Darius, which was 516 BC, seventy years after the first was destroyed (Ezra 6:15). Haggai asked, "Who is left among you who saw this house in its former glory? How does it look to you now? Does it not appear to you like nothing in comparison?" (Haggai 2:3). Then came the promise that the glory of this latter house would be greater than the former.

It was a modest building, and for many Jews it never quite felt like the answer. Even after the return, the nation was a small province of a Persian empire. They were back in the land, but the promised kingdom still had not come.

# Ezra, Nehemiah and the rebuilt wall
@ref EZR 7:6-10; NEH 2:1-8; NEH 6:15; NEH 8:1-8
@conf scholars

Two more men came after the first return, both with Persian authority. Ezra, a priest and scribe, led a second group to Jerusalem in 458 BC. The text says he "had set his heart to study the Law of the LORD, to practice it, and to teach its statutes and ordinances in Israel" (Ezra 7:10). His concern was the spiritual life of the community, and he found that many had married women from the surrounding peoples and adopted their ways. The book describes a painful process of repentance.

Nehemiah, a cupbearer to King Artaxerxes, was in the palace at Susa in 445 BC when a relative told him that the walls of Jerusalem were still broken down. He wept for days and then prayed. When the king noticed his sad face and asked what he wanted, Nehemiah said a quick prayer to the God of heaven and asked to be sent to rebuild the city. The king agreed and gave him letters of passage and timber.

In Jerusalem Nehemiah inspected the ruins at night and then organized the whole population to build, each family working on the section near its home. Local rivals mocked and then threatened them, so half the men worked while the other half stood guard, with a trumpeter beside the commander. The wall was finished "in fifty-two days" (Nehemiah 6:15).

Then came the high point. On the first day of the seventh month the people gathered in the square, and Ezra stood on a wooden platform and read the Law from morning until noon, while the Levites explained its meaning. The people wept as they heard it, and then they celebrated. The prophet Malachi, whose book comes last in the Christian Old Testament, probably worked in this period. His last words promise the coming of Elijah before the great day of the LORD (Malachi 4:5).

# The four hundred quiet years
@ref MAL 4:5-6; DAN 8:21-22; MAT 1:1; LUK 3:1-2
@conf scholars

Between the last words of Malachi, around 430 BC, and the first words about John the Baptist, there are roughly four hundred years in which the Bible records no new prophetic voice. Protestants call this the intertestamental period, and it was anything but quiet. The Persian empire gave way to Alexander the Great, who swept through the region in 333 and 332 BC, and after his early death his generals split his empire. Judea was caught between two of those kingdoms, the Ptolemies in Egypt and the Seleucids in Syria.

Greek language and culture spread everywhere. In Alexandria in Egypt, in the third century BC, Jewish scholars began translating their Scriptures into Greek, producing what is called the Septuagint, which is the Bible the apostles often quote. Then in 167 BC the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes tried to force the Jews to give up their religion. He banned circumcision and the Sabbath, and set up an altar to Zeus in the temple. A priest named Mattathias and his sons, the Maccabees, started a revolt. Judas Maccabeus recaptured Jerusalem and rededicated the temple in 164 BC, which Jews still remember at Hanukkah.

For about a century the Maccabee family, called the Hasmoneans, ruled an independent Jewish state. Then Rome arrived. General Pompey took Jerusalem in 63 BC, and the Roman senate had named Herod, an Idumean, king of the Jews in 40 BC, though he took Jerusalem only in 37 BC. Herod rebuilt the temple on a magnificent scale. Along the way, groups such as the Pharisees, Sadducees and Essenes formed, and later the Zealots, each with its own idea of how to be faithful.

Through all of it the Jews looked for the fulfillment of the promises: a Messiah, a new covenant, a kingdom. They were back in the land, but under foreign rule. The world was waiting without knowing exactly for what.

# When the time had fully come
@ref GAL 4:4-5; LUK 2:1-7; MAT 2:1-6; MIC 5:2
@conf scholars

Paul says, "when the time had fully come, God sent His Son, born of a woman, born under the law" (Galatians 4:4). The phrase hints that history had been prepared. By the first century AD the Roman Empire had created a stretch of peace around the Mediterranean, with roads and sea lanes in which a traveler could cross the whole region. Greek, the common language, could be understood almost everywhere. Jews had scattered through the empire and set up synagogues in nearly every city, with the Greek Scriptures read each Sabbath, and many Gentiles had become interested in a single God and a moral code. When the message of Jesus came, it found a path laid.

Jesus was born in Bethlehem during the reign of Caesar Augustus. Matthew says it was in the time of Herod the Great, and since Herod died in 4 BC, most scholars place the birth between about 6 and 4 BC. The odd fact that the BC and AD calendar puts Jesus' birth a few years before the year 1 comes from a sixth-century monk, Dionysius Exiguus, who made the calculation. Luke tells how Joseph and Mary, who lived in Nazareth, traveled to Bethlehem for a Roman registration, and how the baby was laid in a manger because there was no room in the inn. Matthew tells of wise men from the east who came looking for the newborn king of the Jews, and of how Herod's advisers quoted Micah: "you, Bethlehem Ephrathah, who are small among the clans of Judah, out of you will come forth for Me One to be ruler over Israel" (Micah 5:2).

The four Gospels each tell the story from a different angle, but they agree on the main fact: the long-promised king came as a baby, in a small town, to an ordinary family, under an occupying army.

# The kingdom of God has come near
@ref MRK 1:14-15; LUK 4:16-21; MAT 6:9-10; LUK 17:20-21
@conf scholars

Luke dates the start of John the Baptist's ministry to "the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar" (Luke 3:1), which is about AD 28 or 29. Jesus came to be baptized by John, heard the Father's voice from heaven, was tested for forty days in the wilderness, and then began to teach. Mark gives the headline of his preaching: "The time is fulfilled," He said, "and the kingdom of God is near. Repent and believe in the gospel!" (Mark 1:15).

The kingdom of God is the central subject of Jesus' teaching, and it is easy to misunderstand. It is not a place in the sky, and it is not simply a feeling inside. It is God's kingly rule, the reign that the prophets had promised, breaking into the world in the person of Jesus. When he heals the sick, drives out demons, forgives sins and eats with outcasts, these are the signs that the king has come. He teaches the disciples to pray, "Your kingdom come, Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven" (Matthew 6:10).

In Nazareth he reads from the scroll of Isaiah about good news for the poor, freedom for prisoners and sight for the blind, and then says, "Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:21). His stories, the parables, tell of seeds and treasure and a wedding feast, saying that the kingdom starts small and grows, costs everything and is a gift, and has already begun although its fullness is still to come.

Jesus chose twelve disciples, a number that many readers connect with the twelve tribes of Israel. His public ministry lasted something like three years. Most Christians hold that the kingdom has come in Jesus, is spreading through the church, and will be completed when he returns.

# The cross at the center
@ref MRK 10:45; MRK 15:33-39; ISA 53:4-6; ROM 5:6-8; GAL 3:13
@conf debated

All four Gospels spend a disproportionate share of their space on the last week of Jesus' life, and the cross stands at the center. Jesus rode into Jerusalem on a donkey while crowds shouted hosanna, overturned the tables in the temple, taught in its courts, and shared a Passover meal with his disciples on Thursday night. He was betrayed by Judas, arrested in the garden of Gethsemane, tried by the Jewish council and by the Roman governor Pontius Pilate, and sentenced to death by crucifixion, which was a Roman form of public execution for slaves and rebels.

Jesus had said ahead of time what it meant: "even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). On the cross he was mocked, darkness fell for three hours at midday, and he cried out in the words of Psalm 22. After a loud cry he died, and the temple curtain was torn from top to bottom. The Roman centurion said, "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (Mark 15:39).

The year is debated. Most scholars say the crucifixion happened on a Friday at Passover, either in AD 30 or AD 33, with a strong majority favoring one of those two. The New Testament, however, cares more about the meaning than the date. Paul writes that "God proves His love for us in this: While we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8).

The church has never had a single official theory of how the cross saves. Christians describe it as a ransom, a victory over the powers of evil, a sacrifice for sin, a substitute taking the penalty, and an example of love. All of them point to the one fact that the earliest preachers placed first: that "Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3).

# The resurrection: God's yes
@ref 1CO 15:3-8; 1CO 15:14-20; MAT 28:1-10; ACT 2:22-32
@conf scholars

According to all four Gospels, on the Sunday after the crucifixion women went to the tomb at dawn and found the stone rolled away and the body gone. Over the following weeks the disciples reported that they met Jesus alive, ate with him, touched him and heard him teach. Their reaction was not what anyone would expect. They had hidden in terror after the arrest, yet within weeks they were preaching in Jerusalem, in the very city where he had been killed, and willing to suffer for the claim.

The earliest written account of the resurrection is not in a Gospel but in a letter. Paul, writing about AD 55, says he is passing on what he received: that Christ died for our sins, was buried, "was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures," and appeared to Peter, to the Twelve, and "to more than five hundred brothers at once, most of whom are still living," then to James and all the apostles, and last of all to Paul himself (1 Corinthians 15:3-8). Scholars across the spectrum, believers and skeptics alike, generally date this summary to within a few years of the events.

Paul understands what hangs on it. "And if Christ has not been raised, our preaching is worthless, and so is your faith" (1 Corinthians 15:14). If he has been raised, then the cross was not a defeat but a victory. Death is not the last word, and the whole story of new creation has begun. Christ is "the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20), the first of a harvest.

The resurrection is the center of historic Christian belief. It is the reason the church exists, the reason Christians meet on the first day of the week, and the reason they say that Jesus is Lord.

# Pentecost: the church is born
@ref ACT 1:8; ACT 2:1-4; ACT 2:36-42; JOL 2:28-29
@conf scholars

For forty days after his resurrection Jesus appeared to his followers, speaking about the kingdom of God, and then he ascended into heaven. Before leaving, he told them to wait in Jerusalem for the gift the Father had promised, and then said, "you will receive power when the Holy Spirit comes upon you, and you will be My witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth" (Acts 1:8).

Ten days later was Pentecost, the Jewish feast of weeks, fifty days after Passover, when Jews from all over the empire were in Jerusalem. The disciples were together in one place, and "suddenly a sound like a mighty rushing wind came from heaven." Tongues like flames of fire rested on each of them, and they began to speak "in other tongues as the Spirit enabled them" (Acts 2:2-4). The crowd heard the message in their own languages, and some mocked, saying that the disciples were drunk.

Peter stood up and explained. This, he said, was what the prophet Joel had foretold, that God would pour out his Spirit on all people. He preached that Jesus of Nazareth, whom they had crucified, God had raised and made both Lord and Messiah. The crowd was cut to the heart, and asked what to do. Peter said, "Repent and be baptized," and about three thousand were added that day (Acts 2:38,41).

The new community met daily for the apostles' teaching, fellowship, the breaking of bread and prayer. They shared their goods with anyone in need. The rest of Acts tells how this small group in one city grew, in about thirty years, into churches in cities across the empire. The Spirit who came on that day is the one who gathers and empowers the church still.

# The gospel crosses every border
@ref ACT 8:4-8; ACT 10:34-48; ACT 11:19-26; ACT 15:6-11; GAL 3:28
@conf scholars

At first the followers of Jesus were all Jews, and many assumed they would stay a movement within Judaism. But persecution after the death of Stephen scattered them, and wherever they went they told the story. Philip preached to the Samaritans, who had been despised by Jews for centuries, and many believed. Then Peter went to the house of Cornelius, a Roman centurion, and saw the Holy Spirit fall on non-Jews as he spoke. "I now truly understand," Peter said, "that God does not show favoritism" (Acts 10:34).

In Antioch, the third largest city in the empire, believers began to speak to Greeks as well as Jews, and a large number turned to the Lord. It was there, Acts says, that "the disciples were first called Christians" (Acts 11:26). The church at Antioch sent out Barnabas and Saul, who is also called Paul, on a mission to the island of Cyprus and then to the interior of what is now Turkey. Paul made three major journeys over about a decade, planting churches in cities such as Philippi, Thessalonica, Corinth and Ephesus.

The great controversy was whether Gentile believers had to be circumcised and keep the law of Moses. About AD 49 the apostles and elders met in Jerusalem to decide. Peter argued that God "made no distinction between us and them, for He cleansed their hearts by faith," and that "we believe it is through the grace of the Lord Jesus that we are saved, just as they are" (Acts 15:9,11). James agreed, and the church sent a letter. Gentiles would be accepted by faith, without circumcision.

Paul later put the new situation in a sentence: in Christ there is "neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female" (Galatians 3:28). The promise to Abraham, that all the families of the earth would be blessed, was starting to come true.

# Letters to young churches
@ref ROM 1:1-7; 1CO 1:10; 2TI 3:14-17; 1PE 1:3-5
@conf scholars

Twenty-one of the twenty-seven books of the New Testament are letters. They were written in the first generation of the church, in most cases by apostles, to congregations or individuals facing particular problems, and they make up the oldest part of the New Testament. Among the earliest are James, Galatians and 1 Thessalonians, written in the late 40s and early 50s AD, before the Gospels as we have them were finished.

Thirteen letters bear the name of Paul. Some are addressed to churches (Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians, 1 and 2 Thessalonians), and four to individuals (1 and 2 Timothy, Titus, Philemon). Hebrews has no named author. The rest are called the general letters because they are addressed to wider circles: James, 1 and 2 Peter, 1, 2 and 3 John, and Jude.

Each letter has a particular occasion. Paul writes to Corinth because the church is divided and tolerating immorality. He writes to Galatia because teachers are insisting that Gentiles must keep the law. He writes to Philippi to thank them for their gift. Yet it is striking how much of the letters is about the same things: the person and work of Christ, the gospel, new life in the Spirit, love, holiness, hope, and the shape of the community. Peter writes to scattered believers who are suffering, reminding them that they have "a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead" (1 Peter 1:3).

Nearly all the doctrine in the next course of this library, on theology, rests on these letters. They were occasional documents, but the church recognized in them the voice of God to every generation. Paul tells Timothy that "All Scripture is God-breathed and is useful for instruction, for conviction, for correction, and for training in righteousness" (2 Timothy 3:16).

# The new heaven and the new earth
@ref REV 21:1-5; REV 22:1-5; ISA 65:17-25; ROM 8:18-23
@conf debated
@q What in the picture of Revelation 21 and 22 most clearly answers what went wrong in Genesis 3?

The Bible ends where it began, in a garden, but a garden that has grown into a city. The last two chapters of Revelation, written by John from the island of Patmos about AD 95 according to the most common view, describe the goal of the whole story. John sees "a new heaven and a new earth, for the first heaven and earth had passed away" (Revelation 21:1). The holy city, the new Jerusalem, comes down out of heaven from God, "prepared as a bride adorned for her husband."

Then he hears the central announcement: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man, and He will dwell with them. They will be His people, and God Himself will be with them as their God." The tabernacle, the temple and the incarnation have all led here, to God living with his people. "He will wipe away every tear from their eyes," and there will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain. The One on the throne says, "Behold, I make all things new."

Almost every feature reverses something from the early chapters of Genesis. The river of the water of life flows through the city, and on both sides stands the tree of life, whose leaves are for the healing of the nations. "No longer will there be any curse." There is no night. God's servants "will see His face," which no one could do after Eden, and they "will reign forever and ever" (Revelation 22:3-5).

Christians differ on how literally to read the images, and on the order of events leading up to this point, which the course on theology takes up. But there is wide agreement about the destination. The story does not end with souls floating off to a distant heaven. It ends with the renewal of creation and the presence of God among his people, and Paul can say that the sufferings of the present are not worth comparing with the glory that will be revealed (Romans 8:18).
