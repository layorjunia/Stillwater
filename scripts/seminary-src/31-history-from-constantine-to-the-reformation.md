# Constantine and a church out of hiding
@ref ROM 13:1-7; 1TI 2:1-4; ACT 5:29
@conf scholars

In AD 312 Constantine, a general fighting for control of the western half of the Roman Empire, marched on Rome to face his rival Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge. Christian writers of the time, including Eusebius and Lactantius, report that he had a vision or dream beforehand that told him to put the sign of Christ on his soldiers' shields. He won the battle. In the following year he and the eastern emperor Licinius agreed on a policy, usually called the Edict of Milan, that granted Christians and everyone else freedom to worship.

The change was enormous. Churches that had been meeting in houses began to build great basilicas. Property seized in the persecutions was returned. Constantine gave money to the clergy, exempted them from certain taxes and made Sunday a day of rest. In AD 330 he dedicated a new capital on the Bosporus, Constantinople, which was designed as a Christian city. He was not baptized until shortly before his death in AD 337, a delay not unusual at the time, and historians still argue about how deep his faith was.

The blessings were real, and so were the dangers. Once the emperor favored the church, the church had to deal with the temptation of power. Bishops became important public figures. People joined for advantage as well as conviction. And the emperor, who thought of himself as a kind of overseer of the church, took an interest in settling its quarrels, which led directly to the Council of Nicaea.

Later Christians have weighed this period very differently. Some see it as the triumph of the gospel, and others as the start of a long entanglement of church and state. Both can be true. After Constantine, Christianity was no longer a movement that might get you killed, and by the end of the century it was the empire's official religion.

# Nicaea and Athanasius
@ref JHN 1:1-3; COL 1:15-17; HEB 1:1-3
@conf scholars

In the early fourth century a priest in Alexandria named Arius taught that the Son of God was the first and greatest of God's creations, but still a creature. "There was a time when he was not," his followers said. Bishop Alexander of Alexandria disagreed, and the quarrel spread across the Greek-speaking church, until the emperor Constantine, who wanted unity, called the bishops together.

More than 300 bishops met in the city of Nicaea, in what is now Turkey, in AD 325. The council condemned Arius and wrote a creed that confessed Christ as "God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God, begotten, not made, of one substance with the Father." The key word was homoousios, "of one substance," which said that the Son shares the Father's very being and is not a lesser being made by him.

The fight did not end there. For decades the emperors and many bishops leaned toward compromise. The man who held the line was Athanasius, who was secretary to Alexander at Nicaea and who became bishop of Alexandria in AD 328. He was exiled five times by different emperors, and is remembered with the phrase "Athanasius against the world." His book On the Incarnation argues that only God could save humanity, and that if Christ were a creature, the salvation he brought would fail. Athanasius is also the first writer known to list exactly the twenty-seven books of our New Testament, in his Easter letter of AD 367.

At the Council of Constantinople in AD 381, the Nicene Creed was expanded, in the form most churches use today, to affirm the full deity of the Holy Spirit. Arianism lingered for a while among some Germanic tribes, but the church had found the language it needed.

# The Cappadocians and the Trinity
@ref MAT 28:19; 2CO 13:14; JHN 15:26
@conf scholars

Nicaea settled that the Son is fully God. But it left the question of how to speak of one God in three persons. The answer was worked out in the later fourth century by three friends from Cappadocia, in what is now central Turkey: Basil of Caesarea, his younger brother Gregory of Nyssa, and their friend Gregory of Nazianzus.

Their contribution was largely a matter of vocabulary. The word ousia, being or substance, would name what the three share. The word hypostasis, person or subsistence, would name what each is distinctively. So God is one ousia in three hypostases. The Father is not the Son, and the Son is not the Spirit, yet all three are fully the one God. The three differ not in power or goodness but in their relations: the Father is unbegotten, the Son is begotten from the Father, and the Spirit proceeds from the Father.

Basil also wrote On the Holy Spirit, defending the Spirit's full divinity, and used the church's own worship as evidence: the Spirit is glorified together with the Father and the Son. Gregory of Nazianzus, nicknamed "the Theologian," gave five great sermons on the Trinity, and Gregory of Nyssa wrote on how the soul grows toward God.

They also showed that theology is done on one's knees. Their works are full of prayer and wonder, and they insist that God is greater than any words can say. The result, in the creed of Constantinople in AD 381, was the formula the church still uses. And all three, Basil in particular, were also pastors who built hospitals and cared for the poor in a time of famine.

# The desert fathers and the monks
@ref MAT 19:21; LUK 5:15-16; PSA 119:97-104
@conf scholars

When the persecutions ended and the church became popular, some Christians felt that the cost of discipleship was being lost. They went out into the desert. The movement began in Egypt in the late third century, and its first great figure was Antony, born about AD 251, who sold his property after hearing Jesus' words to the rich young man and went to live alone in the wilderness. He lived there for about twenty years and was said to have fought with demons. People began to seek him out, and his life, written by Athanasius, spread the ideal across the Christian world.

Others tried a different pattern. Pachomius, a former soldier, founded around AD 320 the first communal monastery, where monks lived together under a common rule of prayer, work and obedience. Basil of Caesarea wrote rules for monastic life in the East, and in the West the greatest was written by Benedict of Nursia, who around AD 529 founded the monastery of Monte Cassino in Italy. His Rule is short, practical and balanced, organized around the daily round of worship and work, and it shaped monastic life in the West for a thousand years.

The monks left a deep legacy. They kept learning alive through the collapse of the western empire, copying manuscripts of the Bible and many classical authors. They opened hospitals and hospitality. They became missionaries. Their sayings, collected as the Sayings of the Desert Fathers, are short and sharp and still read today.

The movement had its excesses, such as extreme self-denial, and the Reformers later criticized the idea that monks had a higher spiritual calling than other believers. But in an age when many Christians were drifting toward comfort, the monks asked what it truly costs to follow Christ.

# Augustine
@ref ROM 13:13-14; PSA 42:1-2; ROM 5:12-21
@conf scholars

Augustine of Hippo is one of the most influential thinkers in the history of Christianity, in both the Catholic and Protestant traditions. Born in AD 354 in Thagaste, in North Africa, to a pagan father and a devoted Christian mother, Monica, he became a brilliant teacher of rhetoric. For years he pursued pleasure, ambition and the sect of the Manichees. He lived with a woman for about fifteen years and had a son with her.

His conversion is told in his Confessions, written around AD 397 to 400, the first great spiritual autobiography. In a garden in Milan in 386, he heard a child's voice say "Take up and read," and opened the Bible to Romans 13, which told him to put on the Lord Jesus Christ and give up the desires of the flesh. He was baptized by Ambrose the following Easter. The Confessions opens with a line that has been quoted ever since: "You have made us for yourself, O Lord, and our heart is restless until it rests in you."

He became bishop of Hippo in AD 395 and served until his death in 430, while the Vandals were besieging his city. His works are vast. In response to the sack of Rome by the Visigoths in 410, he wrote The City of God, arguing that earthly empires rise and fall, while the city of God endures. In the controversy with Pelagius, he insisted on the priority of God's grace in salvation, which led him to his teachings on original sin and predestination. His writings on the Trinity, on time and on the nature of the church shaped the Middle Ages.

Luther and Calvin both counted themselves as Augustine's followers, and so do many Catholics. He is a reminder that the greatest theology often comes out of a struggle, and that the one who writes about grace is the one who needed it most.

# The Bible in Latin: Jerome
@ref 2TI 4:13; PSA 119:105; ACT 8:30-31
@conf scholars

Jerome was born around AD 347 in what is now Croatia and educated in Rome. He was a brilliant and difficult man, an ascetic with a sharp tongue, who spent years in the Syrian desert and then in Rome as secretary to Pope Damasus. For the church of his time, his great work was the translation of the Bible into Latin.

Latin versions already existed, collectively called the Old Latin, but they were uneven and varied from place to place. In AD 382 Damasus asked Jerome to prepare a standard text. Jerome began with the Gospels, and later moved to Bethlehem, where he lived in a monastery for the last thirty-four years of his life. There he did something that no Christian scholar had done before. He learned Hebrew and translated the Old Testament directly from the Hebrew, rather than from the Greek Septuagint. He completed the work around AD 405.

His translation was eventually called the Vulgate, from the Latin for "common," and it was the main Bible of western Christendom for more than a thousand years. Many of the phrases that entered English came through it. The Council of Trent in 1546 declared it the authoritative Latin text for the Catholic Church.

Jerome made enemies, and he was criticized for translating from Hebrew and for calling some of the books of the Septuagint apocryphal. He also left a famous line that every Bible student should remember: "Ignorance of Scripture is ignorance of Christ." He is the patron saint of translators, and his work is a reminder that the Bible has always needed to be put into the language of the people who read it.

# Chalcedon and the churches of the East
@ref JHN 1:14; COL 2:9; HEB 2:14-17
@conf scholars

After Nicaea affirmed that Christ is God, the next question was how his divinity and humanity fit together. At the Council of Ephesus in AD 431, the church condemned Nestorius, the patriarch of Constantinople, whose teaching seemed to divide Christ into two persons, and affirmed that Mary can be called Theotokos, "God-bearer," not to exalt her but to protect the truth that her son was truly God.

Then came an opposite error, held by Eutyches, who said that after the union Christ had only one nature, a blend of divine and human. In AD 451 the emperor called a council in Chalcedon, a city across the strait from Constantinople. About 500 bishops attended. They accepted a letter from Pope Leo of Rome, called the Tome of Leo, and issued a definition of faith: Christ is one person in two natures, "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation."

Not everyone accepted it. Many Christians in Egypt, Syria and Armenia held that Chalcedon had gone too far in dividing the natures and followed the teaching of Cyril of Alexandria, "one incarnate nature of the Word." The churches that rejected the council, today called the Oriental Orthodox churches, include the Coptic, Syriac, Armenian and Ethiopian churches. In recent decades theologians in these churches and the Eastern Orthodox and Catholic churches have reached agreements that say the differences were largely a matter of words.

Alongside them stood another ancient church, called the Church of the East, which was condemned along with Nestorius and carried Christianity across Persia, India and Central Asia, and even to China by the seventh century. The map of the churches today still shows the lines drawn in the fifth century, which is a good reason to take them seriously.

# Missionaries to the north and west
@ref ACT 16:9-10; MAT 28:19-20; ROM 15:20-21
@conf scholars

As the western Roman Empire fell apart in the fifth century, Christianity moved into the lands beyond it. A series of missionaries carried the faith to peoples who had never been part of Rome.

Patrick, born in Roman Britain in the early fifth century, was kidnapped at sixteen by Irish raiders and held as a slave, herding sheep for six years. He escaped and returned home, but later had a dream in which the people of Ireland seemed to call him back. He went to Ireland as a bishop and spent the rest of his life preaching and founding churches. His Confession, written in rough Latin, is one of the few writings that survive from this period, and tells of his faith and his struggles.

Irish monasteries then sent their own missionaries. Columba founded a monastery on the island of Iona off Scotland in 563, which became a base for evangelizing the Picts and the north of England. Pope Gregory the Great, who led the church from 590 to 604, sent a monk named Augustine to England, who arrived in Kent in 597 and baptized King Ethelbert. Later Boniface, an English monk, went to Germany in the eighth century. According to his biographer he cut down an oak sacred to the god Thor in about 723, and when nothing happened, many were converted. He was killed by raiders in Frisia in 754.

The missionaries worked slowly, learning languages and winning the rulers. Their churches and monasteries became centers of learning, literacy and care for the poor. In this way, over some six or seven centuries, much of Europe became Christian. The pattern repeats through the history of the church: the faith spreads from the edges as much as from the center.

# The faith meets Islam
@ref 1PE 3:15; ACT 17:22-31; EPH 6:12
@conf scholars

In the seventh century a new faith appeared that changed the map of the Christian world. Muhammad, born in Mecca about AD 570, taught that there is one God and that he himself was the last of the prophets. After his death in 632, the Arab armies that followed him carried Islam outward with extraordinary speed. They took Damascus in 635 and Jerusalem in 637. Alexandria fell in 641, and by about 720 Muslim rule stretched from Spain to Persia.

Some of the oldest Christian lands were affected. The churches of Syria, Egypt and North Africa, which had produced Athanasius, Cyril and Augustine, came under Muslim rule. In most places Christians were allowed to keep their faith as a protected minority, with legal limits and an extra tax, and the churches survived, but they shrank over the centuries. Today the Coptic Church in Egypt still numbers in the millions.

Christians first met Islam as a challenge to explain themselves. John of Damascus, who served at the Muslim court in the early eighth century before becoming a monk, wrote the first Christian response, defending the Trinity and the deity of Christ. Later in the Middle Ages the contact included both warfare and scholarship, because Arabic translations of Greek philosophy came back to the Latin West through Spain and Sicily.

What later became a long history of conflict and exchange began as a question of faith. Muslims honor Jesus as a prophet but deny that he is the Son of God, that he died on the cross and that God is a Trinity, and these remain the points on which the two faiths part. Christians are asked by Peter to be ready to give a reason for their hope with gentleness and respect, and the long encounter has taught the church how hard that can be.

# The Great Schism of 1054
@ref EPH 4:3-6; JHN 17:20-23; 1CO 1:10
@conf scholars

The Christian church began as one, and the divide between East and West came slowly. The two halves of the old empire spoke different languages, Greek in the East and Latin in the West, and had grown apart in culture and politics. After the western empire fell, the Pope of Rome came to claim an authority over the whole church, while the patriarchs in the East saw him as the first among equals. Disputes accumulated over the centuries.

One concerned a clause in the Nicene Creed. In the West the phrase "and the Son," in Latin filioque, was added to the statement that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father, first in Spain in the sixth century. The East objected, both to the theology and to a change made to the creed without a council of the whole church. Other differences concerned the use of leavened or unleavened bread in the Lord's Supper, the marriage of priests and the pope's claim to universal power.

The break is dated to 1054, when a papal legate, Cardinal Humbert, laid a bull of excommunication on the altar of the Hagia Sophia in Constantinople against the patriarch Michael Cerularius, and the patriarch responded in kind. It was not seen as final at the time, and contact continued. But the Fourth Crusade, in 1204, when western soldiers sacked Constantinople, left a bitterness that has lasted. The mutual excommunications were formally lifted in 1965 by Pope Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras, but the churches are still not in full communion.

Today the Eastern Orthodox Church, with some 200 to 300 million members, and the Roman Catholic Church, with some 1.3 billion, remain separate. Jesus prayed that his followers might be one (John 17:21), and the division is still a wound.

# The Crusades
@ref MAT 5:43-45; ROM 12:17-21; JHN 18:36
@conf scholars

In 1095 Pope Urban II spoke at Clermont in France, calling on the knights of Europe to help the Byzantine emperor against the Seljuk Turks and to recover Jerusalem for Christendom. The response was enormous. The First Crusade took Jerusalem in July 1099, after a siege, and the army massacred many of its inhabitants, Muslims and Jews alike. The crusaders set up several states in the Levant, which lasted for about two centuries.

Many more crusades followed, and they went badly. The Second Crusade failed in 1148. Saladin recaptured Jerusalem in 1187, though he treated its people more mercifully than the crusaders had. The Fourth Crusade turned aside in 1204 to sack the Christian city of Constantinople. The so-called Children's Crusade of 1212 ended in disaster. The last crusader stronghold, Acre, fell in 1291. Crusading ideas were also used against heretics in Europe, such as the Cathars, and against pagans in the Baltic.

Historians still debate the motives. Many crusaders were sincere, and they believed they were doing penance and defending fellow Christians. Others were drawn by land, loot or adventure. The crusades lie at the center of Muslim memory of Christian aggression, and the Eastern church remembers 1204.

The church's own sources contain a challenge to the whole enterprise. Jesus told Pilate that his kingdom was not of this world, so that his servants would not fight (John 18:36), and told his disciples to love their enemies. Today most Christians regard the crusades as a grave failure to follow him. A story of faith should be honest about this part. The church has been the source of great good and also of great harm, and it is part of the Christian understanding of sin that this includes its own members.

# Faith seeking understanding: Anselm and Aquinas
@ref ROM 1:19-20; 1PE 3:15; PRO 2:1-6
@conf scholars

In the high Middle Ages the universities of Europe produced a new kind of theology, in which the church's teaching was explored with rigorous reasoning. It is called scholasticism, and two of its greatest minds were Anselm and Thomas Aquinas.

Anselm, born in Italy about 1033, became a monk in Normandy and later Archbishop of Canterbury. He described his method as "faith seeking understanding," beginning with belief and then asking why it is so. In his Proslogion he offered a famous argument for God's existence, now called the ontological argument, which says that God is "that than which nothing greater can be conceived," and that such a being must exist in reality, not only in thought. His Cur Deus Homo, "Why God Became Man," argued that the offense of sin against God's honor required satisfaction that only a God-man could make, and it shaped later views of the atonement.

Thomas Aquinas was born around 1225 into a noble family in Italy and joined the Dominicans against his family's wishes. He studied under Albert the Great in Paris and Cologne, and became the most influential theologian of the medieval church. His great work, the Summa Theologiae, written from 1265, treats the whole of Christian doctrine through thousands of carefully argued questions. He drew on Aristotle, whose works had just returned to the West, and held that faith and reason can work together, since both come from the same God.

In December 1273 he stopped writing, after what seems to have been some profound experience at Mass, and told a friend that everything he had written seemed to him like straw compared with what he had seen. He died in 1274 before finishing the Summa. In the Catholic Church he is called the Angelic Doctor. Protestants disagree with him on several points, but his clarity has been admired by almost everyone.

# Francis, Dominic and the friars
@ref MAT 10:5-10; LUK 9:1-6; 2CO 8:9

The thirteenth century saw a new kind of religious order. Instead of withdrawing into monasteries, the friars lived among the people, begged for their food and preached in the cities. Two men founded the most influential.

Francis was born about 1181 in Assisi, the son of a rich cloth merchant. As a young man he was a carefree party leader, and then a soldier who was captured and fell ill. While praying in the broken chapel of San Damiano, he heard a voice from the crucifix telling him to rebuild the church. He renounced his inheritance, in a famous scene in the square, and gave himself to poverty. Others joined him, and about 1209 or 1210 Pope Innocent III approved his simple rule. Francis loved all creation and wrote the Canticle of the Sun. In 1224 he is said to have received the stigmata, the marks of Christ's wounds, and he died in 1226.

Dominic, born in Spain about 1170, saw that heresy was spreading in southern France, and decided that the answer was better preaching, by educated men who lived simply. His Order of Preachers, approved in 1216, trained its members in theology and sent them to the universities. Aquinas was a Dominican.

The friars brought the gospel into the new towns, taught and cared for the poor. Their strengths became their weaknesses: Franciscans quarreled about how strict their poverty should be, and some of the orders later became wealthy. But the idea that the church should be among the poor, in simple trust, has returned again and again. Francis' prayer, often attributed to him although it comes from a later time, begins, "Lord, make me an instrument of your peace."

# Wycliffe, Hus and the voices before the Reformation
@ref 2TI 3:14-17; ACT 17:11; PSA 119:130
@conf scholars

By the fourteenth century many in the church felt that it needed reform. The popes had spent seventy years in Avignon in France, and then from 1378 there were two and sometimes three rival popes. The clergy were often wealthy and poorly educated, and the Bible was available only in Latin to most. In this setting, two men raised their voices.

John Wycliffe was an English scholar at Oxford, born around 1330, who argued that Scripture, and not the pope, is the supreme authority, and that every Christian should be able to read it. He attacked the sale of indulgences and some teachings about the Lord's Supper. He inspired the first complete translation of the Bible into English, made from the Latin Vulgate in the 1380s by him and his followers. His followers, the Lollards, were persecuted. He died in 1384 of natural causes, but in 1428 the church had his bones dug up and burned.

Jan Hus was a Czech priest and professor in Prague, who was influenced by Wycliffe's writings and preached in the language of the people. He was summoned to the Council of Constance in 1414 with a promise of safe conduct, and was arrested. He refused to recant what he believed to be true and was burned at the stake on 6 July 1415. His followers, the Hussites, fought for years, and some became the Moravian Church. According to one tradition, he said before his death that a goose, which is the meaning of "Hus" in Czech, would be roasted, but that a swan would come after him who could not be silenced. Many later saw Luther as the swan.

Wycliffe has been called the Morning Star of the Reformation. Both men believed that ordinary people needed the Bible in their own language, and that conviction would soon be carried by a new invention.

# The printing press and the open Bible
@ref PSA 119:105; 2TI 3:15; ISA 40:8
@conf scholars

Around 1455 a goldsmith in Mainz named Johannes Gutenberg finished printing a Bible in Latin using movable metal type. Before that, every book had to be copied by hand, which could take a monk a year or more to copy a Bible. Now hundreds of copies could be produced, and printing spread through Europe faster than almost any technology before or since. By 1500 there were presses in over 250 towns.

The Bible was the first great book to be printed, and the effect on the church was enormous. In 1516 the Dutch scholar Erasmus published the first printed edition of the Greek New Testament, with his own Latin translation beside it, which showed readers where the Vulgate differed from the Greek. Erasmus wrote in the preface that he wished that the Scriptures were translated into every language, so that the farmer at his plow and the weaver at his loom could read them. His Greek text was the basis of Luther's German translation and of the English New Testament of Tyndale, and eventually of the King James Version.

Ideas moved quickly now. Luther's writings were printed and sold in the thousands within weeks. Pamphlets, sermons, hymnbooks and catechisms flowed from the presses. The Reformation was the first great movement to be spread by the printed word.

The press made the Bible possible for ordinary readers. It also began a new problem that the church is still working through: when everyone can read, who decides what the text means? That question became the central argument of the next century.
