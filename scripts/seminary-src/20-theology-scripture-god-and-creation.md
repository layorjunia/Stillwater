# What theology is for
@ref PSA 119:105; 2TI 3:16-17; JHN 17:3
@q What is one belief about God that you hold only because someone told you, and what would it take to test it against Scripture?

Theology means, literally, words about God. It is the work of thinking carefully and honestly about who God is and what he has done, and it is as old as the Bible itself. Everyone has a theology. A person who says that God is whatever you want him to be is doing theology, and so is one who says there is no God at all. The question is not whether to have one but whether it is well founded.

Christians usually divide the subject into branches. Biblical theology follows the story and the themes of the Bible as they unfold. Systematic theology gathers what the Bible teaches on a topic, such as God or salvation, and arranges it into an orderly whole. Historical theology studies how the church has understood these things over the centuries. Practical theology asks how it should shape worship, preaching and daily life.

The sources are ranked. Scripture is the final authority for most Christians, and Protestants say it alone is the norm that judges all others. The church's tradition is a trusted guide to how Scripture has been read, and Catholic and Orthodox Christians give it more weight than Protestants do. Reason is needed to read well and to draw conclusions. Experience can confirm or challenge our reading but cannot overrule the text.

Theology is not meant to be mere information. Jesus says, "this is eternal life: that they may know You, the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom You have sent" (John 17:3). The aim is to know a person, and the study succeeds when it ends in worship and obedience. Paul says Scripture is given so that the believer may be "complete, fully equipped for every good work."

# How God makes himself known
@ref PSA 19:1-4; ROM 1:19-20; HEB 1:1-3
@conf debated

Theologians distinguish two ways in which God reveals himself. The first is general revelation, which is available to everyone, everywhere, through creation and the human conscience. The second is special revelation, which comes through particular acts and words, in the history of Israel, in the Scriptures and above all in Jesus Christ.

The case for general revelation is made in a famous psalm: "The heavens declare the glory of God; the skies proclaim the work of His hands. Day after day they pour forth speech" (Psalm 19:1-2). Paul adds that since the creation of the world God's "invisible qualities, His eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly seen, being understood from His workmanship, so that men are without excuse" (Romans 1:20). The sky does not give a map to salvation, but it tells every person that there is a powerful and glorious Maker.

General revelation is real but not enough. It leaves people accountable without telling them how to be forgiven, and our fallen minds distort what we see. That is why God also spoke in particular ways. Hebrews opens with the claim that "On many past occasions and in many different ways, God spoke to our fathers through the prophets, but in these last days He has spoken to us by His Son" (Hebrews 1:1-2). The Son is "the radiance of God's glory and the exact representation of His nature."

Christians differ about how much can be known of God from nature apart from Scripture. Some traditions, including Roman Catholicism, hold that human reason can reach real truths about God by itself, such as his existence. Many Reformed thinkers hold that, because of sin, nature's testimony is truly clear but is not rightly received without God's grace. All agree that the fullest and clearest self-disclosure of God is the person of Jesus.

# God-breathed: what the Bible says about itself
@ref 2TI 3:16-17; 2PE 1:20-21; MAT 5:17-18
@conf debated

The Bible claims to be more than a record of human religious ideas. Paul puts it in one memorable sentence: "All Scripture is God-breathed and is useful for instruction, for conviction, for correction, and for training in righteousness" (2 Timothy 3:16). The word translated God-breathed, in Greek theopneustos, is unique in the New Testament. It does not say that God breathed into the writers. It says the writings themselves are breathed out by him.

Peter says how it worked: "no such prophecy was ever brought forth by the will of man, but men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit" (2 Peter 1:21). The human authors were not secretaries taking dictation. They wrote in their own styles, from their own settings, using their own research (Luke 1:1-4) and memories. Yet the result is also God's word. Christians call this inspiration, and the shape of it is often compared to the person of Christ, who is fully human and fully divine.

Jesus treats the Old Testament as authoritative. He says that not a letter or stroke of the Law will pass away until everything is fulfilled (Matthew 5:18), he settles arguments by saying "it is written," and he says that Scripture cannot be set aside (John 10:35). The apostles treat each other's writings as Scripture too. Peter refers to Paul's letters among "the other Scriptures" (2 Peter 3:16).

Because it is inspired, the Bible is authoritative. It is the standard by which the church tests its teaching, its worship and its life. Christians differ on how to describe that authority in detail, and the next lesson looks at some of the words they use. But the basic claim is shared across the church: that when Scripture speaks, God speaks.

# Inerrant, infallible, sufficient
@ref PSA 12:6; JHN 17:17; PSA 19:7-9
@conf debated

Christians use several words to describe the quality of the Bible, and they do not all mean the same thing. Understanding the words helps in understanding the arguments.

Infallible means that Scripture will not fail or mislead us in what it teaches. Inerrant means that it is without error in all it affirms. Many Christians use the two words nearly interchangeably. Others prefer infallible, because inerrant seems to them to invite a demand for scientific precision that the text itself does not make. A group of evangelical scholars produced the Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy in 1978, which says that Scripture is free from error in all its teaching, including matters of history and science when it speaks on them, while interpreting each passage according to its kind. Not every Christian tradition uses these words, but all the large traditions say that the Bible is trustworthy.

The question of what a passage actually affirms matters. Jesus' parables are not reports of events. Poetry uses imagery, and ancient writers described numbers and dates in conventions different from ours. The test is to ask what the author meant to say, and that requires attention to genre, which a later lesson takes up.

Two other claims are made. The first is clarity: the central message of the Bible is plain enough that an ordinary reader can understand how to be saved, even though some passages are hard (2 Peter 3:16). The second is sufficiency. Protestants hold that Scripture contains everything necessary for salvation and for the life of faith, and the psalm says that "The law of the LORD is perfect, reviving the soul" (Psalm 19:7). Catholic and Orthodox Christians agree that Scripture is the supreme written authority but also hold that it was handed down within a church whose living tradition helps to interpret it.

# One God, and no other
@ref DEU 6:4-5; ISA 45:5-6; 1CO 8:4-6
@q What difference does it make to your day to say that there is only one God?

The first and greatest claim of the Bible about God is that there is only one. It was not self-evident in the ancient world. Egypt, Babylon and Canaan all had many gods: gods of the sun, the storm, the harvest and the sea. Israel alone, among its neighbors, confessed that the LORD is the only God, the creator of everything, and that there is none besides.

The great declaration is the Shema, spoken by Moses: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is One" (Deuteronomy 6:4). Isaiah gives the same message in God's own voice: "I am the LORD, and there is no other; there is no God but Me" (Isaiah 45:5). Paul repeats it to the church in Corinth: "there is no God but one" (1 Corinthians 8:4). The Bible mocks idols, because they are made of wood and metal by people who need them.

This claim, called monotheism, has several consequences. If there is one God, then all of reality comes from one mind and one will, and there is a unity to truth. If there is one God, then there is one moral standard, and it is not a matter of what a tribe prefers. If there is one God, then he is the God of everyone, and no nation can claim him as its private possession. And if there is one God, then he alone deserves worship, and every other loyalty must be below it.

The New Testament holds this together with a remarkable additional claim: that this one God is Father, Son and Holy Spirit. The doctrine of the Trinity, which a coming lesson takes up, does not abandon monotheism. It is the church's attempt to say who the one God has shown himself to be. Paul, a lifelong monotheist, can write that "for us there is but one God, the Father, from whom all things came and for whom we exist. And there is but one Lord, Jesus Christ."

# What God is like
@ref EXO 34:6-7; PSA 139:7-10; JER 10:10
@conf scholars

Theologians sort the qualities of God into two groups, and the sorting is helpful even though it is a human tool. The first group are the incommunicable attributes, those that belong to God alone and that no creature shares. The second are the communicable attributes, which God has in perfect form and which we can reflect in limited ways, since we are made in his image.

Among the first group are these.
- Independence, or aseity: God needs nothing and depends on nothing. He is the source of everything else.
- Eternity: God has no beginning or end and is not bound by time as we are.
- Immutability: God does not change in his character, his purposes or his promises.
- Omnipresence: God is present everywhere. "Where can I go to escape Your Spirit?" asks the psalmist (Psalm 139:7).
- Omniscience: God knows everything, past, present and future.
- Omnipotence: God can do all that is consistent with his nature, which means that he cannot lie or deny himself.

The communicable attributes include holiness, love, goodness, justice, mercy, wisdom, faithfulness and patience. A few verses carry many of them at once. When God passed in front of Moses on Sinai, he said that he is "compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in loving devotion and faithfulness" (Exodus 34:6). The prophet Jeremiah says that the LORD is "the true God; He is the living God and eternal King" (Jeremiah 10:10).

None of these is a separate piece. God is not partly love and partly justice. Each attribute describes the whole of God from a different angle, and each is perfectly harmonious with the others. The Bible does not give us a list of properties. It shows us a person, in his dealings with creation and with his people, and the doctrine of God is just that person's biography put into careful words.

# Holy, holy, holy
@ref ISA 6:1-5; REV 4:8; 1PE 1:15-16
@q When did you last feel something close to awe before God, and what produced it?

The one attribute the Bible repeats three times for emphasis is holiness. In Isaiah's vision of the temple, the seraphim cry to one another, "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of Hosts; all the earth is full of His glory" (Isaiah 6:3). In Revelation the four living creatures say the same, day and night without rest. No other quality of God is raised to the third power in Scripture.

The root idea of holiness is separateness. God is set apart from creation, not in the sense of being far away, but in the sense of being utterly different. He is not simply the biggest and best of beings. He is of another order. Holiness also has a moral side. God is perfectly pure, without any trace of evil, and therefore he cannot approve of sin. These two meanings are joined: because God is wholly other, he is wholly good, and he is the standard for all goodness.

The right response is awe. When Isaiah sees the Lord, he does not say how wonderful it is. He says, "Woe is me, for I am ruined, because I am a man of unclean lips" (Isaiah 6:5). The encounter with holiness reveals sin, and in the passage a seraph then touches his mouth with a coal from the altar and says his guilt is taken away. The same pattern runs through the Bible: holiness exposes, and mercy cleanses.

God does not keep his holiness to himself. He calls his people to share in it. Peter quotes from Leviticus: "just as He who called you is holy, so be holy in all you do" (1 Peter 1:15). That is why the tabernacle and temple had boundaries and rules, why sin is serious, and why the cross was needed. Holiness is the reason the gospel is good news.

# God is love, and God is just
@ref 1JN 4:8-10; EXO 34:6-7; ROM 3:23-26
@conf scholars

Two statements in the Bible seem to some readers to pull in opposite directions. John says, "God is love" (1 John 4:8). And the same God, in the passage where he describes himself to Moses, says that he forgives iniquity, transgression and sin, "yet He will by no means leave the guilty unpunished" (Exodus 34:7). How can both be true?

The answer begins with seeing that love, in the Bible, is not mere sentiment. It is a firm commitment to the good of another, and it takes sin seriously because sin damages the one loved. A parent who did not care how a child was treated would not be loving. A judge who let a violent person go free would not be kind, but would be failing the victims. God's justice is part of his love. It is his love for the world, set against whatever destroys it.

The great question then is how God can forgive sinners and still be just. Paul says that the cross is where the two meet. God presented Jesus "as an atoning sacrifice in His blood through faith, in order to demonstrate His righteousness," so as "to be just and to justify the one who has faith in Jesus" (Romans 3:25-26). Justice is satisfied, and mercy is offered, in one act.

The love of God is shown in the Bible at its fullest in the gift of the Son: "This is how God’s love was revealed among us: God sent His one and only Son into the world, so that we might live through Him" (1 John 4:9). Love does not mean that God overlooks sin. It means that he paid the price himself, and an honest reading of Scripture will not drop either truth in favor of the other.

# Sovereignty and human responsibility
@ref ACT 2:23; GEN 50:20; PHP 2:12-13; PRO 16:9
@conf debated
@q Where do you most struggle to hold both God's rule and your own responsibility?

The Bible says that God is in control of all things, and it says that people are responsible for what they choose. Both claims run through Scripture, often in the same passage. Peter says on the day of Pentecost that Jesus was handed over "by God’s set plan and foreknowledge," and that the people who did it acted by lawless hands (Acts 2:23). Joseph tells his brothers that although they meant evil, God meant it for good (Genesis 50:20). The proverb says, "A man’s heart plans his course, but the LORD determines his steps" (Proverbs 16:9).

Paul puts the two together in a single sentence: "continue to work out your salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God who works in you to will and to act on behalf of His good purpose" (Philippians 2:12-13). He does not say that one is true and the other is only apparent. He says both, and he draws a practical conclusion from the pairing, which is humility and effort at once.

Christians have tried to explain how they fit together, and they have given different answers. The Reformed tradition, following Augustine and Calvin, stresses that God's rule is exhaustive and that his purposes cannot be thwarted, and it argues that human freedom is real but is exercised within God's decree. The Arminian and Wesleyan traditions stress that God has chosen to give people genuine freedom to respond, and that his foreknowledge does not remove it. Catholic and Orthodox traditions have their own versions of the question and emphasize both grace and cooperation.

All of them agree on some things. God is not the author of sin. People are not puppets. God's purposes will be accomplished. And the right response to God's rule is trust, and the right response to human duty is obedience. Most Christians have found that the mystery is easier to live in than to resolve.

# One God in three persons
@ref MAT 28:19; 2CO 13:14; JHN 1:1-3; JHN 14:16-17
@conf scholars

The Trinity is the central and most distinctive teaching of Christianity about God. It says that there is one God, who eternally exists as three persons: the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. The three are not three gods, and they are not three roles or masks that one person puts on. They are three who are distinct and yet share one divine nature.

The word does not appear in the Bible. It was used by Tertullian in North Africa around AD 200, and the doctrine was worked out because the Bible's data demanded it. The evidence has several parts. First, the Old and New Testaments insist that God is one. Second, the New Testament speaks of the Father as God, the Son as God, and the Holy Spirit as God. Third, the three appear together, as when Jesus tells his disciples to baptize "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19), and when Paul closes a letter with a blessing from all three (2 Corinthians 13:14). Fourth, the three are distinguished from one another. The Son prays to the Father, the Father sends the Spirit, and at Jesus' baptism all three are present at once.

The church worked it out in the fourth century. At the Council of Nicaea in AD 325 it declared that the Son is "of one substance" with the Father, against Arius, who held that the Son was a created being. The Council of Constantinople in AD 381 affirmed the full deity of the Spirit and completed the Nicene Creed. The formula that emerged was one being, three persons.

No analogy is adequate. Water, ice and steam fail because they are one substance in three states, and a person who is father, son and husband fails because it is one person in three roles. The doctrine is held as a mystery that is revealed rather than explained. It also matters for what it says about God's character: before creation, God was already a communion of love, and love was never something he had to learn.

# The Father
@ref MAT 6:9; ROM 8:15-17; EPH 1:3-6; JHN 14:8-9

When Jesus taught his disciples to pray, he began with a word that startled his hearers: "Our Father in heaven." The Old Testament uses the image of father for God a handful of times. In the Gospels it is the name Jesus uses almost every time he speaks of God, and in Aramaic he addressed God as Abba, the word a child used for a father at home.

The Father is the first person of the Trinity, and the Bible associates him with certain acts in particular. He is the source: "from Him and through Him and to Him are all things." He is the planner, and the plan of salvation begins in his choice: "He chose us in Him before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless in His presence. In love He predestined us for adoption as His sons through Jesus Christ" (Ephesians 1:4-5). He sends the Son, and he gives the Spirit. Everything flows from the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit, and it returns to the Father in praise.

The title Father tells us what God is like in relation to his people. A father provides, protects, disciplines and delights in his children. Paul says that those who belong to Christ received "the Spirit of adoption to sonship, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!'" (Romans 8:15). The same Spirit testifies that we are God's children.

Christians have also stressed that God the Father is not male in the way humans are. The Bible uses images of motherhood for God occasionally (Isaiah 66:13), and it denies that God has a body (John 4:24). The name Father is the one that Jesus gave, and so the church has kept it. Jesus also said that anyone who has seen him has seen the Father (John 14:9), so what we learn about the Father, we learn by looking at the Son.

# The deity of the Son
@ref JHN 1:1-3; JHN 1:14; COL 1:15-17; PHP 2:5-8
@conf scholars

The most important question the New Testament asks is "Who is Jesus?" And its answer is bold: he is God the Son, who became a human being. The claim runs through the whole New Testament, and it was not invented by later councils.

John opens his Gospel with the plainest statement: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (John 1:1). Then he says that "The Word became flesh and made His dwelling among us" (John 1:14). Paul writes that the Son "is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn over all creation. For in Him all things were created." Everything was made through him, and "in Him all things hold together" (Colossians 1:15-17). The Gospels also say Jesus forgave sins, which only God can do, accepted worship, and said "before Abraham was born, I am" (John 8:58), taking the divine name from Exodus 3.

In a famous hymn Paul writes that Christ Jesus, "existing in the form of God, did not consider equality with God something to be grasped, but emptied Himself, taking the form of a servant" (Philippians 2:6-7). The Greek verb for "emptied" does not mean that he gave up being God. It means that he took the lowest possible position while remaining who he was.

Early in church history some taught that Jesus was a created being, or that he was a man adopted by God, or that he only seemed to be human. The Council of Nicaea in AD 325 rejected the view of Arius, who said "there was a time when he was not," and confessed that the Son is "God from God, light from light, true God from true God." The Christian claim is not that Jesus was an unusually good man or a great teacher. It is that the one who made the world walked in it.

# The Holy Spirit
@ref JHN 14:16-17; JHN 16:7-13; ACT 5:3-4; ROM 8:26-27

The Holy Spirit is the third person of the Trinity, and the one that is hardest to picture. The Bible uses images such as wind, breath, fire, water and a dove, and these tell us about what the Spirit does, but not about who he is. It is important to see that the Bible speaks of the Spirit as a person and not just as a force or influence.

The evidence is in how he is described. He teaches, speaks, testifies, guides, intercedes, and can be grieved or lied to (Ephesians 4:30; Acts 5:3). Jesus calls him "another Advocate," a word meaning someone who comes alongside, and says he will be with the disciples forever: "the Spirit of truth. The world cannot receive Him, because it neither sees Him nor knows Him. But you do know Him, for He abides with you and will be in you" (John 14:16-17). Peter tells Ananias that lying to the Holy Spirit is lying to God (Acts 5:3-4), which places the Spirit with God.

The Spirit has been active since the first verses of Genesis, where he hovers over the waters. In the Old Testament he empowers leaders, artists and prophets. After Pentecost, he comes to live in all believers. His work includes convicting the world of sin, giving new birth, opening the Scriptures, producing the fruit of love, joy and peace, giving gifts for service, and assuring believers that they are God's children. "the Spirit Himself intercedes for us with groans too deep for words" when we do not know how to pray (Romans 8:26).

Jesus says that the Spirit "will not speak on His own," but will take what is Christ's and declare it to us (John 16:13-14). So the Spirit's work always points to the Son. A Christian is someone in whom the Spirit has begun to make Christ real, and the whole of life, from first faith to final resurrection, is lived in his power.

# Creation out of nothing
@ref GEN 1:1; PSA 33:6-9; HEB 11:3; COL 1:16
@conf debated

The Bible begins with the statement that God created the heavens and the earth, and it never wavers from it. Christians have drawn from the text a doctrine called creation out of nothing, or in Latin creatio ex nihilo. It says that God did not make the world out of pre-existing matter, and that nothing existed alongside him. He called everything into being.

The key verses are brief. "By the word of the LORD the heavens were made, and all the stars by the breath of His mouth," says the psalm. "For He spoke, and it came to be; He commanded, and it stood firm" (Psalm 33:6,9). The writer of Hebrews says that "the universe was formed at God's command, so that what is seen was not made out of what was visible" (Hebrews 11:3). Paul says all things in heaven and on earth were created through Christ and for him.

The doctrine matters for several reasons. It says that God is distinct from his creation. The world is not a part of God, and it is not a rival to him. It says that matter is good, since God made it from nothing and called it good. It also means the world depends on God for its existence at every moment, not just at the beginning.

Christians differ over how to read the details of Genesis 1 and the age of the earth. Young earth creationists read the six days as ordinary days a few thousand years ago. Old earth creationists accept the scientific dating of the universe at some 13.8 billion years and read the days as ages or as a literary framework. Some accept the evidence for biological evolution and see it as the method God used, while others reject it. These are serious debates, but they are debates among people who agree that God made all there is, and that it is good.

# Providence: God holds everything
@ref ACT 17:24-28; PSA 104:10-15; MAT 6:25-30; ROM 8:28
@conf debated

If creation is where the world starts, providence is how God keeps it going. The word, from the Latin for "foresight," names God's continuing care for and rule over everything he has made. The Bible does not picture a God who wound up the universe like a clock and then walked away.

Paul tells the Athenians that God "gives everyone life and breath and everything else" and that "in Him we live and move and have our being" (Acts 17:25,28). Jesus says that God feeds the birds and clothes the lilies and that not one sparrow falls without the Father's knowledge. Hebrews says that Christ sustains "all things by His powerful word" (Hebrews 1:3). The psalm sees the rain, the grass, the wild animals and the harvest as all given by God's hand.

Theologians usually describe three parts to providence. Preservation means that God sustains the existence of creation. Concurrence means that God works through the actions of creatures, so that events have both natural and divine causes. Governance means that God directs everything toward his purposes. Together they mean that there is no corner of life outside God's care.

Providence is most comforting in trouble. "We know that God works all things together for the good of those who love Him, who are called according to His purpose" (Romans 8:28). That does not mean that every event is good. It means that God is able to bring good out of it, as he did with Joseph in Egypt and with the cross.

Christians differ on the details, such as how God's providence relates to human freedom and to miracles, and on the hard question of evil. Some hold that God ordains everything, and others that he permits much that he does not will. But the heart of the doctrine is shared: the world has not been abandoned, and the one who made it looks after it.

# Angels, demons and the unseen world
@ref HEB 1:14; PSA 91:11-12; 1PE 5:8-9; COL 1:15-17
@conf debated

The Bible takes for granted that the visible world is not all there is. It speaks of a great number of spiritual beings, called angels, and also of a rebellion among them. The teaching is smaller and more modest than much popular imagination, but it is real, and it matters for how Christians think about spiritual life.

Angels are created beings. They are not divine, and they are not former humans. Paul says that thrones, dominions, rulers and authorities were all created through Christ and for him (Colossians 1:16). Hebrews describes them as "ministering spirits sent to serve those who will inherit salvation" (Hebrews 1:14). They worship God (Isaiah 6), carry messages (Luke 1), protect (Psalm 91:11) and carry out his judgments. Names are rare. Only Michael and Gabriel are named in the sixty-six books. When people try to worship an angel in Scripture, the angel stops them (Revelation 22:8-9).

The Bible also speaks of Satan, the adversary, and of demons. Most Christians have read Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28 as describing the fall of a created being through pride, though many scholars say that these chapters are first about earthly kings. The New Testament says that the devil "has been sinning from the very start," and that Jesus came "to destroy the works of the devil" (1 John 3:8). He is powerful but limited. He is a liar and a tempter, a "roaring lion" looking for someone to devour, but believers are told to resist him and he will flee (1 Peter 5:8; James 4:7).

Christians differ about how far to speak of demonic activity in daily life, and about whether each person has a guardian angel. The sober center is that angels serve God and his people, that the devil is a defeated enemy, and that Christ has triumphed over every power by his cross (Colossians 2:15).
