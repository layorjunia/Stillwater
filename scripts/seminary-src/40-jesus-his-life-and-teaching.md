# Four portraits of one life
@ref LUK 1:1-4; JHN 20:30-31; MRK 1:1
@conf scholars
@q Which of the four Gospels have you read the least, and what do you expect to find in it?

The New Testament opens with four accounts of Jesus. The word gospel means "good news," and each of the four tells the same story of a ministry, a death and a resurrection, from a distinct angle. They are not four rival versions, and they are not carbon copies. Think of four portraits of one face, painted by four artists who each noticed different things.

Matthew, Mark and Luke are called the Synoptic Gospels, from a Greek word meaning "seen together," because they can be laid side by side and they share much of their material, often in the same order and wording. Most scholars think Mark was written first, and that Matthew and Luke each used it, along with a collection of sayings and material of their own. John is different. It has its own events, such as the wedding at Cana and the raising of Lazarus, and long conversations about who Jesus is.

Each has a flavor. Matthew writes for readers who knew the Hebrew Scriptures, and shows Jesus as the Messiah and the new Moses. Mark, short and fast, shows the Son of God who serves and suffers. Luke, a careful researcher, shows Jesus as the Savior of outsiders and the poor. John, who gives his purpose in plain words, writes "so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God" (John 20:31).

Scholars call the Gospels a kind of ancient biography. They were not written as neutral reports. Their authors wanted readers to believe, and the church has always held that this makes them more trustworthy and not less, since the people who knew Jesus best were the ones convinced that the world should know him. Reading all four gives a fuller picture than any one of them.

# The birth stories
@ref MAT 1:18-25; LUK 2:1-20; MAT 2:1-12
@conf scholars

Only Matthew and Luke tell of Jesus' birth, and they tell it differently, with scenes that fit together without duplicating each other. Matthew tells the story from Joseph's side. Joseph, a righteous man, finds that his fiancée Mary is pregnant and decides to divorce her quietly, until an angel tells him in a dream that "the One conceived in her is from the Holy Spirit" and that he is to name him Jesus, "because He will save His people from their sins" (Matthew 1:20-21). Matthew then describes the visit of wise men from the east, who follow a star to the house in Bethlehem, and the flight of the family to Egypt to escape Herod.

Luke tells it from Mary's side. The angel Gabriel appears to Mary in Nazareth, and she answers with her song, the Magnificat. Joseph and Mary travel to Bethlehem for a Roman census, and the baby is born and laid in a manger, "because there was no room for them in the inn." Shepherds in the fields hear angels announce the news, and they go to see.

Two points get attention from scholars. One is the census, which Luke says took place "while Quirinius was governor of Syria." Roman records show a census under Quirinius in AD 6, which is later than Herod's death in 4 BC, and historians have proposed several ways to harmonize the data. The other is the date of Christmas. The Gospels do not give a day, and December 25 is first mentioned in a Roman calendar of about AD 336. Scholars debate how it was chosen.

What both writers insist on, with the church's creeds, is that Jesus was born of a virgin, by the work of the Spirit. They tell it quietly, in the setting of an ordinary village and an occupied land, and they place the birth in the line of Abraham and David.

# The baptism and the wilderness
@ref MRK 1:9-13; MAT 4:1-11; DEU 8:3
@conf scholars

When Jesus was about thirty, he came to the Jordan River, where John the Baptist was calling people to repent, and asked to be baptized. John at first objected, since he knew Jesus had no sin to confess. But Jesus went down into the water, and as he came up, "He saw the heavens breaking open and the Spirit descending on Him like a dove. And a voice came from heaven: 'You are My beloved Son; in You I am well pleased'" (Mark 1:10-11). It is one of the few scenes where all three persons of the Trinity are present together.

The next thing was a test. "Then Jesus was led by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil" (Matthew 4:1). For forty days he fasted. Israel had spent forty years in the wilderness and failed, and Jesus now relives their story and succeeds. The devil then made three attempts. He told Jesus to turn stones into bread, and Jesus answered from Deuteronomy: "Man shall not live on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of God." He took Jesus to the pinnacle of the temple and told him to throw himself down, quoting a psalm, and Jesus answered that it is written not to put the Lord God to the test. He offered him the kingdoms of the world in exchange for worship, and Jesus answered that it is written to worship the Lord alone.

Each temptation was a shortcut: provide comfort, display power, or take the kingdom without the cross. Jesus rejected each by trusting what God had said. Hebrews says that he "was tempted in every way that we are, yet was without sin."

Then, "the devil left Him, and angels came and ministered to Him." Jesus returned to Galilee in the power of the Spirit, and his public ministry began.

# The Twelve
@ref MRK 3:13-19; MAT 10:1-4; LUK 6:12-16

After a night of prayer on a mountain, Jesus called his followers together and chose twelve of them, whom he named apostles, which means "sent ones." He appointed them "to accompany Him, to be sent out to preach." The number is not an accident. It echoes the twelve sons of Jacob and the twelve tribes of Israel, as if to say that a renewed people was being gathered around him.

The names appear in four lists, with small differences. They are Simon, whom Jesus named Peter, and his brother Andrew, both fishermen; James and John, the sons of Zebedee, also fishermen, whom he called "sons of thunder"; Philip; Bartholomew, probably the same as Nathanael; Matthew, the tax collector; Thomas; James son of Alphaeus; Thaddaeus, who is also called Judas son of James; Simon the Zealot; and Judas Iscariot, who betrayed him.

They were not a promising group. They were mostly working men from Galilee, with no religious training and little standing. They included one who worked for the hated Roman tax system, and another who had belonged to a nationalist party that opposed it. They misunderstood Jesus again and again, argued over who was the greatest, fell asleep in Gethsemane, ran away at his arrest, and one of them denied him three times.

Yet these were the men to whom Jesus entrusted the message. After the resurrection and the coming of the Spirit, they led the church, and their testimony is the foundation of the New Testament. Paul says the church is "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus Himself as the cornerstone" (Ephesians 2:20). The choice shows how God likes to work, which is to use ordinary people who have been with Jesus.

# The Beatitudes
@ref MAT 5:1-12; LUK 6:20-26; PSA 1:1-3
@conf scholars

The Sermon on the Mount, in Matthew 5 to 7, is the longest block of Jesus' teaching in the Gospels, and it begins with eight short blessings, called the Beatitudes from the Latin word for blessed. They sound like nothing a crowd would expect to hear.

"Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted. Blessed are the meek, for they will inherit the earth. Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they will be filled. Blessed are the merciful, for they will be shown mercy. Blessed are the pure in heart, for they will see God. Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called sons of God" (Matthew 5:3-9). Then comes a final blessing for those who are persecuted for righteousness.

The Greek word for blessed, makarios, describes a deep happiness that comes from being right with God, and it does not depend on circumstances. Every beatitude turns the world's list upside down. The world admires the strong, the successful and the secure. Jesus says that the kingdom belongs to those who know they are empty, who grieve, who are gentle, who long for what is right, who show mercy, who are sincere, who make peace.

Each blessing has a promise that matches the condition: the kingdom, comfort, the earth, satisfaction, mercy, the vision of God, and the name of God's children. These promises are partly present now and partly future.

Luke has a shorter version with four blessings and four matching woes, addressed to the poor and the rich, the hungry and the full. Taken together they show that the kingdom reverses human values. The Beatitudes are not rules to be met in order to enter the kingdom. They describe the character of those who already belong to it.

# The Sermon on the Mount: how to read the hard parts
@ref MAT 5:17-20; MAT 5:21-48; MAT 7:24-27
@conf debated
@q Which of the demands of the Sermon on the Mount do you most want to explain away, and what would it mean to take it at face value?

After the Beatitudes, Jesus says that he has not come to abolish the Law and the Prophets but to fulfill them. Then he gives six examples, each introduced with the formula "You have heard that it was said," followed by "But I tell you." They are about murder, adultery, divorce, oaths, retaliation and love for enemies, and in each he goes to the root of the commandment.

On murder, he says that "anyone who is angry with his brother will be subject to judgment." On adultery, that lust is already a breach of the heart. On oaths, that a person's simple word should be enough. On retaliation, that rather than demanding an eye for an eye, his followers should turn the other cheek. And on enemies: "love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you, that you may be sons of your Father in heaven" (Matthew 5:44-45).

These are demanding, and Christians have read them in different ways. Some take them as a new law to be followed literally, as the Anabaptist tradition and many pacifists do. Others hold that they are meant to show what perfect righteousness looks like and drive people to depend on grace, as Luther did. Others say they describe the ethics of the coming kingdom, which are partly realized now. Still others stress that they are aimed at the attitudes of the heart and not at specific actions, and require wisdom in application. Whatever the reading, no one can come away thinking that Jesus' standard is low.

The sermon ends with a story of two builders. The wise one built on rock and the foolish on sand, and the storm came to both. The difference was that the one "hears these words of Mine and acts on them," and the other did not. The sermon is meant to be done, not just admired.

# The Lord's Prayer
@ref MAT 6:5-15; LUK 11:1-4; ROM 8:15
@q Which of the petitions of the Lord's Prayer do you pray most easily, and which most rarely?

One of the disciples asked Jesus, "Lord, teach us to pray," and he gave them a model. Matthew's version, in the Sermon on the Mount, is the one the church has used most: "Our Father in heaven, hallowed be Your name. Your kingdom come, Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread. And forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors. And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one" (Matthew 6:9-13).

It has six petitions, in two groups. The first three are about God. Hallowed be your name means that God's name should be honored as holy. Your kingdom come asks for his reign to be seen. Your will be done asks that earth be like heaven. The last three are about us. Daily bread asks for what we need for today, which was a request of daily dependence. Forgive us our debts brings the need for forgiveness, and ties it to our forgiving others. And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one asks for protection.

The prayer begins with "Our Father," which Jesus said to his disciples as a startling new intimacy, and it is in the plural: "Our." Jesus warned against empty repetition and showy prayer, and said that the Father knows what we need before we ask.

The familiar ending, for Yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory forever, is not in the oldest manuscripts of Matthew and was probably added in worship, which is why some Bibles leave it out and others print it. Luke's shorter form begins simply with "Father." The prayer has been recited by Christians every day for two thousand years. It is a pattern more than a formula: what to ask for and in what order.

# Why Jesus told stories
@ref MRK 4:1-12; MAT 13:10-17; PSA 78:1-2
@conf scholars

About a third of Jesus' recorded teaching in the first three Gospels is in the form of parables, short stories drawn from ordinary life. The word comes from the Greek for "placing alongside." The stories are about farmers, fishermen, wedding feasts, lost coins, unjust judges and fathers with two sons, and they are memorable because they are concrete.

Jesus used them for more than one reason. A good story gets past defenses. When the prophet Nathan told David the story of a poor man's lamb, David was angered by the injustice before he realized he was the man, and Jesus' parables often work in a similar way. They invite hearers to take a side and then to see themselves in the picture. They also make the truth easy to remember.

But they also did something else. When his disciples asked why he spoke in parables, Jesus answered that the knowledge of the secrets of the kingdom had been given to them, "but to those on the outside everything is expressed in parables," quoting Isaiah on those who see and do not perceive (Mark 4:11-12). The parables separate those who really want to understand from those who do not. They have to be asked about, thought about and lived with.

Most parables have one main point, and it is easy to go wrong by allegorizing every detail. The sower, the treasure, the lost sheep and the unforgiving servant each make a primary claim about the kingdom or about God. The context helps: Jesus told the Good Samaritan to answer a lawyer's question, and the lost sheep, coin and son to answer grumbling about the company he kept. A parable is a window, not a code.

# The sower and the four soils
@ref MRK 4:1-20; MAT 13:1-23; LUK 8:4-15

The parable of the sower is the first of Jesus' kingdom parables in Matthew, Mark and Luke, and Mark says that if you do not understand it, you will not understand any of the parables. A farmer goes out to sow. The seed falls in four kinds of ground, and each ends differently.

Some seed fell along the path, and the birds came and ate it. Some fell on rocky ground where there was not much soil, and it sprang up quickly but withered in the sun because it had no root. Some fell among thorns, which grew up and choked it. And some fell on good soil, where it grew and produced a crop, "thirtyfold, sixtyfold, or a hundredfold" (Mark 4:20).

Jesus explains it privately to his disciples. The seed is the word of God. The path is the hearer who never takes it in, because the evil one snatches it away. The rocky ground is the person who receives it with joy, but has no root, and falls away when trouble comes. The thorns are the worries of life, the deceit of wealth and other desires that choke the word. The good soil is the one who hears the word, receives it and bears fruit.

Several things are worth noticing. The sower sows generously, even into ground that looks unpromising, as God does. The seed is the same each time, and the difference is in the soil, which is the response of the heart. Three of the four fail, which is realistic about how people respond, and a warning for any hearer. And the story ends with a harvest greater than anyone had a right to expect. The parable challenges its hearers to ask what kind of soil they are, and encourages those who sow the word that some of it will take root.

# The Good Samaritan
@ref LUK 10:25-37; LEV 19:18; 2KI 17:24-41

A lawyer stands up to test Jesus and asks what he must do to inherit eternal life. Jesus asks him what the Law says, and he answers with love for God and love for neighbor. Then, "wanting to justify himself," he asks, "And who is my neighbor?" (Luke 10:29). Jesus answers with a story.

A man traveling from Jerusalem down to Jericho is attacked by robbers, who strip him, beat him and leave him half dead. The road, about seventeen miles, drops some 3,000 feet and was known for bandits. A priest comes by and passes on the other side. A Levite, a temple assistant, does the same. Then a Samaritan comes, and "when he saw him, he had compassion." He bandages his wounds, pours on oil and wine, puts him on his own animal, takes him to an inn and pays the bill, promising to return and repay whatever more is needed.

For Jesus' audience the shock was the hero. Samaritans and Jews despised each other. They were descendants of a mixed population from the time of the Assyrian conquest, with a rival temple, and Jews often avoided Samaritan territory. To say that a Samaritan was the one who obeyed the law of love was an insult to the pride of the lawyer.

Jesus then turns the question around. The lawyer asked who his neighbor is. Jesus asks, "Which of these three do you think was a neighbor to the man who fell into the hands of robbers?" The lawyer cannot bring himself to say the word Samaritan: "The one who showed him mercy." And Jesus says, "Go and do likewise" (Luke 10:36-37). Neighbor is not a category to be defined and limited, but a role to take on, wherever there is a need.

# The two sons and their father
@ref LUK 15:11-32; EZK 18:23; ROM 5:8
@q Which brother do you recognize in yourself more, and what would it take to come home?

Luke 15 contains three parables about something lost: a sheep, a coin and a son. The third is the best known, and is often called the parable of the prodigal son, but it is really about two sons and a father. Jesus told it because Pharisees and scribes were grumbling that he welcomed sinners and ate with them.

The younger son demands his share of the inheritance, which was a way of saying that he wished his father dead, and goes off to a distant country, where he wastes it all in reckless living. A famine hits, and he ends up feeding pigs, an unclean animal to a Jew, and wishing he could eat what they eat. He comes to his senses and decides to return and ask to be hired as a servant.

The surprise is the father. "While he was still in the distance, his father saw him and was filled with compassion. He ran to his son, embraced him, and kissed him" (Luke 15:20). An elderly man in that culture did not run. Before the son can finish his prepared speech, the father calls for the best robe, a ring and sandals, and a feast.

Then the story turns to the older brother, who has stayed home and worked and is furious. He refuses to come in. The father goes out to plead with him, too. The older brother complains that he has been slaving for years, and the father answers, "Son, you are always with me," and "all that is mine is yours. But it was fitting to celebrate and be glad, because this brother of yours was dead and is alive again; he was lost and is found" (Luke 15:31-32).

The story ends without saying what the older son did. Jesus leaves it open, because the Pharisees listening are the older brother, and the choice is theirs.

# Parables of the kingdom
@ref MAT 13:24-52; MRK 4:26-32; LUK 13:18-21
@conf scholars

Matthew 13 gathers seven of Jesus' parables about the kingdom of heaven, each of which looks at a different side of what it is. Together they correct some wrong expectations. Many people wanted a kingdom that would arrive with armies and force. Jesus described one that is hidden, gradual, costly and certain.

The mustard seed says it starts small. "Although it is the smallest of all seeds, yet it grows into the largest of garden plants and becomes a tree, so that the birds of the air come and nest in its branches" (Matthew 13:32). The leaven makes the same point from a different side: a little yeast "mixed into three measures of flour, until all of it was leavened." The kingdom works quietly from within and finally affects everything.

The weeds among the wheat say that good and evil will grow together until the end, when the harvest will separate them, so believers need patience and not a campaign to root out all evil. The net full of fish says the same about the final sorting.

The treasure in the field and the pearl of great price describe the value of the kingdom. In each case someone finds something worth more than everything they own, and "in his joy he went and sold all he had" and bought it. The cost is complete, but it is paid gladly, because what is received is worth far more.

The last parable says that every scribe trained for the kingdom is like a householder who brings out of his storeroom what is new and what is old. The kingdom was both the fulfillment of what had gone before and the start of something new. These parables invite readers to ask what they would give up, and whether they have found what is worth giving everything for.

# The miracles of Jesus
@ref MRK 1:21-28; MAT 11:2-6; JHN 20:30-31
@conf scholars

The Gospels record about thirty-five distinct miracles of Jesus, and many more are summarized in general terms. They fall into four groups: healings of the sick and disabled, the casting out of demons, miracles over nature, such as calming the storm and feeding the crowds, and the raising of the dead, of which three are told: Jairus' daughter, the son of the widow of Nain and Lazarus.

They are not presented as tricks to impress people. Jesus often refused to perform wonders on demand, and told some of those he healed to keep it quiet. The Gospels call them mighty works and signs, and each points to something about who he is and what the kingdom means. When John the Baptist, in prison, sent messengers to ask whether Jesus was the one who was to come, Jesus told them to report what they had heard and seen: "the blind receive sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the good news is preached to the poor" (Matthew 11:5). He was pointing them back to Isaiah's promises of what the Messiah's age would look like.

The miracles also show his compassion. He heals out of pity, touches lepers whom everyone else avoided, and weeps at a grave. And they show his authority: over disease, over spirits, over nature and over death. The people who saw them asked, "Who is this, that even the wind and the sea obey Him?"

Not everyone believed even then. Jesus' critics did not deny that something extraordinary was happening, and instead said that it was done by the power of Satan. The Gospels suggest that miracles can strengthen faith but cannot create it. John says he recorded some so that readers might believe. The church has always understood that the greatest of them is the resurrection itself.

# The paralyzed man and the forgiveness of sins
@ref MRK 2:1-12; ISA 43:25; PSA 103:2-3

Early in his ministry, Jesus is teaching in a house in Capernaum, and so many people have crowded in that there is no room, not even outside the door. Four men are carrying a paralyzed friend on a mat, and when they cannot get through, they climb onto the flat roof, dig through the clay and branches and lower him down in front of Jesus.

Jesus sees "their faith" and says something unexpected: "Son, your sins are forgiven." The paralyzed man had come for healing, and Jesus offered something deeper. Some scribes sitting there think to themselves, "Why does this man speak like this? He is blaspheming! Who can forgive sins but God alone?" They are exactly right about the question. Only God can forgive sins, and Jesus is claiming to do it.

Jesus knows what they are thinking and asks which is easier, to say your sins are forgiven or to say get up and walk. Anyone can say the first, since no one can see whether it has happened. The second is easily tested. "But so that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins," he says to the paralytic, "I tell you, get up, pick up your mat, and go home." The man stands up, picks up his mat and walks out in front of everyone. The crowd is amazed and says, "We have never seen anything like this!"

The story shows that the healing was meant to demonstrate the authority of the one who healed. It also shows the connection that runs through the Gospels between sin and what is broken in the world. And it shows the priorities of Jesus: first the forgiveness that deals with the deepest need, then the healing that deals with the visible one.

# The bread of life
@ref JHN 6:1-14; JHN 6:35; JHN 6:48-58
@conf scholars

The feeding of the five thousand is the only miracle of Jesus before the passion that appears in all four Gospels. A great crowd had followed him across the Sea of Galilee, and by evening they were far from any town. Jesus asked Philip where they could buy bread, to test him, though he knew what he intended. A boy had five barley loaves and two small fish. Jesus had the people sit on the grass, gave thanks, and distributed the food. They all ate as much as they wanted, and twelve baskets of fragments were left over. Matthew says that there were five thousand men, besides women and children.

John does what none of the others does. He follows the miracle with a long discourse the next day in the synagogue at Capernaum, which explains what the miracle means. The crowd wants more bread, and reminds Jesus that their ancestors ate manna in the wilderness. Jesus answers, "I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to Me will never hunger, and whoever believes in Me will never thirst" (John 6:35).

He goes further, and says that the bread that God gives is himself, and that unless people eat his flesh and drink his blood they have no life in them. Many of the disciples find this too hard, and "From that time on many of His disciples turned back and no longer walked with Him" (John 6:66). Jesus asks the Twelve whether they want to leave also, and Peter answers, "Lord, to whom would we go? You have the words of eternal life."

Christians have read the passage in different ways, some linking it with the Lord's Supper and others reading it as a picture of faith. Everyone agrees that it makes a sweeping claim: that the hunger of the human heart is finally met in Christ.

# The storm and the sea
@ref MRK 4:35-41; MAT 14:22-33; PSA 107:23-30
@conf scholars

The Sea of Galilee is a freshwater lake about thirteen miles long and eight wide, set in a bowl of hills about seven hundred feet below sea level. Cold air from the surrounding heights can pour down the valleys and whip up violent storms with very little warning. The disciples, several of whom were professional fishermen, knew the lake well.

One evening Jesus told them to cross to the other side. He fell asleep on a cushion in the stern. A furious squall came up, and the waves broke over the boat so that it began to fill. The disciples woke him in a panic: "Teacher, don't You care that we are perishing?" He rose, rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, "Silence! Be still!" The wind died down, and there was a great calm. Then he asked them, "Why are you so afraid?" He asked. "Do you still have no faith?" And they were terrified and asked each other, "Who is this, that even the wind and the sea obey Him?" (Mark 4:38-41).

The Old Testament gives the context. Psalm 107 says that those who go down to the sea in ships see the works of the LORD, and that he calms the storm, so that the waves are hushed. In the Scripture, only God does this. The disciples were beginning to realize that the man in the boat with them was doing what God does.

Another time, Jesus came to them walking on the lake at night, and Peter asked to come to him on the water. He walked a few steps, then saw the wind and began to sink, and cried out, "Lord, save me!" Jesus took his hand. The scenes teach that Jesus is Lord of the storm, and that he is with his people in it, even when he seems to be asleep.

# Lazarus: the last sign
@ref JHN 11:1-44; JHN 11:25-26; ISA 25:8
@conf scholars

The raising of Lazarus is the seventh and greatest of the signs in John, and it prepares for the end. Lazarus, who lived in Bethany, a village about two miles from Jerusalem, with his sisters Mary and Martha, was a close friend of Jesus. When he fell ill, the sisters sent word to Jesus, and Jesus did something surprising. He stayed two more days where he was. When he arrived, Lazarus had been in the tomb for four days.

Martha went to meet him and said, "Lord, if You had been here, my brother would not have died." Jesus told her that her brother would rise again, and she said that she knew he would rise in the resurrection on the last day. Jesus answered, "I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in Me will live, even though he dies. And everyone who lives and believes in Me will never die. Do you believe this?" (John 11:25-26). Her answer is one of the great confessions of the New Testament: "Yes, Lord," she answered, "I believe that You are the Christ, the Son of God, who was to come into the world."

When Jesus saw Mary and the others weeping, "He was deeply moved in spirit and troubled." Then comes the shortest verse in the English Bible: "Jesus wept." He wept in the face of death, though he knew what he was about to do. Then he went to the tomb, had the stone rolled away, and called with a loud voice, "Lazarus, come out!" The dead man came out, still wrapped in his grave clothes, and Jesus said, "Unwrap him and let him go."

The miracle led directly to the plot to kill Jesus. The Sanhedrin met and said that if they let him go on, everyone would believe in him. The sign of life led to the cross, and the cross to the resurrection that is the true answer to Martha's question.

# The seven "I am" sayings
@ref JHN 6:35; JHN 8:12; JHN 10:9-11; JHN 11:25-26; JHN 14:6; JHN 15:5
@conf scholars

In the Gospel of John, Jesus makes seven statements that begin "I am" and then name a picture. The phrase "I am" calls to mind God's own name in Exodus 3, "I AM WHO I AM," and Jesus also uses it without a predicate, as when he says to those who challenged him, "before Abraham was born, I am" (John 8:58). The seven are a collection of the ways John presents what he offers.

"I am the bread of life" (6:35): he satisfies the deepest hunger. "I am the light of the world. Whoever follows Me will never walk in the darkness, but will have the light of life" (8:12): he shows the way and drives out the darkness. "I am the gate" (10:9): he is the one way in, and those who enter are safe and find pasture. "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down His life for the sheep" (10:11): he knows his sheep and gives his life for them. "I am the resurrection and the life" (11:25): he conquers death. "I am the way and the truth and the life. No one comes to the Father except through Me" (14:6): he is the only road to God. And "I am the vine and you are the branches" (15:5): his followers live from him and bear fruit only by staying close.

Each of these is a claim that only God could make. And each is rooted in the Old Testament, in which God is the bread-giver, the light, the shepherd of his people, the life-giver and the vine's owner. Jesus takes these roles for himself.

Two of them draw particular attention. The claim in 14:6 is exclusive, and the church has always understood Jesus to mean what he says. The statement in 15:5, "apart from Me you can do nothing," is a reminder that the Christian life is not a matter of effort alone, but of connection.

# Jesus and the Pharisees
@ref MAT 23:1-28; MRK 7:1-13; LUK 18:9-14
@conf scholars

The Pharisees are the most frequent opponents of Jesus in the Gospels, and it is easy to flatten them into villains. They were a lay movement in Judaism of the first century, with a few thousand members, who stressed careful keeping of the Law and the oral traditions that applied it to everyday life. They believed in the resurrection, in angels and in the authority of the whole Hebrew Bible, and they were respected by many ordinary people. Paul was a Pharisee, and Nicodemus and Joseph of Arimathea, who honored Jesus after his death, were probably sympathizers.

The conflicts were real. Jesus and the Pharisees clashed over the Sabbath, over the traditions of the elders about washing and purity, over eating with tax collectors and sinners, and above all over his authority. Jesus accused them of valuing human tradition over God's word, of emphasizing outward correctness while neglecting the heart. In Matthew 23 he pronounces a series of woes on the scribes and Pharisees: "You pay tithes of mint, dill, and cumin. But you have disregarded the weightier matters of the law: justice, mercy, and faithfulness" (Matthew 23:23). He said they were like whitewashed tombs, clean outside and full of death within.

It would be a mistake to take this as a judgment on every Pharisee, or as a statement about Jews as a people. Jesus himself was a Jew, and these were arguments within Judaism, in the style of prophets before him. The church has too often used passages like this to justify hatred of the Jews, and that is a grave misuse.

The warning is meant for every religious person. The parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector in Luke 18 shows it. The Pharisee prays, "God, I thank You that I am not like other men." The tax collector says only, "God, have mercy on me, a sinner!" And Jesus says it was the tax collector who went home justified.
