# Job
@id book-job
@ref JOB 1:20-22; JOB 19:25-27; JOB 42:1-6
@conf scholars

Job has forty-two chapters and is one of the great books on suffering. It begins and ends in prose, and in between it is a long poem, a series of speeches. It belongs to the wisdom books, with Proverbs and Ecclesiastes, and it takes on the hardest question those books raise: why do the innocent suffer?

The book does not say who wrote it or when. Job himself lives in the land of Uz, and the details of his life, such as his wealth counted in animals and his role as the family priest, resemble the patriarchal world of Genesis. The language of the poetry is rich and difficult. The date of writing is debated, and the book has no connection with the history of Israel as such.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 and 2: a righteous man loses his children, wealth and health, after a challenge from Satan in the court of heaven.
- Chapters 3 to 31: Job laments and argues with three friends, Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar, who insist that he must have sinned.
- Chapters 32 to 37: a young man named Elihu offers a fourth view.
- Chapters 38 to 42: the LORD answers Job out of the whirlwind, and Job responds. He is restored.

## What to look for
Watch what the friends get wrong. They are certain that suffering always follows sin, and at the end God says they have not spoken rightly of him, while Job has (42:7). God's answer does not explain the reason for Job's pain. It shows him the scale of creation and asks whether Job understands how the world is run. The answer is a presence, not an argument. Job also glimpses a living Redeemer who will stand on the earth in the end (19:25), a hope Christians read in light of Christ.

# Psalms
@id book-psa
@ref PSA 1:1-3; PSA 23:1-6; PSA 150:1-6

The book of Psalms has 150 poems and is the longest book in the Bible, the hymnbook and prayer book of Israel. The Hebrew title is Tehillim, "Praises," although many of the psalms are laments. Jesus and the apostles quote it often, and Christians have prayed and sung it for two thousand years.

The psalms were written over perhaps a thousand years. The headings credit David with seventy-three, Asaph with twelve, the sons of Korah with eleven, and Solomon, Moses (Psalm 90), Ethan and Heman with a few. Many are anonymous. They were collected over time into five books, perhaps echoing the five books of Moses, and the whole was in place by the time of the second temple.

## How it is arranged
- Book 1, Psalms 1 to 41: mostly David's psalms, many of them laments and personal prayers.
- Book 2, Psalms 42 to 72: more of David, and the psalms of Korah, ending with a prayer for the king.
- Book 3, Psalms 73 to 89: Asaph and the psalms of national crisis.
- Book 4, Psalms 90 to 106: the LORD as King, in the wake of exile.
- Book 5, Psalms 107 to 150: thanksgiving, the Songs of Ascents (120 to 134), Psalm 119, and the closing praise.

## What to look for
Learn the kinds of psalm. Laments cry out of trouble and usually turn to trust. Hymns praise God for who he is. Thanksgiving psalms look back on rescue. Royal psalms, such as Psalms 2 and 110, point toward a king whom the New Testament identifies as Christ. Wisdom psalms, like Psalm 1, contrast two paths. The Psalms give honest words to every mood, and they teach that bringing a hard feeling to God is itself an act of faith.

# Proverbs
@id book-pro
@ref PRO 1:7; PRO 3:5-6; PRO 31:10-12

Proverbs has thirty-one chapters of short, memorable sayings, and it is the Bible's main book of wisdom. A proverb in the Bible is a pointed observation about how life usually works under God's rule. It is not a promise or a guarantee, and reading it that way is a common mistake.

Solomon is named at the start (1:1) and again at 10:1 and 25:1, where "the men of Hezekiah" are said to have copied more of his sayings about two centuries later. Two other voices appear, Agur in chapter 30 and King Lemuel in chapter 31. The book probably took its final form in the days of Hezekiah or later.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 9: a father urges his son to seek wisdom and avoid folly, with Wisdom and Folly pictured as two women calling from the street.
- Chapters 10 to 22: Solomon's sayings, mostly two-line contrasts.
- Chapters 22 to 24: sayings of the wise, including warnings and advice for rulers.
- Chapters 25 to 29: more of Solomon's proverbs, gathered under Hezekiah.
- Chapters 30 and 31: the words of Agur and Lemuel, and a closing poem on the woman of strength.

## What to look for
The foundation is in chapter 1: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge." Wisdom is skill for living, and it begins in reverence. Look at topics such as speech, money, work, friendship, anger and family. Notice that proverbs often pair up and seem to contradict each other (see 26:4-5), because wisdom is knowing which saying fits which situation. In chapter 8 Wisdom speaks as one present at creation, and Paul says Christ is the wisdom of God (1 Corinthians 1:24).

# Ecclesiastes
@id book-ecc
@ref ECC 1:2-3; ECC 3:1-8; ECC 12:13-14
@conf scholars

Ecclesiastes has twelve chapters and is the most puzzling of the wisdom books. The title is the Greek form of the Hebrew Qoheleth, usually translated "the Preacher" or "the Teacher," a word that comes from a root meaning to gather an assembly. The book is his sustained meditation on life "under the sun."

The writer calls himself "son of David, king in Jerusalem," which is traditionally taken as Solomon. Many modern scholars, noting the style of the Hebrew, place the final form later, in the Persian period. Those who accept Solomon point to his wealth and wisdom, which the book claims. Either way, the book is presented as the Teacher's hard-won conclusions.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 and 2: the search for meaning in wisdom, pleasure and achievement, all of which prove to be vapor.
- Chapters 3 to 6: time and eternity, injustice, work, wealth and the limits of what people can know.
- Chapters 7 to 10: practical wisdom, with proverbs about folly and advice for a confusing world.
- Chapters 11 and 12: live boldly and joyfully, remember your Creator while you are young, and fear God.

## What to look for
The famous word is hevel, "vapor," "breath," or "meaningless," which appears nearly forty times. It does not mean nothing matters. It means that life is fleeting and cannot be grasped or controlled. The Teacher says that the answer is not to give up but to receive each day's food, work and love as a gift from God. The book ends with the conclusion that to fear God and keep his commandments is the whole duty of man, and Paul's remark that creation was subjected to futility (Romans 8:20) echoes this book.

# Song of Solomon
@id book-sng
@ref SNG 2:10-13; SNG 6:3; SNG 8:6-7
@conf debated

The Song of Solomon has eight chapters, and it is a collection of love poems between a young woman and a man, with a chorus of friends. Its Hebrew title, Shir Hashirim, means "the Song of Songs," that is, the best of songs. It is the only book in the Bible devoted entirely to romantic love.

The title says "of Solomon," which could mean by, for, or about him. If Solomon wrote it, the date would be the tenth century BC. Many scholars think the poems were collected later. The setting is the countryside and the city, with gardens, vineyards, gazelles and spices, in language that is unusually frank and tender for ancient poetry.

## How it is arranged
The book is hard to outline because it is a series of lyrical scenes, but readers usually see a rough progress.
- Chapters 1 to 3: courtship, longing and the first meetings.
- Chapters 3 to 5: the wedding and its night, and a dream of loss.
- Chapters 5 to 8: reconciliation, deepening love, and the seal of love in the closing chapters.

## How to read it
Christians have read the Song in three main ways. Many in the early and medieval church took it as an allegory of Christ and his church, or of God and the soul. Others read it as literal poetry about marriage, which God made good. Some hold both: it celebrates human love, and that love points to a greater one. The great statement at the end, "love is as strong as death," holds either way, and the Song shows that desire and commitment belong together (8:6-7).

# Isaiah
@id book-isa
@ref ISA 6:1-8; ISA 40:28-31; ISA 53:4-6
@conf scholars

Isaiah has sixty-six chapters and is the longest of the prophetic books, sometimes called the fifth gospel because of how often the New Testament quotes it. Isaiah's name means "the LORD saves," and the book is about salvation in a world of judgment.

Isaiah son of Amoz prophesied in Jerusalem from about 740 BC, in the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah. Many scholars think chapters 40 to 66 come from the time of the exile and after, and refer to a later prophet or school. Others hold that one prophet wrote the whole book and foresaw the exile and Cyrus. The great Isaiah scroll found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, about a thousand years older than any other complete copy, shows the book already in its full form by about 125 BC.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 12: the call of Isaiah, warnings to Judah, and promises of a future king, the child Immanuel.
- Chapters 13 to 35: oracles to the nations and woes against faithless leaders, with visions of the future kingdom.
- Chapters 36 to 39: a historical bridge, in which Hezekiah faces the Assyrians and then Babylon.
- Chapters 40 to 55: comfort for the exiles and the Servant of the LORD, culminating in chapters 52 and 53.
- Chapters 56 to 66: the future of God's people, ending with a new heavens and a new earth.

## What to look for
Isaiah's favorite name for God is "the Holy One of Israel." The book holds judgment and hope together, and it keeps pointing to a coming king and a suffering servant. Look at how chapters 7, 9 and 11 speak of the king and chapter 53 of the servant who bears sin. Jesus read from Isaiah 61 at Nazareth. The ending, with the new creation in chapters 65 and 66, is picked up in Revelation.

# Jeremiah
@id book-jer
@ref JER 1:4-10; JER 29:10-14; JER 31:31-34

Jeremiah has fifty-two chapters and is one of the longest books in the Bible. It is the record of a prophet who lived through the end of Judah and who suffered for telling people the truth about it. It is deeply personal. Jeremiah's own prayers and complaints are woven in as in no other prophet.

Jeremiah was a priest from Anathoth, called about 627 BC under King Josiah, and he prophesied until after the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC. His scribe Baruch wrote down his words (chapter 36). The material is not in order of date, since it was arranged by theme and by the way the book was edited, which can confuse a new reader.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: Jeremiah's call.
- Chapters 2 to 25: warnings and calls to repent, with images of the potter, the broken jar and the linen belt, and the "temple sermon."
- Chapters 26 to 29: conflict with false prophets, and the letter to the exiles.
- Chapters 30 to 33: the Book of Consolation, with the promise of the new covenant.
- Chapters 34 to 45: stories of the siege, the fall and the aftermath.
- Chapters 46 to 52: oracles against the nations, and an appendix about the fall.

## What to look for
Jeremiah's message is that outward religion without a changed heart cannot save. His own life is the sermon: he is not allowed to marry, he is thrown into a cistern, and he weeps over those he warns. The center of hope is the new covenant, in which God writes his law on hearts and forgives sins. Jesus calls the cup at the Last Supper the new covenant, and Hebrews 8 quotes the whole passage.

# Lamentations
@id book-lam
@ref LAM 3:21-26; LAM 5:19-22

Lamentations has five chapters, and it is a book of five poems of grief over the destruction of Jerusalem. The Hebrew title, Eikhah, "How," is the first word of the book, the cry of someone staring at a ruin: how the city sits alone. It is the Bible's funeral song for a city.

The book is anonymous. Greek and Latin tradition connect it to Jeremiah, who did write laments for Josiah (2 Chronicles 35:25), though nothing in the book says so. It was written after the fall of the city in 586 BC and probably within a generation, by someone who had seen it.

## How it is arranged
The first four poems are acrostics, in which each verse or group of verses begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, as if to say that all grief from A to Z is being put into words.
- Chapter 1: the city sits desolate, and no one comforts her.
- Chapter 2: God's anger against Zion, and the suffering of children.
- Chapter 3: the poet's personal pain, and a turn to hope at the center. This chapter has three verses for each letter.
- Chapter 4: the siege and its horrors, remembered.
- Chapter 5: a final prayer for restoration, not an acrostic but with twenty-two verses.

## What to look for
Lamentations gives permission to grieve. It does not rush to explain, and yet at the center it makes one of the great statements of faith: "Because of the loving devotion of the LORD we are not consumed, for His mercies never fail." The final lines ask God to restore his people, and they end with a question that is left open. The book teaches that faith can sit in the ashes, and still call God faithful.
