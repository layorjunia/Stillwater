# The church in the first century
@ref ACT 28:30-31; 1PE 4:12-16; REV 1:9
@conf scholars

The first generation of the church is the best documented, because Luke tells the story in Acts and the apostles wrote the letters that fill the rest of the New Testament. By the time Paul reaches Rome around AD 60, congregations of Jews and Gentiles had been planted across the eastern Mediterranean, in cities such as Antioch, Ephesus, Corinth and Philippi, and Christianity was already known to the authorities in Rome as a movement distinct from Judaism.

The Roman historian Tacitus records the first state action. In AD 64 a great fire destroyed much of Rome, and Nero, who was rumored to have started it, blamed the Christians. Tacitus says they were arrested in large numbers, and some were torn by dogs or burned as torches at night. Early tradition holds that both Peter and Paul were killed in Rome in the aftermath, though the New Testament does not tell us. Their graves were honored from very early on.

In AD 70 the Romans crushed the Jewish revolt, and the temple in Jerusalem was burned. This was an enormous event for the young church. Jesus had foretold that not one stone of the temple would be left on another (Mark 13:2), and Christians took the destruction as a sign that the old system of sacrifice had given way to the new. The church in Jerusalem, according to a tradition recorded by Eusebius, had left the city for Pella before the siege.

By the end of the century the apostles had mostly died. John, according to tradition, lived the longest, in Ephesus, and wrote his Gospel, letters and Revelation late in life. Christians now spread through the empire as a community with no temples, images or priests of the kind the Romans knew. They met in homes. They refused to sacrifice to the emperor. And as they grew, they came to be seen as a danger, and sometimes as an enemy, by the world around them.

# Why Christians were persecuted
@ref 1PE 2:12; REV 2:10; ACT 17:6-7
@conf scholars

The first three centuries were not a constant bloodbath. Persecution was local and sporadic for most of the time. It flared up under particular emperors and governors, and for long stretches the church was left alone. But the danger was real, and the reasons for it are worth understanding.

The Romans were tolerant of foreign religions as long as they did not threaten public order and as long as their followers also honored the gods of the state. Christians refused. They would not sacrifice to the emperor, whom many Romans treated as divine, or join in the civic festivals, since these involved idols. Neighbors accused them of atheism, because they had no images, and of immorality, because of rumors about their secret meetings and the language of "eating the body" and "love feasts." Merchants who sold idols or sacrificial meat lost business. And whenever there was a flood, famine or plague, people were quick to say that the gods were angry at the Christians.

A famous letter around AD 112 shows how it worked. Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia, wrote to the emperor Trajan to ask what to do about Christians. He had been questioning those accused, letting those who denied the faith go if they offered wine and incense to the emperor's image, and executing those who refused. He reported that they met before dawn to sing a hymn to Christ "as to a god," and to bind themselves by an oath not to steal, commit adultery or break their word. Trajan replied that Christians should not be hunted out, but those who were accused and refused should be punished.

The first empire-wide persecution came under Decius in AD 250, who ordered every citizen to sacrifice and obtain a certificate. The Great Persecution under Diocletian, from AD 303, was the last and the most severe, and ended with the toleration decree of Galerius in 311. Tertullian told the authorities that "the blood of Christians is seed," and in fact the church grew.

# Polycarp and Perpetua
@ref REV 2:8-11; MAT 10:28-33; 2TI 4:7-8
@conf scholars

The Greek word martys means witness, and in the early church it came to mean someone who bore witness to Christ with their life. Accounts of martyrs were collected and read aloud, and two of the earliest are especially vivid.

Polycarp was bishop of Smyrna, a city of Asia Minor, and he had known the apostle John, according to Irenaeus. In about AD 155 or 156, when he was an old man, he was arrested during a festival. The proconsul urged him to swear by the emperor and curse Christ, and promised to release him. Polycarp answered, "Eighty-six years I have served Him, and He has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King who saved me?" He was put to death, and the account of it, written by his church in the form of a letter, is one of the oldest Christian documents outside the New Testament.

Perpetua was a young noblewoman of Carthage in North Africa, about twenty-two years old, with a baby at the breast. She was arrested in AD 203 with several others, including a slave woman named Felicity, for being a catechumen, which means a new convert preparing for baptism. Her prison diary is the earliest surviving writing by a Christian woman. In it she describes her father's pleading that she give up her faith, the dreams she had in prison, and her calm answer to him that she could be called nothing other than what she was, a Christian. She and her companions were killed in the arena.

The stories were not told to glorify death. They were told to show that the faith was worth more than life and that the Lord kept his people. The church honored the martyrs' graves, and the date of death was celebrated as their birthday into the life to come.

# The first church fathers
@ref ACT 20:28-31; 1JN 4:1-3; JUD 1:3
@conf scholars

The generation after the apostles is called the apostolic fathers, writers of the late first and early second century who were taught by the apostles or their followers. Their writings are not Scripture, but they show what the earliest Christians believed and how they lived.

Clement of Rome wrote a long letter to the church at Corinth around AD 96, urging them to restore elders they had removed from office. He cites the Old Testament and some of Paul's letters, and speaks of the apostles appointing leaders in each church. Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, was arrested and taken to Rome to be executed about AD 110. On the way he wrote seven letters, urging unity around the bishop and insisting against some who said that Christ only seemed to have a body that he truly suffered and rose. The Didache, or Teaching of the Twelve Apostles, is a short church manual that may date from the first century. It describes baptism, fasting, the Lord's Supper and two ways, a way of life and a way of death.

Another work, the Epistle of Barnabas, and the Shepherd of Hermas, both read in some churches, were eventually left out of the New Testament. Polycarp wrote to the Philippians, and Papias, bishop of Hierapolis, collected sayings about Jesus and gave some of the earliest testimony to the origin of Mark and Matthew.

These writers show a church that was already worshiping Christ as God, meeting weekly on Sunday, baptizing, sharing the Lord's Supper and holding to the apostles' teaching. They also reveal the first challenges: false teaching about the person of Christ, and splits within the community. As the eyewitnesses died, the question grew sharper: how can the church be sure of what the apostles taught? The answers came in the second century, in the form of the Scriptures, the creed and the bishops.

# Gnostics, Marcion and the church's answer
@ref 1TI 6:20-21; 1JN 4:1-3; COL 2:8
@conf scholars

In the second century the church faced its first major internal crisis. Several movements offered a different Christianity, and the way the church responded shaped what it became.

Gnosticism was a family of teachings that promised salvation through secret knowledge, in Greek gnosis. Most Gnostics held that the material world was made by a lesser or even evil being, not the true God, and that the spirit must escape from matter. Some said Christ only seemed to have a body, since a divine being could not be tainted by flesh. Gnostic writings such as the Gospel of Thomas and the Gospel of Judas were written in the second century or later, and many were found in the Egyptian desert at Nag Hammadi in 1945. They claim secret teaching from the apostles, but they come long after the apostles.

Marcion, a wealthy shipowner from Pontus who came to Rome about AD 140, went in a different direction. He taught that the God of the Old Testament, a God of law and wrath, was a different God from the Father of Jesus, who is a God of love. He threw out the whole Old Testament and made his own Bible, with an edited Luke and ten letters of Paul. The church in Rome expelled him around AD 144.

The church replied in three ways. First, it pointed to the apostolic succession: the churches founded by the apostles had preserved their teaching, and leaders could show how it had been handed down. Second, it formulated a rule of faith, a short summary of the basic beliefs, which grew into the Apostles' Creed. Third, it moved toward a settled canon of Scripture, in part because Marcion had forced the question of which books were authoritative. The danger had led to clarity, as a later lesson on the canon describes.

# Irenaeus and Tertullian
@ref JUD 1:3; 2TI 1:13-14; 1CO 15:3-4
@conf scholars

Two of the greatest early defenders of the faith worked at opposite ends of the Mediterranean, and between them they laid the foundations of orthodox theology.

Irenaeus was born in Asia Minor, where in his youth he heard Polycarp preach, and became bishop of Lyon in Gaul about AD 177. Around AD 180 he wrote Against Heresies, a long refutation of the Gnostics. He argues that the one God made the world through the Word and the Spirit, that matter is good, and that Christ became man to save the whole person. He says that the apostles' teaching has been kept openly in the churches, and he gives a short statement of the faith that is a forerunner of the creeds. He describes the whole story of salvation as a recapitulation, a summing up, in which Christ goes over again the path of Adam and reverses it.

Tertullian was a lawyer from Carthage in North Africa who became a Christian about AD 197 and wrote in Latin. He was the first great Latin theologian, with a sharp pen. He coined many terms that became standard, including the Latin word trinitas, Trinity, and the formula "one substance, three persons." He defended the church against the charge of being a danger to society, and he asked, "What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?", by which he meant that the church did not need Greek philosophy to tell it what to believe. Late in life he drifted into the Montanist movement, which stressed new prophecy and strict discipline, and left the main church.

Both men worked with a conviction that is easy to overlook: that the faith was handed down, and was not invented by each age. Their writings show a church that was already confessing God as Trinity, Christ as divine and human, and the body as part of the redemption.

# Alexandria, Clement and Origen
@ref ACT 18:24-28; 1CO 1:20-25; COL 2:8
@conf scholars

Alexandria in Egypt was the second city of the empire, famous for its library and its scholars, and by the late second century it had become a center of Christian learning. The catechetical school there tried to show that the faith could be taught with the best learning of the age.

Clement of Alexandria, who taught there around AD 190 to 200, argued that Greek philosophy had been a schoolmaster to bring the Greeks to Christ, in the way that the law had been for the Jews. Origen, his greatest student, was the most gifted scholar of the early church. Born around AD 185, he had a father who was killed in a persecution, and as a teenager he wanted to go to martyrdom himself and was stopped only because his mother hid his clothes. He taught from the age of eighteen and wrote on a huge scale.

His Hexapla set six versions of the Old Testament side by side, in Hebrew, in Hebrew letters transliterated into Greek, and in four Greek translations, the earliest serious work in textual criticism. He wrote commentaries on most books of the Bible and a great work of theology, On First Principles. He taught that Scripture has several levels of meaning, literal, moral and spiritual, a method of interpretation that influenced centuries of readers. Some of his speculations were later condemned, including that souls existed before bodies and that all might finally be saved.

During the persecution of Decius in AD 250 he was imprisoned and tortured, and he died not long afterward, about AD 253. Even those who rejected parts of his thought honored his learning. His example showed that the faith can ask hard questions and give honest answers, and also that a gifted mind can wander beyond what the church accepted.
