# Made in the image of God
@ref GEN 1:26-27; GEN 9:6; PSA 8:3-6; JAS 3:9
@conf debated
@q How would you treat people differently if you remembered all day that each one carries the image of God?

The Bible's answer to the question of what a human being is begins in Genesis 1: "So God created man in His own image; in the image of God He created him; male and female He created them" (Genesis 1:27). Nothing else in creation is said to be made in God's image. It is the thing that sets people apart from animals, and it is the basis of the dignity of every person.

What does the phrase mean? Christians have given three main answers, and most think the truth combines them. The structural view says the image is something in us, such as reason, conscience, moral awareness or the capacity to speak and love. The relational view says it is the ability to be in relationship with God and with each other, for God himself is a communion of persons. The functional view notes that the next sentence in Genesis speaks of ruling over creation, and says we image God by acting as his representatives, caring for the world on his behalf.

The consequences are far-reaching. Murder is wrong because the victim bears God's image (Genesis 9:6). Cursing a person is wrong because people "have been made in God’s likeness" (James 3:9). Every human life has value, whether it is useful or not, strong or weak, born or unborn, rich or poor. The Bible says that God made man "a little lower than the angels" and crowned him "with glory and honor" (Psalm 8:5).

The image was marred in the fall, but not erased, and the New Testament says it is being restored. Christ is "the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15), and those who belong to him are being "conformed to the image of His Son" (Romans 8:29). The true human being is the one who shows us what the original design was meant to look like.

# Body and soul
@ref GEN 2:7; MAT 10:28; 1TH 5:23; 1CO 15:42-44
@conf debated

The Bible says that human beings are made of more than one part, but it does not give a single neat answer on how many. Genesis describes the first man as dust shaped by God and filled with breath, becoming a living creature. He is body and breath, and he is one person.

Over the centuries Christians have used three main frameworks. Dichotomy holds that a person has two parts, a material body and an immaterial soul, or spirit, with the two words describing the same inner part. Jesus seems to say this when he tells his hearers not to fear those who "kill the body but cannot kill the soul" (Matthew 10:28). Trichotomy holds that there are three parts, body, soul and spirit, and points to Paul's prayer that "your entire spirit, soul, and body be kept blameless" (1 Thessalonians 5:23), and to Hebrews 4:12. Holism, which has grown in popularity, says that the Bible views the person as an integrated unity, and that words such as soul, spirit and heart describe aspects of the whole person rather than separate parts.

The question has practical effects. If a person is a body-soul unit, then the body matters, and the Christian hope is not an escape from the body but its redemption. That is why the Bible does not speak of floating souls as the final goal. Paul says that "What is sown is perishable; it is raised imperishable" and that it is "raised a spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:42-44). The Apostles' Creed says, "I believe in the resurrection of the body."

Another matter is what happens between death and resurrection. Most Christians hold that the soul survives death and is with the Lord, as Paul suggests when he says he would "prefer to be away from the body and at home with the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:8). A few hold that the person sleeps until the resurrection. The next lessons on last things return to this.

# What sin is
@ref ROM 3:23; 1JN 3:4; PSA 51:3-4; JAS 4:17
@q Which kind of sin is easiest for you to excuse: the sin of doing wrong or the sin of leaving good undone?

Sin is the central problem in the Bible, and the Bible has more than one word for it. The most common Hebrew and Greek words describe missing a mark, going astray, crossing a line, rebellion, guilt and uncleanness. Together they paint sin as many things: a failure to hit the target, a path that wanders off, a wall that is crossed, a debt that is owed, a stain that needs cleaning.

At its heart, sin is a failure to love and honor God as he deserves. John writes, "Everyone who practices sin practices lawlessness as well. Indeed, sin is lawlessness" (1 John 3:4). Paul says that "all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23). When David confesses his adultery and murder, he says to God, "Against You, You only, have I sinned" (Psalm 51:4), because every wrong done to another person is also a wrong against the Lord of all.

Sin includes actions, but it is not limited to them. Jesus taught that anger is a root of murder and lust of adultery. Sin includes attitudes, words and omissions. James says, "Anyone, then, who knows the right thing to do, yet fails to do it, is guilty of sin" (James 4:17). Sin also includes a condition, a bent in the human heart, so that people sin because they are sinners and not only the reverse.

Because sin offends against a holy God, it is serious. It cannot be dealt with by saying that it does not matter, or by doing more good deeds than bad, since a debt is not paid by earning other money. The Bible's answer is that sin has to be forgiven and removed, and that only God can do that. The doctrine of sin is not meant to leave us in despair, but to show the need for the doctrine of salvation, and the whole of the gospel depends on taking the first seriously.

# How sin spread to everyone
@ref ROM 5:12-19; PSA 51:5; EPH 2:1-3
@conf debated

If Adam and Eve sinned in Genesis 3, how does that reach everyone else? The question is old, and the church has given more than one answer. The key text is Romans 5: "just as sin entered the world through one man, and death through sin, so also death was passed on to all men, because all sinned" (Romans 5:12). Paul sets Adam against Christ: "just as through the disobedience of the one man the many were made sinners, so also through the obedience of the one man the many will be made righteous" (Romans 5:19).

Augustine, the North African bishop who died in AD 430, taught that all humanity was somehow present in Adam, so that his sin is ours both in guilt and in a corrupt nature passed down. This is called original sin. He developed the view in his fight with Pelagius, a British monk who argued that people are born morally neutral and that Adam only set a bad example. The church at the Council of Carthage in AD 418 rejected Pelagius.

Later traditions refined the picture. Roman Catholics hold that original sin is inherited and removed by baptism, though it leaves a tendency toward sin. The Eastern Orthodox emphasize the inheritance of mortality and corruption more than of guilt. Lutheran and Reformed Christians stress that people are born guilty and corrupt. Some Protestants, including many Baptists and Wesleyans, hold that people inherit a corrupted nature and are accountable for their own sins. Many hold that children who die in infancy are saved by God's mercy.

All the major traditions agree that all people sin, that the tendency is present from the start (David says, "I was brought forth in iniquity," Psalm 51:5), and that the answer is found in Christ, the second Adam. The point of Romans 5 is not to puzzle us about Adam, but to say that if one man's act could bring so much ruin, one man's act of obedience can bring much more life.

# How bad is it? The effects of sin
@ref EPH 2:1-5; ROM 3:9-12; GEN 3:14-19; ROM 8:20-21
@conf debated

The Bible describes the effects of sin in several layers. The first is on our relationship with God. After the fall the man and woman hide from him, and Paul says that before Christ we were "dead in your trespasses and sins" (Ephesians 2:1). The second is on one another: blame, shame, violence and broken marriages come into the story at once. The third is on ourselves, in the form of a darkened mind, a hardened conscience, and desires that pull the wrong way. The fourth is on creation, which is "subjected to futility" and in "bondage to decay" until it is set free (Romans 8:20-21). And the fifth is death, which is the penalty for sin, and which enters through one man.

Theologians use the phrase total depravity, one of the points of the Reformed acronym TULIP. It is often misunderstood. It does not mean that people are as bad as they could be, or that they can do no good. Non-Christians can be kind, honest and brave. It means that sin has touched every part of a person, the mind, will, emotions and body, so that nothing in us is untouched, and that we are unable to save ourselves or to please God by our own power. The word "spiritually dead" describes the extent of the problem.

Not all Christians agree on how far inability goes. Reformed theology says that without the Spirit's work, a person cannot choose God, and that grace is irresistible. Arminians say that sin has made us unable to turn to God by ourselves, but that God gives a grace to everyone that frees the will enough to respond. Catholic theology teaches that human nature is wounded but not totally corrupted. The debate matters most for the doctrine of how people are saved.

The right response to this teaching is not to dwell on the dark side, but to see why the rescue was so costly. As Paul writes, "But because of His great love for us, God, who is rich in mercy, made us alive with Christ even when we were dead in our trespasses" (Ephesians 2:4-5).

# The Word became flesh
@ref JHN 1:14; PHP 2:5-8; GAL 4:4-5; HEB 2:14-17
@conf scholars

The word incarnation comes from Latin and means "becoming flesh." It names the central event of Christian history: that God the Son took on a full human nature and was born as a baby in Bethlehem. John says it in one line: "The Word became flesh and made His dwelling among us" (John 1:14).

Several things stand out in the New Testament accounts. First, the birth was a real birth. The Son was conceived by the Holy Spirit in the womb of Mary, a virgin, and grew like any child. He was hungry, tired and thirsty. He grew in wisdom (Luke 2:52). He wept at the grave of Lazarus. He suffered and died. Second, he took our nature for a purpose. Hebrews says, "Since the children have flesh and blood, He too shared in their humanity, so that by His death He might destroy him who holds the power of death" (Hebrews 2:14). He had to become like his brothers in every way, in order to be a merciful and faithful high priest.

Third, he did not stop being God. Paul says that he "emptied Himself, taking the form of a servant" (Philippians 2:7). The best understanding has been that he laid aside the privileges of divine glory, but not his divine nature. The one who made the world became a creature, and he did so without losing anything of what he is.

The incarnation is God's answer to the question of how a holy God can be close to sinful people. He did not shout across a distance. He came. It is the reason Christians say that Jesus reveals God, and the reason the church has insisted so firmly on both halves of the claim, that he is truly God and truly man, and that if either is lost, the gospel is lost.

# Two natures, one person
@ref JHN 1:1-14; COL 2:9; HEB 4:15
@conf scholars

Once the church affirmed that Jesus is God and that he is man, the next question was how the two fit together. The fifth century was spent on it, and the answer remains the standard for almost all Christians: Jesus is one person with two natures, divine and human.

Several wrong turns were taken and rejected. Docetism held that Jesus only seemed to have a body. Arianism held that he was a created being. Apollinarianism held that he had a human body but not a human mind. Nestorianism seemed to divide him into two persons, one divine and one human. Eutychianism, or monophysitism, held that the two natures were blended into one, so that his humanity was swallowed up. Each of these tries to simplify the mystery by dropping one half of the truth.

The Council of Chalcedon met in AD 451 near Constantinople and issued a definition that is still honored by Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant churches. It confesses one and the same Christ, the Son, the Lord, "in two natures, without confusion, without change, without division, without separation." The differences of the natures are not removed by their union, and the properties of each are preserved in one person. The Oriental Orthodox churches, such as the Coptic and Armenian, did not accept the council, but today many of them hold that the disagreement was largely about words.

This means that what Jesus did, he did as one person. When he was hungry, it was the Son of God who was hungry. When he said to the paralyzed man that his sins were forgiven, it was a man who spoke with God's authority. Paul says, "For in Christ all the fullness of the Deity dwells in bodily form" (Colossians 2:9). Because he is truly man he can represent us, and because he is truly God his sacrifice can save.

# The sinless life of Jesus
@ref HEB 4:15; 2CO 5:21; 1PE 2:21-23; JHN 8:46
@q What would it mean to you that Jesus faced every kind of temptation and never gave in?

All four Gospels present Jesus as someone without sin. He is tempted, but he does not fall. He is accused of many things, but when he challenges his opponents with "Which of you can prove Me guilty of sin?" (John 8:46), no one answers. Those who knew him best bear the same witness. Peter says that "He committed no sin, and no deceit was found in His mouth" (1 Peter 2:22). Paul says that God made "Him who knew no sin to be sin on our behalf, so that in Him we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Corinthians 5:21). Hebrews adds that we have a high priest who "was tempted in every way that we are, yet was without sin" (Hebrews 4:15).

The sinlessness of Jesus has two uses. It qualifies him to be the sacrifice for sin. In the Old Testament a lamb offered for sin had to be without blemish, and a person who is himself guilty cannot make up for another's guilt. Jesus' perfect life is what makes his death a worthy offering. And it gives him a perfect obedience that can be credited to others. Paul says that "through the obedience of the one man the many will be made righteous" (Romans 5:19), and Reformed theology calls this the active obedience of Christ.

It also matters for how we think of temptation. Jesus' temptation in the wilderness was real, and he resisted it by trusting God's word. Because he faced temptation without giving in, he understands it more fully than we do, since the person who never yields feels the full force of the pressure. Hebrews tells believers to "approach the throne of grace with confidence," because we are not coming to a judge who cannot sympathize. We are coming to one who has been where we are.

# What the cross did: five ways to say it
@ref MRK 10:45; ROM 3:23-26; COL 2:13-15; 1PE 2:24
@conf debated

The cross is the center of the Christian faith, and the New Testament describes what happened there in many ways. The church has never officially adopted a single theory of the atonement, a word meaning "at-one-ment," the reconciling of God and humanity. It has gathered several biblical pictures, each of which captures part of the truth.

Ransom. Jesus said that he came "to give His life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). A ransom is a price paid to free someone held captive. Early Christian writers such as Origen and Gregory of Nyssa built on this picture, and some of them asked whom the price was paid to.

Victory. Paul says that on the cross God "disarmed the powers and authorities" and made a public spectacle of them, "triumphing over them by the cross" (Colossians 2:15). The Swedish theologian Gustaf Aulén called this view Christus Victor in 1931, and the Eastern church has long stressed it.

Satisfaction and substitution. Paul says that God presented Jesus "as an atoning sacrifice in His blood" to demonstrate his righteousness (Romans 3:25). Anselm of Canterbury, in 1098, argued that the offense against God's honor required satisfaction. The Reformers, notably Calvin, added that Christ took the penalty of sin in our place. Peter says, "He Himself bore our sins in His body on the tree" (1 Peter 2:24). This view is called penal substitution, and many evangelicals hold it as central.

Example and love. Peter says Christ left us an example. In the twelfth century Peter Abelard argued that the cross shows us God's love so that it moves us to love him in return, which is called the moral influence view.

Many theologians say that each of these has strong biblical support, and that the weakest accounts are the ones that choose one and throw out the rest. All of them agree on the main thing. The death of Jesus was not an accident or a defeat. It was the purposeful act by which God dealt with sin, defeated evil and reconciled the world to himself.

# He is risen, and he reigns
@ref 1CO 15:3-4; ACT 1:9-11; HEB 7:25; ROM 8:34
@conf scholars

The death of Jesus is not the end of his story. On the third day he rose from the dead, in a real body that could be touched and that ate fish, but that also passed through closed doors. The resurrection was not a resuscitation, like that of Lazarus, who would die again. It was a new kind of bodily life, the beginning of the new creation. Paul calls Christ the firstfruits, the first piece of a harvest still to come.

Forty days later, according to Luke, he ascended into heaven. The disciples watched him taken up in a cloud, and two men in white said, "This same Jesus, who has been taken from you into heaven, will come back in the same way you have seen Him go into heaven" (Acts 1:11). The ascension is not just a farewell. It is the enthronement of the king. Christ now sits at the right hand of the Father, a place of honor and authority.

What is he doing there? The New Testament says first that he rules. All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to him. Second, he intercedes for his people. Hebrews says, "Therefore He is able to save completely those who draw near to God through Him, since He always lives to intercede for them" (Hebrews 7:25). Third, he prepares a place for his followers, and sends the Spirit. And fourth, he waits, until his enemies are put under his feet.

Most Christians confess that Christ will return visibly. That is the subject of a lesson on last things. For now it is enough to notice what the resurrection and ascension together mean. The one who suffered is now Lord of all, and the life of the believer is hidden with him there, which is why Paul says, "set your minds on things above" (Colossians 3:2).

# Prophet, priest and king
@ref DEU 18:15-19; PSA 110:1-4; ZEC 9:9; HEB 1:1-3
@conf scholars

In the Old Testament three kinds of people were anointed with oil to serve God and his people: prophets, priests and kings. When the church tried to describe what Christ does, it noticed that he fulfills all three roles, and since John Calvin the pattern has been called the threefold office of Christ.

As prophet, Jesus speaks God's word to the people. Moses had promised that God would raise up a prophet like himself, whom the people must hear (Deuteronomy 18:15). Jesus teaches with authority, reveals the Father, and tells the future of the kingdom. Hebrews says that God, who spoke through the prophets, has spoken finally "by His Son."

As priest, Jesus represents the people before God. A priest offers sacrifice and prays. Jesus offered himself as the sacrifice, and he continues to intercede. Psalm 110 had spoken of a king who is also "a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek," and Hebrews takes the idea and shows that Jesus is this priest, with a priesthood that does not end because he lives forever.

As king, Jesus rules. He is the son of David who inherits the throne that was promised for ever. Zechariah had predicted that the king would come "righteous and victorious, humble and riding on a donkey" (Zechariah 9:9), and the Gospels show Jesus entering Jerusalem in that way. His kingdom is already present, in the church and in the hearts of those who follow him, and it will be complete when he returns.

The three offices answer three needs. We are ignorant, and the prophet teaches us. We are guilty, and the priest atones. We are weak and rebellious, and the king rules and protects. And they hold together in the one name, Christ, which means "the anointed one."
