# How the Old Testament took shape
@ref LUK 24:44; LUK 11:50-51; ROM 3:1-2
@conf scholars
@q Why might it matter that the Old Testament Jesus read is the same one Christians read today?

The Old Testament that Christians read is the same collection of books that Jews call the Tanakh, though arranged in a different order. The word Tanakh is an acronym of the three sections of the Hebrew Bible: Torah, the Law of Moses; Nevi'im, the Prophets; and Ketuvim, the Writings. The Jewish count has twenty-four books, since the Twelve Minor Prophets are counted as one, and Samuel, Kings, Chronicles and Ezra with Nehemiah are each counted as one. It is the same text as the thirty-nine books in a Protestant Bible, only organized differently.

Jesus knew this collection and spoke of it by its parts. After the resurrection he told his disciples that everything written about him "in the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms" must be fulfilled (Luke 24:44). When he speaks of the martyrs "from the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah" (Luke 11:51), he is probably moving from the first book of the Hebrew Bible, Genesis, to the last, Chronicles, where the death of a Zechariah is recorded (2 Chronicles 24:20-22).

How the collection was fixed is not told in detail. The Law was recognized first, probably by the time of Ezra in the fifth century BC. The Prophets followed, and by about 200 BC the writer of Sirach could speak of the Law, the Prophets and the other books. The Writings were the last to be settled. The Jewish historian Josephus, writing around AD 95, lists twenty-two books as the sacred Scriptures that no one has dared to add to or take from.

An older theory held that the Jewish scholars at a council in Jamnia around AD 90 fixed the canon. Most scholars now doubt it, since the evidence shows that the discussions there were limited, and that the core of the collection was settled well before. The evidence points to a gradual recognition, since these books had already been read as the word of God by the community for centuries. Paul says that the Jews "have been entrusted with the very words of God" (Romans 3:2).

# The Septuagint and the Apocrypha
@ref ROM 3:1-2; 2TI 3:15; HEB 11:35
@conf debated

In the third century BC the large Jewish community of Alexandria, Egypt, spoke Greek and could no longer read Hebrew well, and so Jewish scholars began translating their Scriptures into Greek. The tradition told in a book called the Letter of Aristeas says that seventy-two scholars did it in seventy-two days, and from the round number seventy the translation became known as the Septuagint, often abbreviated LXX. It was the Bible of Greek-speaking Jews and of the first Christians, and the New Testament writers often quote it.

The Septuagint included several books that are not in the Hebrew Bible, written in the last centuries before Christ: Tobit, Judith, Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach (also called Ecclesiasticus), Baruch, and 1 and 2 Maccabees, along with additions to Esther and Daniel. Protestants call these books the Apocrypha, from a Greek word meaning "hidden," and Catholics call them the deuterocanonical books, meaning "second canon."

The churches differ on them. The Catholic Church accepts them as Scripture, and the Council of Trent declared them canonical in 1546. The Eastern Orthodox churches accept them and some others besides, such as 3 Maccabees and an extra psalm numbered 151. The Reformers, following Jerome and the Hebrew canon, held that they are useful for reading but are not a basis for doctrine. Luther placed them in a separate section of his Bible, and the King James Version of 1611 printed them between the testaments. Most Protestant Bibles dropped them in the nineteenth century.

Why the difference? Part of the answer is which Old Testament the early church used. The New Testament never quotes from these books as Scripture, though it sometimes alludes to them, and Hebrews 11:35 about women receiving back their dead has been connected with 2 Maccabees. The books are still valuable for understanding the Jewish world between the testaments, including the Maccabean revolt.

# The Dead Sea Scrolls
@ref ISA 40:8; ISA 53:1-12; PSA 119:89
@conf scholars

In 1947, according to the usual story, a Bedouin shepherd boy named Muhammad edh-Dhib was looking for a lost goat among the cliffs west of the Dead Sea, and threw a stone into a cave. He heard the sound of breaking pottery. Inside were clay jars holding ancient scrolls wrapped in linen. It was the first of what became the greatest manuscript discovery of the twentieth century.

Over the next ten years, eleven caves near the ruins of Qumran yielded fragments of about nine hundred scrolls, written between about 250 BC and AD 68. Scholars think that they were hidden by a Jewish community, probably the Essenes, as the Roman army approached in the first revolt. About a quarter of the scrolls are copies of books of the Hebrew Bible, and every book of the Old Testament is represented except Esther. The rest are other writings, including the rule of the community, commentaries and hymns.

The most famous is the Great Isaiah Scroll, a complete copy of the book of Isaiah about 24 feet long, dated to around 125 BC. Before the discovery, the oldest complete Hebrew manuscripts of the Old Testament were from about AD 930 and 1008, so the scrolls were more than a thousand years older. Many expected to find major differences. What scholars found was that the text of Isaiah in the scroll was remarkably close to the later text, with differences mostly in spelling and minor wording. The scrolls showed that the Old Testament had been copied with great care for a thousand years.

They also showed that some books circulated in more than one form, and that the text in some places matches the Septuagint and not the later Hebrew text, which helps in the study of how the text developed. And they are valuable for understanding Judaism at the time of Jesus. As Isaiah says, "The grass withers and the flowers fall, but the word of our God stands forever."

# How the New Testament was gathered
@ref 2PE 3:15-16; COL 4:16; 1TH 5:27
@conf scholars

The twenty-seven books of the New Testament were written over about fifty years, by different authors, to different readers. Nobody sat down and compiled them at a single moment. The collection grew as the churches shared and recognized the writings that carried apostolic authority.

The process began early. Paul told the Colossians to pass his letter on to Laodicea, and to read the Laodicean letter in turn (Colossians 4:16). By the time Peter wrote his second letter, Paul's letters were being read as Scripture along with "the other Scriptures" (2 Peter 3:16). In the second century, the four Gospels were widely read in the churches, and the bishop Irenaeus, around AD 180, argued that there could be neither more nor fewer than four, as there are four winds and four corners of the earth. A Syrian Christian named Tatian tried to weave the four into one narrative, the Diatessaron, but the churches kept the four distinct.

By the end of the second century, most churches accepted the four Gospels, Acts, thirteen letters of Paul, 1 Peter and 1 John. A document called the Muratorian Fragment, usually dated to the late second century, lists most of the New Testament books. A few were doubted for some time, mainly Hebrews, James, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John, Jude and Revelation. Eusebius, the church historian, around AD 325 sorted writings into those that are accepted, those that are disputed and those that are rejected.

In AD 367 Athanasius, the bishop of Alexandria, wrote an Easter letter that lists exactly our twenty-seven books and no others, the earliest known list to do so. The Councils of Hippo in 393 and Carthage in 397, in North Africa, endorsed the same list. These councils did not create the canon. They recorded what the churches had already come to recognize, and by then the matter was largely settled.

# What made a book Scripture?
@ref JHN 14:26; JHN 16:12-15; EPH 2:19-20; GAL 1:8-9
@conf debated

When the church decided which books belonged in the New Testament, it did not invent a list and then impose it. It recognized books whose authority had been felt in the churches for generations. Historians usually point to three tests that were applied, though not as a formal checklist.

The first was apostolic origin: had the book been written by an apostle or by someone closely connected with an apostle? Mark was accepted because he was Peter's interpreter, and Luke because he was Paul's companion. Books written much later under apostolic names, such as the Gospel of Peter, were rejected, because they came too late and reflected ideas that did not match what the churches had received.

The second was agreement with the faith the church already held, which was called the rule of faith. A writing that taught that Jesus only seemed to have a body, for example, did not match the teaching of the apostles. The third was wide use: had the book been read in the churches generally and found to build them up? A writing used in only one small group, or only by those with a particular teaching, was suspect.

There is a difference of emphasis between traditions. Catholic and Orthodox Christians say that the church, led by the Spirit, determined the canon and has the authority to do so. Protestants say that the books have their authority from God, and the church only recognized what was already there, as a person recognizes a voice. Both agree that the Spirit guided the process, and that the church received the books as the voice of Christ.

Jesus had promised his apostles that the Holy Spirit would remind them of everything he said and guide them into all truth (John 14:26; 16:13). The apostles in turn are described as the foundation of the church (Ephesians 2:20). That is the idea behind the canon: the apostles' testimony, written down, is the lasting foundation.

# The books that did not make it
@ref LUK 1:1-4; 1JN 4:1-3; JUD 1:3
@conf scholars

Many other early Christian writings survive, and some are interesting. Reading them helps us see why the church drew the lines it did.

A first group are the writings of the apostolic fathers, such as the letter of Clement of Rome, the letters of Ignatius, the Didache and the Epistle of Barnabas. These are valuable windows into early Christian life, and some were read in churches for a time, but they were not counted as apostolic Scripture, and they are generally clear that they are not on the level of the apostles. The Shepherd of Hermas, a visionary work from the second century, was popular in some places, but it was not accepted because it came from after the apostles.

Another group are the Gnostic gospels, such as the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Philip and the Gospel of Judas. Most of these were written in the second century or later, long after the eyewitnesses. The Gospel of Thomas, for example, is a collection of 114 sayings attributed to Jesus, without any narrative of his birth, death or resurrection, and some of its sayings reflect Gnostic ideas that the body is to be left behind. Texts of this kind were found in 1945 at Nag Hammadi in Egypt.

A third group are the infancy gospels, which fill in the childhood of Jesus with fanciful stories, such as one in which the child Jesus brings clay birds to life. They are late and legendary.

Reading these writings makes one thing clear: the church was not choosing between competing accounts that were equally early. The four Gospels are the only ones that can be traced to the first century. The other writings are interesting evidence for the beliefs of later groups, but they were never in serious contention. The fact that the four Gospels were already used widely by about AD 150 shows how deeply they were rooted.

# How the text was copied
@ref ISA 30:8; JER 36:1-4; 2TI 4:13
@conf scholars

Before the printing press, every Bible was copied by hand, and the transmission of the text is one of the great stories of the ancient world. The New Testament was first written on papyrus, a paper made from reeds, and soon began to be copied in the form of a codex, a book with pages bound along one edge, which Christians adopted early. Later copies were made on parchment, which is animal skin, and last of all on paper.

The number of surviving manuscripts is far greater than for any other ancient work. For the New Testament there are about 5,800 Greek manuscripts, from tiny fragments to complete Bibles, and more than 10,000 in Latin and thousands more in Syriac, Coptic and other languages. By contrast the works of Homer survive in about 2,000 copies, and most Greek and Roman authors in only a handful.

The earliest fragment of the New Testament is a scrap of papyrus, called P52, kept in the John Rylands Library in Manchester, England. It contains a few verses of John 18, and it is dated to about AD 125 to 150, within a few decades of the writing of the Gospel. The Chester Beatty papyri, from about AD 200, contain much of the Gospels, Acts and Paul's letters. The two great fourth-century Bibles are Codex Vaticanus, in the Vatican library, and Codex Sinaiticus, found by the German scholar Constantin von Tischendorf at St. Catherine's Monastery on Mount Sinai in 1844 and 1859, and now mostly in the British Library.

Scribes in some periods worked under very careful systems, and in others they were less careful. The Jewish scribes who preserved the Old Testament, the Masoretes, counted the letters and words of each book in order to avoid errors. The result is that we have an extraordinarily rich body of evidence, much of it from a short time after the originals were written, which lets scholars reconstruct the text with high confidence.

# Copying errors and what we can trust
@ref MRK 16:9-20; JHN 8:1-11; 1JN 5:7-8
@conf scholars

Because the New Testament was copied by hand for fourteen centuries, there are differences among the manuscripts. Scholars call them variants, and there are hundreds of thousands of them, though the number is less alarming than it sounds. It counts every difference in every manuscript, so one misspelling copied into a thousand manuscripts is counted a thousand times.

Most variants are trivial: differences of spelling, word order, or a skipped or doubled word, or the way a name is written. Many cannot even be translated into English. A small number are meaningful, and fewer still affect the sense of a passage. None changes any major teaching of the Christian faith. A few have received attention, and Bibles generally mention them in footnotes.

The last twelve verses of Mark, 16:9 to 20, are not found in the two oldest manuscripts, Vaticanus and Sinaiticus, and the earliest writers seem not to know them. Most scholars think Mark ended at 16:8, or that the original ending was lost. The story of the woman caught in adultery, John 7:53 to 8:11, is also missing from the oldest manuscripts, and it appears at different places in later ones, though many think it preserves a true memory of Jesus. And a clause in 1 John 5:7 about the three heavenly witnesses, found in the King James Version, is not found in any Greek manuscript before the fourteenth century. It was added to later copies.

The scholars who work with these differences are called textual critics. Their method compares manuscripts, favoring the older and more reliable, and asks which reading best explains the others. The aim is to recover the original wording, and their work has shown that, for nearly every verse, the text is secure. Even a skeptic like the textual scholar Bart Ehrman, who has argued that the changes matter, agrees that most do not. The biggest lesson is that nothing has been hidden: the evidence is open for anyone to see.

# The Old Testament text
@ref DEU 4:2; MAT 5:18; PSA 119:89
@conf scholars

The Hebrew Old Testament has its own history of transmission. For the first thousand years it was copied by scribes, and the text was written only with consonants, since ancient Hebrew had no letters for vowels. Readers knew from memory how the words were pronounced. As Hebrew ceased to be a spoken language, there was a risk that the pronunciation would be lost.

Between about AD 500 and 1000, a group of Jewish scholars called the Masoretes, from a word meaning "tradition," added a system of dots and dashes to the text to mark the vowels, and notes in the margins. They counted verses, words and letters of each book, recorded which was the middle verse and the middle word, and made lists of unusual spellings. These were checks against errors in copying. They also preserved a standard text, which is now called the Masoretic Text.

The oldest complete Hebrew Bible is the Leningrad Codex, dated to AD 1008, now in St. Petersburg, and most modern translations are based on it. The Aleppo Codex, about AD 930, was once complete, but part of it was lost in a riot in 1947. Before the discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls, these were the earliest manuscripts we had.

Scholars also compare other ancient witnesses. The Septuagint is the Greek translation from the third century BC and later. The Samaritan Pentateuch is the version of the Law kept by the Samaritan community. The Latin Vulgate and Syriac Peshitta are early translations. Where the Masoretic Text seems to have a problem, these can sometimes help. Translators usually follow the Masoretic Text and refer to the others in footnotes.

The result is a text that, across a very long period, has been kept with great care. Jesus said that not the smallest letter or stroke of the Law would disappear (Matthew 5:18). The Dead Sea Scrolls, which were a thousand years older than the Masoretic manuscripts, bear out how faithfully it was transmitted.

# The Bible in English
@ref NEH 8:8; PSA 119:130; 2TI 3:15-17
@conf scholars

The story of the English Bible is a story of translators who risked their lives to put Scripture into the language of ordinary people. Parts of the Bible were put into Old English as early as the seventh and eighth centuries, but the first complete English Bible was the work of John Wycliffe and his followers around 1382, translated from the Latin Vulgate and copied by hand. The church banned it.

William Tyndale, working from the Hebrew and the Greek, printed his New Testament in 1526 and was executed ten years later. Much of what he wrote survives in later versions. Miles Coverdale published the first complete printed English Bible in 1535. The Geneva Bible of 1560, produced by English exiles in Switzerland, was the first English Bible with verse numbers throughout and study notes, and it was the Bible of Shakespeare and the Pilgrims. The King James Version of 1611 became the standard for three centuries.

The twentieth century brought a wave of new translations as older manuscripts were found and English changed. The Revised Version appeared in 1885 and the American Standard Version in 1901, followed by the Revised Standard Version in 1952, the New American Standard Bible in 1971, the New International Version in 1978, the English Standard Version in 2001, and many others. The New King James Version of 1982 updated the language of the King James and kept its textual basis.

The Berean Standard Bible, which this app uses, is a modern, fairly literal translation made by the Berean Bible translation team. Its publishers dedicated it to the public domain on April 30, 2023, which is the reason it can be freely included here.

Today, the Bible has been translated, in whole or in part, into more than three thousand languages, thanks to translators who work, as Tyndale did, on the belief that everyone should be able to read the Scriptures in their own tongue.

# Choosing a translation
@ref NEH 8:8; ACT 2:6-8; 1CO 14:9
@conf scholars

With dozens of English translations, a new reader may feel lost. Translators face a real problem, which is that no two languages match one to one, and the choices can be arranged along a spectrum.

At one end are word-for-word translations, also called formal equivalence, such as the New American Standard Bible, the English Standard Version, the New King James Version and the Berean Standard Bible. They stay as close as possible to the structure of the Hebrew and Greek, and they are useful for careful study, but they can sound stiff and sometimes confusing in English. In the middle are thought-for-thought translations, which aim for the meaning in natural English. The New International Version and the Christian Standard Bible fall in this area. Further along are dynamic or free translations, such as the New Living Translation and the Good News Bible, which prefer readability to precision.

At the far end are paraphrases, such as The Message and The Living Bible. These are not translations in the strict sense. They restate the text in the paraphraser's own words, and they can be fresh and moving, but they are not good for close study.

There is no single best translation. Each has strengths. A good practice is to use a formal translation for study and a more readable one for reading in large portions, and to compare more than one when a passage is hard. Where translations differ, it is a clue that the original is difficult or has more than one sense, and a footnote may tell you.

Some people hold strongly that one translation is the only right one, often the King James Version. That is not a position the church has held through its history, and it was not the stance of the translators themselves, who wrote in their preface that even the meanest translation contains the word of God. The best translation is the one you will read.

# Chapters and verses
@ref PSA 119:1-176; 2TI 3:16-17; NEH 8:8

The Bible's authors did not write in chapters and verses. Those are later conveniences, added to help readers find their place, and it is worth remembering when a chapter break seems to interrupt an argument.

The chapter divisions were made around AD 1227 by Stephen Langton, a professor at Paris and later Archbishop of Canterbury, who divided the Latin Vulgate into the chapters we use today. They were soon added to Hebrew manuscripts of the Old Testament, and later to Greek ones. They are mostly sensible, though sometimes they cut in unfortunate places. The story of Abraham's offering of Isaac, for instance, runs without a break, while the argument of Romans 8 begins partway through a thought and the story of Isaac's blessing falls across a chapter division.

The verse numbers came later. In about 1440 a Jewish scholar, Rabbi Isaac Nathan ben Kalonymus, devised verse numbers for the Hebrew Old Testament in his concordance. Robert Estienne, a French printer also called Stephanus, then numbered the verses of the Greek New Testament in his edition of 1551, reportedly while traveling on horseback, and of the whole Bible in 1555. The first English Bible to use them was the Geneva New Testament of 1557.

Neither division was inspired, and a modern reader should use them with care. They are useful for reference, as when we say John 3:16, but they sometimes break a thought or run two thoughts together. Some modern editions print the text in paragraphs and put the verse numbers in the margin or in small type, which helps reading. A good habit is to read a paragraph or a whole chapter at a time and to look at what comes before and after.

The longest chapter is Psalm 119, with 176 verses, and the shortest is Psalm 117, with two. There are 594 chapters before Psalm 117 and 594 after it, so it is the middle chapter of the Bible, which is a pleasing fact, but not an important one.
