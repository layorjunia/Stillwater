# What the spiritual disciplines are for
@ref 1TI 4:7-8; ROM 12:1-2; PHP 2:12-13
@conf scholars
@q Which one practice would help you most to notice God's presence in an ordinary week?

Christians have always practiced certain habits meant to open a person to God: prayer, reading Scripture, fasting, rest, worship, giving, confession and service. They are often called the spiritual disciplines. The word does not suggest gloom. A discipline is what a disciple does, and it comes from the same root. Paul uses the picture of athletic training: "Train yourself for godliness" (1 Timothy 4:7). A runner trains in order to run, and a believer practices these habits in order to know God and to become like Christ.

It matters to say what they are not. They are not a way of earning God's favor. The gospel says that we are accepted in Christ, and that nothing we do adds to that. They are not a test of how spiritual we are. Jesus warned against practicing them to be seen by others (Matthew 6). And they are not ends in themselves. Fasting, prayer or silence will not help a person who treats them as achievements. They are means of grace, which is to say, they put us in the path where God has promised to meet us, like a farmer who ploughs and plants but cannot make the crop grow.

The best-known modern book on the subject, Richard Foster's Celebration of Discipline, published in 1978, divided them into inward disciplines such as meditation, prayer, fasting and study; outward ones, such as simplicity, solitude, submission and service; and corporate ones, such as confession, worship, guidance and celebration. Other traditions divide them differently, but the underlying list has been much the same for two thousand years.

Paul's phrasing in Philippians holds the balance: we "work out" our salvation, for "it is God who works in" us. Effort and grace go together. The disciplines are our part in a relationship in which God has done the first and larger part.

# How to pray
@ref MAT 6:5-13; LUK 11:1-4; PHP 4:6-7; 1TH 5:17
@q What is one thing you have been anxious about that you could put into words before God today?

The disciples saw Jesus pray, and one of them asked him, "Lord, teach us to pray." Prayer was central to his life. He went out early in the morning, "while it was still dark," to a solitary place (Mark 1:35). He spent whole nights in prayer before big decisions, and he prayed in the garden before his arrest. If anyone could have managed without it, he could, and he did not.

The Bible describes prayer in many ways. It is a conversation with a Father who knows what we need before we ask, and who still wants us to ask. Paul puts together the main parts: "Be anxious for nothing, but in everything, by prayer and petition, with thanksgiving, present your requests to God. And the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:6-7). That mentions asking, thanking and the peace that comes as a result.

Many Christians remember the main elements by a simple scheme: adoration, confession, thanksgiving and supplication, in that order. We begin by looking at who God is, we admit what we have done wrong, we thank him for what he has given, and then we bring our needs and those of others. It is not a formula, and the Psalms follow no fixed order, but it is a helpful safeguard against a prayer that is only a shopping list.

Jesus gave several instructions. Pray privately, not for show: "go into your inner room, shut your door, and pray to your Father, who is unseen." Do not pile up empty words. Pray persistently. He told a parable about "their need to pray at all times and not lose heart" (Luke 18:1). And forgive those who have wronged you. Paul says, "Pray without ceasing" (1 Thessalonians 5:17), which suggests not a constant recitation but a settled habit of turning to God throughout the day. The simplest way to learn to pray is to start.

# Praying with the Psalms
@ref PSA 62:8; PSA 22:1-5; PSA 34:18; COL 3:16
@q Which psalm puts into words something you have felt but never said to God?

The book of Psalms has served as the prayer book of God's people for about three thousand years. Jesus prayed the psalms. On the cross he cried out the opening words of Psalm 22, "My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?" The early church sang them, and monks worked their way through all 150 in a week. Dietrich Bonhoeffer, writing in 1940, called the psalter "the prayer book of the Bible."

Athanasius, the bishop of Alexandria, wrote a letter around AD 360 to a friend named Marcellinus on how to read the psalms. He made the point that most of Scripture speaks to us, but the psalms speak for us. They put into words things that we feel and cannot express: joy, anger, shame, longing, fear and thanks. He said each person could find his own condition in them, as if looking in a mirror.

That range is part of their value. The psalms include praise, thanksgiving and trust, but a third of them are laments, prayers full of complaint, doubt and grief. "Pour out your hearts before Him. God is our refuge" (Psalm 62:8). They teach that it is permitted to bring any honest feeling to God. And most laments move in the same direction: they begin in pain, and end, often after a pause, in trust.

A way to pray a psalm is to read it slowly, perhaps aloud, and pause where a phrase catches. Make the words your own. Where the psalm expresses something that is not true of you today, pray it on behalf of someone for whom it is. Where a psalm speaks of an enemy, remember that the New Testament says our real enemies are not people. Paul told the Colossians to let the word of Christ dwell richly among them, as they taught one another "with all wisdom," and sang psalms, hymns and spiritual songs.

# Fasting
@ref MAT 6:16-18; ISA 58:6-7; ACT 13:2-3; JOL 2:12
@conf scholars

Fasting is going without food, or without something else, for a spiritual purpose. It is one of the oldest of the disciplines, and appears all through the Bible. Israel fasted once a year on the Day of Atonement, and also in times of national mourning or crisis. Moses fasted forty days on Sinai, Elijah forty days on the way to Horeb, and Jesus forty days in the wilderness. Esther asked her people to fast before she went to the king. In Acts, the church at Antioch was fasting and worshiping when the Spirit told them to set apart Barnabas and Saul.

Jesus does not say if you fast, but "When you fast," and he gives instructions on how: "do not be somber like the hypocrites, for they disfigure their faces to show men they are fasting," and "anoint your head and wash your face, so that your fasting will not be obvious to men, but only to your Father, who is unseen" (Matthew 6:16-18). The point is that fasting is a private matter between a person and God.

The prophet Isaiah gives a warning that is as relevant now as then. People were fasting and complaining that God did not notice, while they oppressed their workers and quarreled. God answered with a question: "Isn't this the fast that I have chosen: to break the chains of wickedness, to untie the cords of the yoke, to set the oppressed free and tear off every yoke? Isn't it to share your bread with the hungry" (Isaiah 58:6-7). A fast that does not lead to justice and mercy has missed the point.

What does fasting do? It reminds the body that we depend on God, loosens the hold of appetite, makes room for prayer, and expresses grief or longing too deep for words. It is not a way of twisting God's arm or a diet. Some people cannot fast from food for medical reasons and can fast from other things, such as screens or spending. The early church fasted on Wednesdays and Fridays, according to the Didache, and many traditions still keep Lent, the forty days before Easter.

# Sabbath: rest as a gift
@ref EXO 20:8-11; DEU 5:12-15; MRK 2:23-28; HEB 4:9-10
@conf debated
@q What would it look like to stop for one day a week, and what would you fear might fall apart?

The Sabbath is one of the Ten Commandments, and the Bible gives two reasons for keeping it. In Exodus, it is rooted in creation: God worked six days and rested on the seventh, and so his people are to do the same (Exodus 20:8-11). In Deuteronomy it is rooted in the exodus: "Remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and that the LORD your God brought you out of there with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm. That is why the LORD your God has commanded you to keep the Sabbath day" (Deuteronomy 5:15). A day off is a declaration of freedom. Slaves work every day, and a free people can stop.

The command was radical in its time. Ancient societies had no weekly rest, and the idea that servants, foreigners and even animals should also rest was striking. It said that human worth does not depend on productivity. It also trained trust: those who stop work for a day are acknowledging that the world will go on without them, and that God provides.

By the time of Jesus, the rules for keeping it had multiplied, and the Pharisees argued over what counted as work. Jesus often healed on the Sabbath, which led to conflict, and he said, "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27). He did not abolish it. He restored its purpose as a gift of rest and a day for doing good.

Christians differ on how it applies today. Seventh-day Adventists and some others keep Saturday. Many Christians have understood Sunday, the day of the resurrection, as the Lord's Day and as a day of worship and rest, and some, like the Puritans, held it to be the Sabbath transferred. Others, based on passages like Colossians 2:16 and Hebrews 4, hold that the Sabbath is fulfilled in Christ, who is our rest, and that the principle of regular rest remains wise. What most agree on is that a pattern of rest and worship is good for body and soul.

# Meditation and Scripture memory
@ref PSA 119:11; PSA 1:1-3; JOS 1:8; COL 3:16
@conf scholars

Meditation in the Bible does not mean emptying the mind. It means filling it. The Hebrew word, hagah, can mean to murmur or mutter, and it describes someone turning a text over, repeating it softly and thinking about it. The first psalm says that the blessed man's "delight is in the Law of the LORD, and on His law he meditates day and night," and that he is like a tree planted by streams of water (Psalm 1:2-3). God told Joshua that the book of the law should not depart from his mouth, and that he was to meditate on it day and night.

One way it has been practiced for centuries is called lectio divina, Latin for "divine reading." In the twelfth century a monk named Guigo II described four steps: lectio, reading the text slowly; meditatio, thinking about its meaning; oratio, responding in prayer; and contemplatio, resting in God's presence. The aim is not to get through as much as possible, but to let a short passage sink in.

Memorizing Scripture has the same purpose. The psalmist says, "I have hidden Your word in my heart that I might not sin against You" (Psalm 119:11). A verse we know by heart is available at any hour, in the car or in the middle of the night, and it can correct a wrong thought. Jesus himself answered the devil in the wilderness with verses that he knew from Deuteronomy.

To start, choose a short passage, such as a psalm or a paragraph from one of the Gospels, and read it slowly several times. Notice a word or phrase that stands out. Ask what it says about God, and what it asks of you. Write it down, and carry it with you. Ten minutes of attention to a few verses can do more than an hour of skimming.

# Confession and forgiveness
@ref 1JN 1:8-9; JAS 5:16; PSA 32:1-5; PRO 28:13
@conf scholars

Confession means agreeing with God about our sin. The word in the New Testament, homologeo, literally means to say the same thing, to agree. It is one of the oldest and most freeing practices of the faith, and also one of the most avoided. We prefer to hide, as Adam and Eve did.

The Bible is clear on the basic point. "If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us." But "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:8-9). It is the nature of God that makes it safe. He is faithful, because he has promised, and just, because Christ has paid. Confession is not an attempt to persuade a reluctant judge, but an agreement with a forgiving one.

Psalm 32 describes what happens when a person does not confess: "When I kept silent, my bones became brittle from my groaning all day long." And what happens when he does: "I acknowledged my sin to You and did not hide my iniquity," and "You forgave the guilt of my sin." Silence eats away at us, and confession brings relief.

The Bible also speaks of confessing to one another. James says, "Therefore confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed" (James 5:16). Traditions have differed on how to do this. Catholic and Orthodox Christians confess to a priest, who pronounces forgiveness in Christ's name. Protestants usually confess directly to God, and many also practice it in small groups or with a trusted friend or pastor. All agree that sin thrives in secrecy, and that being known by another is part of being healed.

A simple practice is to end each day by asking God to show what needs to be confessed, naming it specifically, and receiving his forgiveness with thanks.

# Money, generosity and trust
@ref MAT 6:19-24; 2CO 9:6-8; 1TI 6:6-10; 1TI 6:17-19
@conf debated

Jesus spoke about money often, and not because he was fundraising. He knew that few things compete as strongly for a person's loyalty. "No one can serve two masters," he said, and "You cannot serve both God and money" (Matthew 6:24). He warned that "where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matthew 6:21), and the order is worth noticing: the heart follows the treasure. If we want to know what we love, we can look at where our money goes.

The Bible does not say that wealth is sinful or poverty holy. Abraham, Job and Solomon were very rich, and the Old Testament describes wealth as a blessing. But it hedges it with warnings. It condemns getting rich by exploiting others, and it condemns hoarding. Paul's advice to Timothy is balanced: those who are rich should not be arrogant or trust in wealth, "but in God, who richly provides all things for us to enjoy," and should "be generous and ready to share" (1 Timothy 6:17-18). He adds that "the love of money is the root of all kinds of evil," and that contentment with food and clothing is great gain.

On giving, the Old Testament established the tithe, a tenth of one's income. Christians differ on whether it is still a standard. Some teach that the tithe is the minimum, and others that the New Testament sets a different pattern of generosity that is not bound to a percentage. Paul writes to the Corinthians: "Each one should give what he has decided in his heart to give, not out of regret or compulsion. For God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Corinthians 9:7). The early church shared with those in need, and this was one of its marks.

What the traditions agree on is that giving is a form of worship and a way to loosen the grip that possessions have on us. A good habit is to give regularly and planned, and not just when it is convenient, and to give first to the poor, to the church and to the work of the gospel.

# Work as calling
@ref GEN 2:15; COL 3:22-24; 1TH 4:11-12; PRO 14:23
@conf scholars

In ordinary speech, a calling is a religious vocation, such as ministry. Much of the history of the church has treated priests and monks as having the real spiritual work, while farmers, merchants and craftspeople did something less holy. The Reformers pushed back. Martin Luther taught that every honest occupation is a calling from God, and that a farmer, a mother or a magistrate who does the work faithfully serves God as truly as a priest at the altar. That idea, called the doctrine of vocation, has shaped Protestant thinking since.

The Bible gives support for it. Before the fall, God put Adam in the garden "to cultivate and keep it" (Genesis 2:15). Work is part of paradise and not a punishment, though after the fall it became hard, with thorns and sweat. Jesus spent most of his life as a carpenter. Paul, the great apostle, supported himself by making tents. In his letters he tells the Thessalonians "to work with your own hands," so that they would be respected by outsiders and not be dependent on anyone (1 Thessalonians 4:11-12).

Paul's advice to slaves in the Roman world can be applied to any worker: "Whatever you do, work at it with your whole being, as for the Lord and not for men" (Colossians 3:23). It changes the question of who we work for. The boss, the customer and the paycheck are real, but the ultimate audience is the Lord. This gives dignity to work that no one notices, and gives a reason for excellence and honesty.

Work serves in several ways. It meets needs, ours and others. It develops the earth's potential, in keeping with the first command to rule and care for creation. It gives the means to give. And it is a place where Christians can show faith in action. The Bible also warns against idleness, and against treating work as an idol. The Sabbath is a counterbalance: we work, and we also rest and remember that we are not what we produce.

# Marriage and family
@ref GEN 2:18-24; MAT 19:3-9; EPH 5:21-33; 1CO 7:1-16
@conf debated

The Bible begins and ends with a wedding. In Genesis, God makes the woman as a partner for the man, and the text says, "a man will leave his father and mother and be united to his wife, and they will become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24). In Revelation the church is pictured as the bride of Christ at the marriage supper of the Lamb. Between these, the Bible uses the marriage relationship as a picture of the covenant between God and his people.

Jesus quoted Genesis when asked about divorce: "Therefore what God has joined together, let man not separate" (Matthew 19:6). He taught that marriage was intended from the beginning as a lasting union. Paul writes at length in Ephesians 5 and says that marriage reflects the relationship between Christ and the church: "Husbands, love your wives, just as Christ loved the church and gave Himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25). The passage begins with a sentence that is often overlooked: "Submit to one another out of reverence for Christ" (Ephesians 5:21).

Christians agree that marriage is a covenant, that it is for faithful love, that children are a gift, and that the family is a place to learn to love. They disagree about some details. Some hold that the Bible assigns distinct roles to husbands and wives, with the husband as the leader, and call this complementarian. Others hold that the Bible teaches an equal partnership in which the roles are not fixed by gender, and call this egalitarian. Both appeal to Ephesians 5 and to the example of Christ. There is also disagreement about divorce and remarriage, with some traditions allowing them in cases such as adultery or desertion, and others not.

The Bible also honors singleness. Jesus was single, and Paul was single when he wrote, and Paul sees a particular gift in it (1 Corinthians 7). The church is called a family, and no one need be alone. Whatever the circumstances, the call is the same: to love as Christ has loved.

# Suffering
@ref ROM 5:3-5; 2CO 1:3-4; PSA 34:18; JAS 1:2-4
@conf scholars
@q Where have you seen suffering do something good in a life, without making the suffering itself good?

The Bible does not hide from suffering. Job, the Psalms, Lamentations and the cross are all about it. It does not offer a theory that explains every pain, and it does not tell us to pretend that things are fine. It gives something more useful: a God who is present in it.

First, it permits honest grief. A third of the Psalms are laments. Jesus wept at the grave of his friend and cried out from the cross, "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" Faith does not require us to be calm. "The LORD is near to the brokenhearted; He saves the contrite in spirit" (Psalm 34:18).

Second, it says that God shares in it. The Son of God took on a human body, was hungry and exhausted, and was betrayed, tortured and killed. We do not have a God who watches from a distance. Hebrews says that we have a high priest who sympathizes with our weakness.

Third, it says that suffering can be used. Paul writes that "we also rejoice in our sufferings, because we know that suffering produces perseverance; perseverance, character; and character, hope" (Romans 5:3-4). It does not say that suffering is good, but that God can bring good from it, and that hope does not disappoint us, "because God has poured out His love into our hearts." He also says that God comforts us in all our troubles "so that we can comfort those in any trouble with the comfort we ourselves have received" (2 Corinthians 1:4). People who have suffered are often best able to help others who suffer.

Fourth, it points to an end. The Bible promises a day when God "will wipe away every tear," and death and pain will be no more. The time is not yet, and the Bible never says that the wait is easy. But it says that the story ends well, and that those who are in Christ will not suffer in vain.

# Forgiving others
@ref MAT 18:21-35; EPH 4:31-32; COL 3:12-13; LUK 23:34
@q Is there a person you have been unwilling to forgive, and what exactly stands in the way?

Forgiveness is at the center of the Christian faith, and it is among the hardest things the faith asks. Jesus makes it a condition in the prayer he taught: "forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors." And Paul gives the reason: "Be kind and tenderhearted to one another, forgiving each other just as in Christ God forgave you" (Ephesians 4:32).

Peter once asked Jesus how often he should forgive a brother, and suggested a generous seven times. Jesus replied, "I tell you, not just seven times, but seventy-seven times!" (Matthew 18:22). Then he told a story of a servant who owed his king ten thousand talents, an impossibly large sum, perhaps what a laborer would earn in two hundred thousand years. The king canceled the debt. The servant went out and seized a fellow servant who owed him a hundred denarii, about a hundred days' wages, and had him thrown in prison. The king summoned him and said, "Shouldn't you have had mercy on your fellow servant, just as I had on you?" The point is that what we have been forgiven is far greater than anything we are asked to forgive.

It helps to say what forgiveness is and what it is not. It is a decision to release the other person from a debt, to give up the right to revenge, and to wish them well. It is not pretending that nothing happened or that it did not matter. It is not the same as trust, which must be rebuilt, and it does not mean there are no consequences or that a person should stay in danger. Forgiveness can be given even when the other person is not sorry, as Jesus did on the cross: "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34). Reconciliation, by contrast, takes two people.

Forgiveness is often a process and not a single moment, and a person may need to forgive again as feelings return. It helps to remember the cross, and to ask God for the strength. We forgive because we have been forgiven.

# Truthfulness and the tongue
@ref EPH 4:25; JAS 3:1-12; PRO 18:21; MAT 5:33-37
@conf scholars

The Bible takes speech very seriously. God made the world by speaking, and people made in his image have the power to build up and to destroy with their words. "Life and death are in the power of the tongue," says Proverbs (18:21), and James gives the longest treatment: "the tongue is a small part of the body, but it boasts of great things. Consider how small a spark sets a great forest ablaze" (James 3:5).

Several commands recur. Tell the truth. Paul says, "each of you must put off falsehood and speak truthfully to his neighbor, for we are all members of one another" (Ephesians 4:25). The reason is that lying damages the body, because trust is the foundation of any community. Jesus said that his followers' word should be reliable enough that no oaths are needed: "Simply let your 'Yes' be 'Yes,' and your 'No,' 'No'" (Matthew 5:37).

Do not slander or gossip. The Bible lists them among the serious sins and warns about speech that tears down. Do not flatter. Do not boast. Control your anger. A gentle answer, says Proverbs, turns away wrath. And speak in ways that give grace, so that your words build others up.

James observes that no human being can tame the tongue, and so a person needs help. The remedy begins in the heart, since "out of the overflow of the heart the mouth speaks," as Jesus said. This means that working on our speech is partly working on who we are. It also means that the Spirit, who changes hearts, changes what comes out of the mouth.

The practical test is simple, and it is borrowed from Paul. Before speaking, ask whether the words are true, whether they are kind, and whether they are needed: whether they will build up and give grace to those who hear.

# Hospitality and the poor
@ref LEV 19:9-10; MAT 25:31-46; ACT 2:44-47; 1JN 3:16-18
@conf scholars

The Bible's concern for the poor, the stranger, the widow and the orphan runs from Moses through the prophets to Jesus and the apostles, and the early church was known for acting on it. It is one of the places where faith and practice most clearly meet.

The law of Moses built care into the economy. Farmers were not to reap to the very edges of their fields or strip their vineyards, but to leave the gleanings for the poor and the foreigner (Leviticus 19:9-10). Every seventh year debts were to be forgiven, and the land lay fallow so that the poor could eat what grew. The prophets thundered against those who ignored these provisions. Isaiah and Amos made justice for the poor a test of genuine worship.

Jesus placed himself in solidarity with the needy. In his picture of the final judgment, the king says to the righteous, "I was hungry and you gave Me something to eat, I was thirsty and you gave Me something to drink, I was a stranger and you took Me in" (Matthew 25:35). When they ask when they did that, he says, "whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers of Mine, you did for Me." John asks the sharp question: "If anyone with earthly possessions sees his brother in need, but withholds his compassion from him, how can the love of God abide in him?" (1 John 3:17).

The first Christians acted on it. Those in Jerusalem shared their goods with anyone in need, and Paul organized a collection for the poor in Jerusalem from the churches he planted. In the following centuries the church cared for widows, orphans, prisoners and the sick, even in plagues, and set up the first hospitals. The emperor Julian, who hated Christianity, complained around AD 362 that the Galileans supported not only their own poor but ours as well.

Hospitality, in the Bible, means welcoming strangers, and Hebrews says that some have entertained angels without knowing it. What the church does is a test of what it believes.

# Christians and the government
@ref ROM 13:1-7; ACT 5:27-29; 1PE 2:13-17; REV 13:1-10
@conf debated

How should a Christian relate to the state? Scripture gives two answers that seem to point in different directions, and the history of the church shows Christians weighing them in different times.

Paul writes in Romans 13: "Everyone must submit himself to the governing authorities, for there is no authority except that which is from God. The authorities that exist have been appointed by God" (Romans 13:1). He goes on to say that rulers are God's servants to promote good and punish wrong, and that Christians should pay their taxes. Peter says something similar: "Submit yourselves for the Lord's sake to every human institution." Both were writing under the Roman empire, which was no friend to the church, and both say that government is part of God's provision for order.

But the Bible also describes the state as capable of becoming an enemy. When the Jewish council told the apostles to stop preaching about Jesus, Peter answered, "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). Daniel refused the king's decree against prayer. And in Revelation 13 the Roman empire appears as a beast that demands worship. The principle is that the state has real authority, but not ultimate authority.

Christians have drawn different conclusions in different settings. Some, in the tradition of Augustine and Luther, have emphasized the duty of obedience and the responsibility of rulers to maintain justice. Anabaptists have stressed separation from the state and nonviolence. Reformed thinkers developed a theory that lesser magistrates could resist tyranny. Others, like Bonhoeffer, concluded that a government could become so evil that resistance was a duty. Some have been involved in politics, and others have not.

What most agree on is a set of principles: respect for lawful authority, prayer for those in power (1 Timothy 2:1-2), obedience except where it requires disobeying God, concern for justice, and a loyalty to Christ that stands above any nation. The kingdom of God is not identical with any earthly government.
