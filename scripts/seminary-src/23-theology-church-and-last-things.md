# What the church is
@ref MAT 16:18; ACT 2:42-47; 1PE 2:9-10; EPH 4:11-16
@q What is one way you depend on other Christians that you would not manage alone?

The English word church translates the Greek ekklesia, which in ordinary use meant an assembly of citizens called together. The Old Testament Greek used it for Israel gathered before God, and the New Testament takes it for the community of people who belong to Jesus. Jesus himself uses the word only twice in the Gospels, and one of those is the promise, "I will build My church, and the gates of Hades will not prevail against it" (Matthew 16:18).

The church is not a building. It is a people. Peter calls it "a chosen people, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for God's own possession, to proclaim the virtues of Him who called you out of darkness into His marvelous light" (1 Peter 2:9). The New Testament gives it many images: a body with Christ as head, a flock with a shepherd, a family, a temple built of living stones, a bride waiting for her husband, an army, a field and a vine. Each of them stresses that belonging to Christ means belonging to others.

Christians distinguish the universal church, which is all believers in every place and time, from the local church, which is a particular gathering in a place. The Nicene Creed confesses that the church is "one, holy, catholic and apostolic": one, because Christ has one body; holy, because it is set apart by God; catholic, which means universal; and apostolic, because it rests on the apostles' teaching. The Reformers added that the marks of a true church are the right preaching of the word and the right administration of the sacraments, and Calvin added discipline.

The early church, according to Acts, "devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and to the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and to prayer" (Acts 2:42). Those four activities, teaching, community, the Lord's Supper and prayer, remain the core of church life. The Bible has no picture of a Christian living in isolation, and Hebrews tells believers not to neglect meeting together.

# Baptism
@ref MAT 28:19; ACT 2:38-41; ROM 6:3-4; COL 2:11-12
@conf debated

Baptism is the rite of washing with water in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, by which a person is welcomed into the Christian community. Jesus commanded it in the Great Commission (Matthew 28:19), and Christians have practiced it from the very first. On Pentecost Peter told the crowd to "Repent and be baptized," and about three thousand were baptized that day (Acts 2:38,41).

The Bible gives several meanings to it. It is a sign of washing and cleansing from sin. It is a picture of union with Christ in his death and resurrection: "We were therefore buried with Him through baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, we too may walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:4). It is a mark of belonging to the people of God, and it is the public sign of faith.

Christians disagree on three questions. The first is who should be baptized. Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Anglican, Presbyterian and many other churches baptize infants, seeing a parallel with circumcision and the household baptisms in Acts, and holding that baptism marks entry into the covenant community. Baptist, Pentecostal and many other churches baptize only those who profess faith, arguing that the New Testament examples show belief preceding baptism. Both have defenders from the early church.

The second is the mode. The Greek word baptizo means to dip or immerse, and many churches immerse. Others pour or sprinkle. An early church manual, the Didache, written perhaps around AD 100, allows pouring water three times on the head when there is not enough for immersion. The third question is what baptism does. Some traditions say it conveys regeneration. Others say it is a sign of what God has already done. A small number, such as the Quakers, do not practice water baptism.

Nearly all Christian traditions agree that baptism is not optional and that its meaning is union with Christ, and most recognize each other's baptisms if they were done with water in the name of the Trinity.

# The Lord's Supper
@ref MAT 26:26-28; 1CO 10:16-17; 1CO 11:23-29; JHN 6:53-58
@conf debated

On the night he was betrayed, Jesus took bread and a cup, shared them with his disciples and told them to repeat it in his memory. Paul passes on the account: "The Lord Jesus, on the night He was betrayed, took bread, and when He had given thanks, He broke it and said, 'This is My body, which is for you; do this in remembrance of Me.'" And after supper the cup: "This cup is the new covenant in My blood" (1 Corinthians 11:23-25). The church has kept this meal ever since, and it is called by several names: the Lord's Supper, Communion, the Eucharist (from the Greek for thanksgiving) and the Mass.

The meaning is rich. It looks back, since it is a memorial of the cross. It looks inward, to a participation in Christ, since Paul asks, "Is not the bread that we break a participation in the body of Christ?" It looks around, because "we who are many are one body; for we all partake of the one loaf" (1 Corinthians 10:17). And it looks forward, because Paul says that each time we eat and drink, "you proclaim the Lord's death until He comes."

The disagreement is about how Christ is present. The Catholic Church teaches transubstantiation, that the bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ while their appearances remain, a doctrine given its name in the twelfth century and defined at the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215. The Orthodox churches hold that Christ is truly present, and prefer to call it a mystery. Martin Luther taught that Christ's body and blood are present "in, with and under" the bread and wine, which is sometimes called sacramental union. John Calvin taught that Christ is truly present by the Holy Spirit, who lifts believers to feed on him spiritually. Huldrych Zwingli, and later many Baptists and others, taught that the meal is a memorial and a sign. Luther and Zwingli met at Marburg in 1529 and could not agree on this one point.

Paul warns believers to examine themselves before taking part. Whatever one's view, the meal says that Christians live by Christ's sacrifice and not by their own.

# How churches are led
@ref ACT 14:23; ACT 20:17-28; TIT 1:5-9; 1TI 3:1-13
@conf debated

The New Testament says less about church government than people often expect, and Christians have organized the church in different ways. What it does show is that from the beginning local churches had leaders, and that qualifications of character mattered more than skills.

Two kinds of local office appear. The first are the overseers, also called elders or pastors. In Acts 20 Paul calls together "the elders of the church" at Ephesus and tells them that the Holy Spirit has made them "overseers" and that they are to "be shepherds of the church of God." Overseer (episkopos) and elder (presbyteros) seem to describe the same people from two angles (Titus 1:5-7). The second are deacons, who serve, and Acts 6 describes seven men appointed to care for the widows. The apostles had a unique role as eyewitnesses and founders.

Three main patterns have developed. The episcopal system is led by bishops, who oversee a region and who are held to stand in succession from the apostles. It is found in the Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican and Methodist churches. The presbyterian system is led by elders, with local elders joined in regional courts, and it is found in the Presbyterian and Reformed churches. The congregational system holds that each local church governs itself under Christ, and it is found in Baptist, Congregational and many independent churches.

Each view appeals to the New Testament, and each has strengths. Early evidence is mixed. About AD 110, Ignatius of Antioch wrote of a single bishop in each city, with presbyters and deacons, while Clement of Rome a little earlier wrote as though the leaders in Corinth were the elders. Christians who disagree about government still share the main point: Christ is the head of the church, and its leaders are servants who will give an account.

# Spiritual gifts
@ref ROM 12:4-8; 1CO 12:4-11; 1CO 14:1-5; EPH 4:11-13
@conf debated
@q Which gift do you think you have been given, and who is it meant to serve?

Paul says that each believer is given something by the Holy Spirit to use for the good of the church: "There are different gifts, but the same Spirit," and "to each one the manifestation of the Spirit is given for the common good" (1 Corinthians 12:4,7). Three of his letters contain lists: Romans 12, 1 Corinthians 12 and Ephesians 4. They include teaching, serving, encouraging, giving, leading, showing mercy, administration, wisdom, faith, healing, miracles, prophecy, discernment, tongues and their interpretation. The lists differ, so they appear to be illustrations and not a complete catalog.

Two principles stand out. The gifts are for others: they are given "for the common good" and not for personal display. And they require love. In the middle of the long discussion in 1 Corinthians comes chapter 13, which says that tongues, prophecy and knowledge are worth nothing without love, and chapter 14 then tells the church to pursue love and to seek gifts, "especially the gift of prophecy," which here means speech that builds the church up.

Christians have disagreed about whether all the gifts continue today. Cessationists hold that miraculous gifts such as prophecy, tongues and healing ceased with the apostolic age, once Scripture was complete. Continuationists hold that all the gifts continue and should be practiced with order. The modern Pentecostal movement began around 1901 in Topeka, Kansas, and the revival at Azusa Street in Los Angeles in 1906 spread it worldwide, and in the 1960s a charismatic renewal brought similar practices into older denominations. Today Pentecostals and charismatics number several hundred million. Many Christians fall somewhere between the two poles.

All agree that the church needs every part, as Paul says of the body: eye, hand and foot cannot say they have no need of one another.

# Worship and the Lord's day
@ref ACT 20:7; 1CO 16:2; REV 1:10; HEB 10:24-25; JHN 4:23-24

Worship is the response of a creature to the glory of God, and it is what the church exists to do. Jesus told the woman at the well that the Father is looking for people who "will worship the Father in spirit and in truth" (John 4:23-24), which means that the place matters less than the heart and the truth about God.

The earliest Christians were Jews, who worshiped in the temple and synagogue, and they took over many of its elements: reading Scripture, singing psalms, prayer and teaching. They added the breaking of bread and the confession that Jesus is Lord. By about AD 150, Justin Martyr described a Sunday meeting in which the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets were read, the leader preached, the people prayed, bread and wine were shared, and a collection was taken for the poor. The outline is close to what most churches do today.

Christians met on the first day of the week, the day of the resurrection. Acts says, "On the first day of the week we came together to break bread" (Acts 20:7), and Paul tells the Corinthians to set aside a gift on the first day of each week. John calls it "the Lord's day" (Revelation 1:10). Over time this replaced the Sabbath for most Christians, and in AD 321 the emperor Constantine made Sunday a day of rest in the empire.

Christians differ about whether the fourth commandment, the Sabbath, still applies on Sunday. Some, such as the Seventh-day Adventists, keep Saturday. Many Reformed Christians hold that the Sabbath principle carries over to Sunday. Others see Sunday as a day of worship that does not carry the same rest requirements, and hold that Christ is the believer's rest (Hebrews 4). Churches also differ in style, from the stately liturgy of Orthodox and Anglican services to the freedom of Quaker silence or Pentecostal exuberance. What is common is the center: the word, prayer, praise and the table, gathered around Christ.

# The mission of the church
@ref MAT 28:18-20; ACT 1:8; 2CO 5:18-20; MAT 5:13-16

The church exists for a purpose beyond itself. Jesus said, in his last words to the disciples, "Therefore go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and teaching them to obey all that I have commanded you. And surely I am with you always, even to the end of the age" (Matthew 28:19-20). This is called the Great Commission, and it has driven the church's mission across two thousand years.

The commission has several parts. It is to go, and to make disciples, which means more than getting decisions; a disciple is a follower who learns. It is to baptize, which brings people into the community. And it is to teach obedience, which means that conversion is a start and not an end. The scope is "all nations," so that no group of people is outside its reach. Acts 1:8 draws the map: Jerusalem, Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth.

Paul gives another way of describing it. God "reconciled us to Himself through Christ and gave us the ministry of reconciliation," and so believers are "ambassadors for Christ" (2 Corinthians 5:18-20). Jesus also says that his followers are "the salt of the earth" and "the light of the world" (Matthew 5:13-14), which means that the church serves society by being what it is, and by doing good works that give glory to the Father.

Christians have held that mission includes both word and deed: preaching the gospel and caring for the poor, the sick and the oppressed, as Jesus did. The balance has been argued at different times. Through history, the church has founded hospitals, schools and universities, and it has sent missionaries. Today most Christians live outside Europe and North America. The commission is still unfinished, and it still carries Jesus' promise that he is with them.

# Death and what comes after
@ref LUK 23:42-43; 2CO 5:6-8; PHP 1:21-24; HEB 9:27
@conf debated

What happens to a person at death, before the resurrection and the final judgment? The Bible says less than we might like, and Christians have held a few views. It does give clear promises to believers. Jesus told the thief on the cross, "Truly I tell you, today you will be with Me in Paradise" (Luke 23:43). Paul writes to the Philippians that he is torn between living and dying: "I desire to depart and be with Christ, which is far better indeed" (Philippians 1:23). And to the Corinthians: he would "prefer to be away from the body and at home with the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:8).

The view of most Christians, called the intermediate state, is that the souls of believers go at once into the presence of Christ and wait there for the resurrection of the body. It is a state of joy, but not the final one, since the person is not yet whole. A minority of Christians, including some Adventists, hold that the dead are unconscious until the resurrection, a view often called soul sleep, and they note that Scripture often calls death sleep.

The Catholic Church teaches that those who die in grace but still need to be cleansed from the effects of sin pass through purgatory before entering heaven. Protestants reject that teaching, holding that Christ's work is complete and that the Bible does not teach a place of purification. The Orthodox churches pray for the dead but do not hold a fully developed doctrine of purgatory.

The Bible treats death as an enemy. Paul calls it "the last enemy to be destroyed" (1 Corinthians 15:26), and Hebrews says that people are destined to die once and after that to face judgment. But because Christ has died and risen, believers do not face it alone, and Paul can say, "to live is Christ, and to die is gain." The comfort of the gospel is not that death is nothing, but that it is no longer the last word.

# The return of Christ
@ref ACT 1:9-11; MAT 24:30-36; 1TH 4:13-18; TIT 2:11-14

The New Testament says repeatedly that Jesus will come back. When he ascended, two men in white told the watching disciples, "This same Jesus, who has been taken from you into heaven, will come back in the same way you have seen Him go into heaven" (Acts 1:11). This is the second coming, and the early church lived in expectation of it. The earliest Christian prayer we know was an Aramaic phrase, Maranatha, "Our Lord, come."

The return will be personal and visible. It will not be a spiritual impression or the spread of Christian ideas. Jesus says that "the sign of the Son of Man will appear in heaven" and "all the tribes of the earth will mourn" (Matthew 24:30). Paul describes it for the church at Thessalonica: "the Lord Himself will descend from heaven with a loud command, with the voice of an archangel, and with the trumpet of God, and the dead in Christ will be the first to rise" (1 Thessalonians 4:16).

The timing is not given. Jesus is plain: "No one knows about that day or hour, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father" (Matthew 24:36). Throughout history, many people have predicted a date, and each time they have been wrong, which Jesus' words would predict. He says instead that his people should be watchful and faithful, like servants who do not know when the master will be back.

The return has several purposes. It will complete salvation, for the dead will be raised and the living changed. It will bring judgment, for he will judge the living and the dead. It will defeat all evil, and it will begin the new creation. Paul says that believers await "the blessed hope and glorious appearance of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" (Titus 2:13). The hope is not meant to make Christians idle or fearful. It makes them hopeful and ready.

# The resurrection of the body
@ref 1CO 15:20-26; 1CO 15:35-44; JHN 5:28-29; DAN 12:2

The creeds confess "the resurrection of the body," and the claim is more specific than a general belief in life after death. It says that God will raise the dead and give them new bodies, and that the final hope is not the soul's escape from the body, but the redemption of the whole person.

The Old Testament hints at it. Daniel 12:2 says, "Many who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake, some to everlasting life, but others to shame and everlasting contempt." Job says that after his skin has been destroyed, yet from his flesh he will see God (Job 19:26). The New Testament pushes it into the open with the resurrection of Jesus, which is the pattern and the guarantee. Paul argues in 1 Corinthians 15 that Christ is the firstfruits, and that "in Christ all will be made alive."

What kind of body will it be? Paul compares it to a seed. What is put in the ground is not what comes up. The body is "sown perishable" and "raised imperishable. It is sown in dishonor; it is raised in glory. It is sown in weakness; it is raised in power. It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:42-44). A spiritual body does not mean a ghost. It means a body suited to the Spirit, with continuity and transformation, like the body of the risen Jesus, who could be touched and who also passed through locked doors.

Jesus says that all will be raised: "all who are in their graves will hear His voice and come out, those who have done good to the resurrection of life, and those who have done evil to the resurrection of judgment" (John 5:28-29). The resurrection of believers is the climax of salvation, and it gives dignity to the body now. Christians have always treated the bodies of the dead with respect and have resisted views that despise the body. What is sown in tears will be raised in glory.

# The last judgment
@ref MAT 25:31-46; 2CO 5:10; REV 20:11-15; ROM 2:5-8

The Bible says that history will end with a judgment. This is part of its good news as well as its warning. A world in which evil never faced consequences, in which the powerful oppressed the weak and died in their beds, would not be a just world. The judgment is God's promise to put things right.

The judge is Jesus. Paul says to the Athenians that God has fixed a day on which he will judge the world by a man he appointed, and gave proof by raising him from the dead. Jesus himself says that "When the Son of Man comes in His glory, and all the angels with Him, He will sit on His glorious throne. All the nations will be gathered before Him, and He will separate the people one from another, as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats" (Matthew 25:31-32). The scene in Revelation 20 shows a great white throne and the dead standing before it, with books opened.

The basis is our deeds, as evidence of the heart. "We must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ, that each one may receive his due for the things done in the body, whether good or bad" (2 Corinthians 5:10). Believers are not judged for salvation, since Christ has borne that, but they do give an account of their lives, and Paul says that there will be rewards, and that some work will be burned up (1 Corinthians 3:13-15). Those who are not in Christ are judged by what they have done and by what they have done with what they knew.

The truth of the judgment is meant to produce seriousness and hope. It tells us that nothing is hidden and that every wrong will be addressed, and also that mercy is available now. The same Jesus who will be the judge is the one who offers forgiveness today.

# Heaven
@ref JHN 14:1-3; REV 21:1-5; REV 22:3-5; ISA 65:17-25
@q What do you most look forward to about the new creation, and does it change anything about today?

Popular pictures of heaven often draw more from cartoons than from Scripture, with people floating on clouds and playing harps. The Bible's picture is richer. In its widest sense, heaven is the place where God dwells, and the believer's destination is to be with him. Jesus says, "In My Father’s house are many rooms," and he is going there "to prepare a place for you" (John 14:2). Paul says that to be absent from the body is to be at home with the Lord, and the dying thief was promised Paradise.

But the final hope is not just a spirit-world far away. The last chapters of the Bible say that the new Jerusalem comes down from heaven to a new earth, and that "the dwelling place of God is with man" (Revelation 21:3). The goal is the renewal of creation, and God's coming to live in it. Isaiah's vision of new heavens and a new earth includes houses built and lived in, vineyards planted and eaten from, and wolf and lamb feeding together. Paul says that "the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to decay and brought into the glorious freedom of the children of God" (Romans 8:21).

The best-known details are about what will not be there: "He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and there will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain." And the best is about what will be: "they will see His face." The great blessing is the presence of God, and everything else, such as friendship, rest, work, worship and beauty, flows from it.

The Bible is modest about the rest. We are told that there will be rewards and varied responsibilities, that the nations will bring their glory into the city, and that the saints will reign. The writers do not try to describe everything. They tell us enough to want it and to trust the one who has prepared it.

# Hell
@ref MAT 25:41-46; MRK 9:43-48; 2TH 1:7-9; REV 20:14-15
@conf debated

Of all the Bible's teachings, hell is the hardest to hear, and a faithful account must say that it comes mostly from the lips of Jesus. He speaks of it more than anyone else in the New Testament, using the image of Gehenna, a valley outside Jerusalem associated with fire and refuse, and speaking of fire, darkness and weeping. In the parable of the sheep and goats he says that the wicked "will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life" (Matthew 25:46).

The Bible gives several descriptions: outer darkness, the lake of fire, the second death, the destruction of body and soul, separation from the presence of the Lord. Paul says that those who do not obey the gospel "will suffer the penalty of eternal destruction, separated from the presence of the Lord and the glory of His might" (2 Thessalonians 1:9). It is described as just, and as the outcome of rejecting God's offer, never as the desire of God, who takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked (Ezekiel 33:11).

Christians have held three main views of its nature. The traditional view, held by most of the church through its history, is that hell is a state of conscious, unending punishment. The second, called annihilationism or conditional immortality, holds that the wicked are finally destroyed and cease to exist, and that the "eternal" refers to the result and not to the process. It has had thoughtful defenders among evangelicals, including Edward Fudge, and John Stott described it as a view he held tentatively. The third, universalism, holds that in the end all will be saved. A few early theologians, such as Origen, considered it, and some modern writers hold it, but the main body of the church has rejected it, in part because of the many warnings of Jesus.

Whatever the view, the doctrine is not meant to be held with relish. It makes the seriousness of sin plain, and it makes the cross more costly. And it is the reason Christians tell others the good news.

# The thousand years: three views
@ref REV 20:1-6; ROM 11:25-27; 1CO 15:22-26
@conf debated

Revelation 20 speaks of a period of a thousand years, in which Satan is bound and the saints reign with Christ. The passage has been read in three main ways, and the labels come from how each connects the return of Christ with the thousand years.

Amillennialism takes the thousand years as a symbolic description of the present age, between Christ's first and second coming, during which Satan's power has been limited and believers who have died reign with Christ in heaven. Christ's return will bring the end of history, the resurrection and the final judgment. It was the view of Augustine, and it is the view of the Catholic and Orthodox churches and of many Reformed and Lutheran Christians.

Postmillennialism holds that the gospel will spread and bring a long period of peace and righteousness on the earth, after which Christ will return. It was widely held among Puritans and by Jonathan Edwards, and it was popular in the nineteenth century, but it lost ground after the two world wars of the twentieth century.

Premillennialism holds that Christ will return before a literal thousand years of his reign on earth. Some of the early Christians, such as Papias, Justin Martyr and Irenaeus, held something like this, and it is called historic premillennialism. A later form, called dispensationalism, was developed by John Nelson Darby in the nineteenth century and spread through the Scofield Reference Bible of 1909. It divides history into distinct ages, sees a future role for national Israel, and often teaches a rapture of the church before a period of tribulation.

Within these views Christians also differ about the timing of the tribulation and the rapture, which Paul describes in 1 Thessalonians 4:17 as believers being "caught up together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air." None of this is part of the historic creeds. The creeds confess only that Christ will come again to judge the living and the dead, and his kingdom will have no end.
