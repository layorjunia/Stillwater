# Ezekiel
@id book-ezk
@ref EZK 1:26-28; EZK 36:24-28; EZK 37:1-14

Ezekiel has forty-eight chapters and is the strangest of the major prophets, full of visions, symbolic acts and detailed plans. Its name means "God strengthens." The book is dated with unusual care, by the year of King Jehoiachin's exile, which makes it the easiest of the prophets to place on a timeline.

Ezekiel was a priest who was carried off to Babylon with King Jehoiachin in 597 BC, and he began to prophesy in 593 BC at about the age of thirty, by the Kebar canal. The latest dated passage is from 571 BC. He lived among the exiles, while Jeremiah was warning those who stayed in Jerusalem, and the two messages match.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 3: the vision of the glory of the LORD on a moving throne and Ezekiel's call as a watchman.
- Chapters 4 to 24: judgment on Jerusalem, with symbolic acts and the departure of the glory from the temple.
- Chapters 25 to 32: oracles against the surrounding nations.
- Chapters 33 to 39: news of the fall, then promises of a new shepherd, a new heart, the dry bones and the defeat of Gog.
- Chapters 40 to 48: a vision of a new temple and a river of life, ending with the name of the city: "The LORD is there."

## What to look for
Ezekiel says again and again, "Then they will know that I am the LORD." Judgment and restoration both serve that end. The great promise in chapter 36 is a new heart and God's Spirit within his people, which many readers hear behind Jesus' words to Nicodemus about being born of water and the Spirit. The last chapters, with a river flowing from the temple, feed into the picture of the river of life in Revelation 22.

# Daniel
@id book-dan
@ref DAN 3:16-18; DAN 7:13-14; DAN 9:24-27
@conf scholars

Daniel has twelve chapters and is a book of two halves. The first six tell stories of Daniel and his friends, who stay faithful in the court of a foreign empire. The last six record visions that Daniel received about the future of the world's kingdoms. It is the model for the later Jewish and Christian writings that scholars call apocalyptic.

The book places Daniel in Babylon from 605 BC, and it ends in the reign of Cyrus. Tradition has held that Daniel wrote it in the sixth century BC. Many critical scholars date it to about 165 BC, during the persecution by Antiochus Epiphanes, because of its detailed accounts of the Seleucid kings and because of certain words in the text. Conservative scholars reply that the language and setting fit the sixth century and that God can reveal the future. This is among the most contested questions in Old Testament studies.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: Daniel and three friends are trained in Nebuchadnezzar's court.
- Chapters 2 to 6: the statue dream, the fiery furnace, the king's madness, the writing on the wall and the lions' den. The text from 2:4 to 7:28 is in Aramaic, not Hebrew.
- Chapters 7 to 12: visions of four beasts and the Son of Man, a ram and a goat, the seventy weeks, and the final conflict and resurrection.

## What to look for
The theme is that God rules the kingdoms of the earth. Empires rise and fall, but "His dominion is an everlasting dominion." Notice how the heroes handle pressure with courage and with respect. In chapter 7 One "like the Son of Man" comes before God and receives an everlasting kingdom, a title Jesus uses for himself more than any other. Chapter 12 holds one of the clearest Old Testament promises of resurrection.

# Hosea
@id book-hos
@ref HOS 1:2-3; HOS 3:1-3; HOS 11:1-4

Hosea has fourteen chapters and is the first of the twelve Minor Prophets. His name means "salvation," and it is the same name as Joshua. Hosea is the story of a prophet whose own broken marriage becomes a picture of God's relationship to Israel.

He prophesied in the northern kingdom from about 755 BC until shortly before the fall of Samaria in 722 BC, a time of political chaos in which several kings were assassinated. He was a contemporary of Amos in the north and of Isaiah and Micah in the south. The book is personal and emotional, and the prophet's voice breaks off, turns, and returns.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 3: Hosea marries Gomer, who proves unfaithful. He buys her back. Their children are given names that carry a message.
- Chapters 4 to 14: oracles against Israel's idolatry and injustice, alternating between charge, judgment and tender appeals, and closing with a call to return.

## What to look for
The key Hebrew word is chesed, loyal love, the covenant love of God that does not let go. God compares himself to a husband whose wife has gone, and to a father teaching a child to walk (11:3). Judgment is real, but it is never the last word: "How could I give you up, O Ephraim?" (11:8). Matthew applies Hosea 11:1 to Jesus coming out of Egypt, and Paul quotes the book to show how God calls those who were not his people.

# Joel
@id book-jol
@ref JOL 2:12-13; JOL 2:28-32; ACT 2:16-21
@conf scholars

Joel has three chapters in English Bibles, and it is a short prophecy about a plague of locusts and the day of the LORD. Its name means "the LORD is God." It is memorable for its images of an invading swarm and for a promise that Peter picked up on the day of Pentecost.

Nothing in the book gives a date. Joel mentions no kings, and scholars have suggested dates ranging from the ninth century BC to the fourth. The book was shaped around a real disaster, a locust plague, and it takes that disaster as a warning of a greater day that is coming.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: the locusts devastate the land, and the people are told to mourn.
- Chapter 2:1 to 17: the locusts are a picture of an army, and the prophet calls for a solemn assembly and repentance.
- Chapter 2:18 to 32: God answers with mercy, restores the harvest and promises to pour out his Spirit.
- Chapter 3: judgment on the nations and a vision of Judah's blessing.

## What to look for
Joel announces a God who is "gracious and compassionate, slow to anger, abounding in loving devotion." The call is to tear hearts and not just clothes. The great promise in 2:28 is that God will pour out his Spirit on all people, so that sons and daughters, old and young, will prophesy. Peter says that this is what was happening on the day of Pentecost, and the book closes with the promise that everyone who calls on the name of the LORD will be saved.

# Amos
@id book-amo
@ref AMO 5:21-24; AMO 7:14-15; AMO 9:11-15

Amos has nine chapters and is the book of a farmer who became a prophet. Amos was a shepherd and a grower of sycamore figs from Tekoa, in Judah, who was sent north to Israel, where he spoke in the days of Jeroboam II and Uzziah, around 760 BC. It was a boom time, with prosperity for the wealthy and misery for the poor.

His book is among the earliest prophetic writings. It begins with a sharp rhetorical trap: he announces judgment on Israel's neighbors, so that his listeners will nod along, and then he turns to Israel itself.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 and 2: judgment on Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, Ammon, Moab, Judah, and finally Israel.
- Chapters 3 to 6: three sermons that begin with "Hear this word," on injustice, empty worship and false security.
- Chapters 7 to 9: five visions, including a plumb line and a basket of summer fruit, with the clash between Amos and the priest Amaziah, and a closing promise of restoration.

## What to look for
Amos is the prophet of justice. He denounces those who sell the needy for a pair of sandals and trample the poor, and he says God despises religious festivals that are not matched by right living. His most quoted line is the call to let justice roll down like waters. Yet the book ends with hope: God will rebuild David's fallen shelter, and James quotes the passage at the council in Jerusalem to show that the Gentiles are to be included (Acts 15:16-17).

# Obadiah
@id book-oba
@ref OBA 1:3-4; OBA 1:10-15; OBA 1:21

Obadiah has one chapter of twenty-one verses and is the shortest book in the Old Testament. The name means "servant of the LORD," and there are a dozen men by that name in the Bible, so it is not clear which one wrote this. The book is a single oracle against Edom.

Edom, south of the Dead Sea, was the nation descended from Esau, Jacob's twin brother, and the two peoples had been enemies for centuries. The prophecy seems to be written after the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC, when Edom gloated, helped plunder the city and cut off those trying to escape (verses 10 to 14). Psalm 137:7 shows that Judah remembered this.

## How it is arranged
- Verses 1 to 9: Edom's pride, secure in its rock fortresses, will be brought down.
- Verses 10 to 14: the charge, that Edom stood aside and rejoiced when its brother was in trouble.
- Verses 15 to 21: the day of the LORD for all the nations, and the restoration of Israel, so that "the kingdom will belong to the LORD."

## What to look for
Obadiah teaches that pride and the failure to help a brother in trouble are sins that God judges. The measure is a fair one: "As you have done, it will be done to you." Its closing verse points beyond the local story to the rule of God over all. The book also reminds us how much family feeling runs through the Old Testament, starting with Jacob and Esau in Genesis.

# Jonah
@id book-jon
@ref JON 1:1-3; JON 4:1-2; MAT 12:39-41
@conf debated

Jonah has four chapters and is the story of the reluctant prophet who ran from God's command and then resented God's mercy. It is the only prophetic book that is mostly narrative. Jonah son of Amittai is named in 2 Kings 14:25 as a prophet under Jeroboam II, in the eighth century BC.

Nineveh, the capital of Assyria, was the most feared city of the age. Jonah is told to go there and call it to repentance, and he gets on a ship for Tarshish, in the opposite direction. Christians have read the book in different ways. Most have taken it as a historical account, as Jesus seems to do when he speaks of "the sign of Jonah." Others see it as a parable or a satire, a story that makes a theological point. The book is meant to challenge its readers, whatever its form.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: Jonah flees, a storm strikes, and sailors throw him overboard. A great fish swallows him.
- Chapter 2: Jonah's prayer from inside the fish.
- Chapter 3: Jonah finally goes to Nineveh, and the city repents.
- Chapter 4: Jonah is angry at God's compassion, and God ends the book with a question.

## What to look for
The book is a mirror. The pagan sailors and the people of Nineveh respond quickly, and the prophet, the one who should know better, is the stubborn one. Jonah says why he ran: "I knew that You are a gracious and compassionate God, slow to anger, abounding in loving devotion." He wanted the enemy judged and not forgiven. Jesus uses the three days in the fish as a sign of his resurrection, and says that the men of Nineveh will stand in judgment, because they repented at Jonah's preaching, and "One greater than Jonah is here."

# Micah
@id book-mic
@ref MIC 5:2-5; MIC 6:6-8; MIC 7:18-20

Micah has seven chapters. Its author, from the village of Moresheth in Judah, prophesied in the reigns of Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah, in the later eighth century BC, so he was a contemporary of Isaiah. He spoke to Samaria and Jerusalem, and he was concerned for ordinary people, who were being squeezed by greedy landowners and corrupt leaders.

The name Micah means "who is like the LORD?", and the last chapter closes with a play on it: "Who is a God like You?" The book has the shape of three cycles, each beginning with "hear" and each moving from judgment to hope.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 3: charges against Samaria and Jerusalem, and against rulers who devour the people like meat.
- Chapters 4 and 5: the future kingdom, with nations coming to learn God's ways, and a ruler from Bethlehem.
- Chapters 6 and 7: a courtroom scene, the famous summary of what God requires, a lament and a closing hymn of forgiveness.

## What to look for
The great sentences are at 6:8, "to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God," and at 5:2, which names Bethlehem as the birthplace of the one who will rule Israel. The chief priests and scribes quote it when Herod asks where the Messiah would be born (Matthew 2:5-6). The book closes with a statement of astonishing forgiveness, in which God hurls all sins into the depths of the sea.

# Nahum
@id book-nam
@ref NAM 1:2-3; NAM 1:7; NAM 3:18-19

Nahum has three chapters and is a poem about the fall of Nineveh. Its name means "comfort," and the comfort is for the people whom Assyria had crushed. More than a century after Jonah's preaching, when Nineveh's repentance had faded, Nahum announces its downfall.

The prophecy falls between the fall of the Egyptian city of Thebes to the Assyrians in 663 BC, which it mentions as a past event (3:8), and the fall of Nineveh to the Babylonians and Medes in 612 BC. It would be a mistake to read the book as only an outburst of nationalism. It is a statement that God sees brutality and will not let it stand.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: a hymn to God as a jealous, avenging and yet good God, who is a refuge for those who trust him.
- Chapter 2: a vivid description of the attack on the city.
- Chapter 3: the reasons for judgment, with bloodshed, deceit and plunder, and a closing taunt.

## What to look for
The book balances two truths: "The LORD is slow to anger and great in power; the LORD will by no means leave the guilty unpunished," and "The LORD is good, a stronghold in the day of distress." It is a word for people who suffer under cruel power, and it assures them that cruelty does not last forever. A line about the feet of one who brings good news (Nahum 1:15) is shared with Isaiah 52:7.

# Habakkuk
@id book-hab
@ref HAB 1:2-4; HAB 2:2-4; HAB 3:17-19

Habakkuk has three chapters and is the book of a prophet who argues with God. Unlike most prophets, he is not speaking to the people for God, but to God for the people. The name may come from a word meaning "embrace" or "wrestle," which suits him.

He wrote when Babylon was rising to power, probably about 605 BC, a time when Judah was full of violence and injustice. Habakkuk asks how long God will tolerate it, and God says he is raising up the Babylonians. That answer is worse than the problem, and Habakkuk objects again.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: the first complaint, God's answer that Babylon is coming, and the second complaint.
- Chapters 2:1 to 20: Habakkuk waits at his post, and God answers with a vision and five woes against the proud.
- Chapter 3: a prayer in the form of a psalm, recalling God's past rescues, and ending in a song of trust.

## What to look for
The center is a single sentence, "the righteous will live by faith" (2:4). It means that those who are right with God keep living by faithfulness, trusting him even when things seem to go wrong. Paul quotes it in Romans and Galatians, and Hebrews quotes it too, to mean that justification is by faith. The book ends with Habakkuk saying that even if everything fails, he will rejoice in the LORD, who is the God of his salvation.

# Zephaniah
@id book-zep
@ref ZEP 1:14-18; ZEP 2:3; ZEP 3:14-17

Zephaniah has three chapters and is a short, sharp prophecy about the day of the LORD. The prophet's name means "the LORD hides" or "treasures," and he traces his own family back four generations to a Hezekiah, perhaps the king, which would make him a royal descendant.

He prophesied in the days of Josiah, between about 640 and 609 BC, before or during Josiah's reforms. The mood is one of fear: Judah has lived through the long, wicked reign of Manasseh, and judgment seems close. The book was probably given early in Josiah's reign, before the great reform of 621 BC.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: the day of the LORD, which will sweep away idolatry and the complacent.
- Chapter 2: a call to seek the LORD, and judgment on Philistia, Moab, Ammon, Cush and Assyria.
- Chapter 3: judgment on Jerusalem, followed by a promise of a purified remnant and a song of rejoicing.

## What to look for
The day of the LORD is a day of darkness, but those who seek him may be hidden. At the end comes one of the most surprising verses in the prophets: the LORD is in your midst, "He will rejoice over you with gladness; He will quiet you with His love; He will rejoice over you with singing." God is not only the judge. He is the one who rejoices over his people and sings.

# Haggai
@id book-hag
@ref HAG 1:2-8; HAG 2:3-9; HAG 2:23

Haggai has two chapters and is the second shortest book in the Old Testament after Obadiah, though it is one of the best dated. Its four messages are each dated to the day, between August and December of 520 BC, in the second year of the Persian king Darius.

The returned exiles had begun to rebuild the temple in 536 BC, then stopped, discouraged by opposition and by their own concerns. Sixteen years later they were living in comfortable houses while the house of God lay in ruins. Haggai and his younger contemporary Zechariah told them to start again. The people obeyed, and the work was completed in 516 BC.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: "Consider your ways." The people are told to rebuild, and they respond within weeks.
- Chapter 2:1 to 9: the new temple seems small, but God promises that its glory will be greater.
- Chapter 2:10 to 19: a question about holiness and defilement, and a promise of blessing from that day.
- Chapter 2:20 to 23: a message to Zerubbabel, the descendant of David, as God's chosen servant.

## What to look for
Haggai is about priorities. People who put their own comfort ahead of God's work find that they are never satisfied, and when they start working, God says, "I am with you." The book also looks forward. God promises to shake the heavens and the earth, and Hebrews 12:26-28 uses that promise to speak of a kingdom that cannot be shaken.

# Zechariah
@id book-zec
@ref ZEC 4:6; ZEC 9:9-10; ZEC 12:10
@conf scholars

Zechariah has fourteen chapters and is the longest of the Minor Prophets, rich in imagery and in hope. The name means "the LORD remembers." Zechariah was a priest as well as a prophet, and he began prophesying in 520 BC, two months after Haggai.

The book falls into two parts. Chapters 1 to 8 are dated to 520 to 518 BC and are addressed to the people rebuilding the temple. Chapters 9 to 14 are undated and are about a coming king and the final day of the LORD. Some scholars think the second part was written by someone else at a later time, and others hold that both parts come from Zechariah.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 6: eight visions given in one night, including horsemen, a measuring line, the high priest Joshua in clean clothes, a lampstand and flying scroll.
- Chapters 7 and 8: a question about fasting, and promises of a restored Jerusalem.
- Chapters 9 to 11: the coming king on a donkey, and the rejected shepherd.
- Chapters 12 to 14: the pierced one, the cleansing fountain and the final day, when the LORD will be king over all the earth.

## What to look for
Zechariah encourages: the work is done "not by might nor by power, but by My Spirit," says the LORD. After Haggai's call to rebuild, he looks beyond the temple to the king. The New Testament draws on these chapters in the Gospels' accounts of Palm Sunday, the thirty pieces of silver and the piercing, and Revelation uses the same visions.

# Malachi
@id book-mal
@ref MAL 3:1-3; MAL 3:10; MAL 4:5-6

Malachi has four chapters in English Bibles and three in Hebrew, and it closes the Old Testament. The name means "my messenger," and some have wondered whether it is a title rather than a name. It is a book of arguments, in which God makes a charge, the people ask "How?", and God answers.

The book is usually dated to about 450 to 430 BC, after the temple had been rebuilt and the walls of the city repaired, when the first enthusiasm of the return had faded. Priests were offering blemished animals, the people were withholding tithes, marriages were breaking down, and some wondered if serving God paid off.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1:1 to 5: God's love for Israel, which the people doubt.
- Chapter 1:6 to 2:9: charges against the priests for dishonoring God's name.
- Chapter 2:10 to 16: unfaithfulness in marriage.
- Chapter 2:17 to 3:5: where is the God of justice? The messenger will come.
- Chapter 3:6 to 4:6: the call to return, with tithes, and a final promise that Elijah will be sent before the great day.

## What to look for
Malachi is about a love that is taken for granted. God tells the people he has loved them, and they respond with doubt. The book builds toward the promise of a messenger who will prepare the way, and the New Testament identifies him with John the Baptist (Matthew 11:10). Then it stops, and the Old Testament closes on a note of waiting, with four centuries of silence ahead.
