# Why the church wrote creeds
@ref 1CO 15:3-4; ROM 10:9; 1TI 3:16; JUD 1:3
@conf scholars
@q If you had to say in one paragraph what you believe about Jesus, what would you include?

The word creed comes from the Latin credo, "I believe." A creed is a short, agreed statement of the faith, said together in worship. It may sound like a later invention, but the habit begins in the New Testament itself. Paul passes on to the Corinthians what he had received: "that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that He was buried, that He was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3-4). Most scholars think he is quoting a formula that was already in use, within a few years of the crucifixion. The shortest creed of all was a confession of two words, "Jesus is Lord" (Romans 10:9). And 1 Timothy 3:16 seems to quote a hymn of the early church.

Creeds grew up for three practical reasons. They were used for baptism, when a new believer was asked questions such as "Do you believe in God the Father, in Jesus Christ his Son, and in the Holy Spirit?" That gave the earliest creeds their threefold shape. They were used for teaching, to summarize what the apostles had handed on. And they were used to answer error, drawing a line when someone taught something different. The early fathers called the summary the rule of faith, and Irenaeus and Tertullian each give versions of it in the second century.

Protestants sometimes object to creeds, saying that the Bible alone is enough. The Reformers, however, kept the great creeds of the early church, and held that they are subordinate to Scripture, but are a useful summary of it. A creed does not replace the Bible. It is more like a map, drawn from the territory, that shows the main roads.

Finally, creeds are said in the first person plural or singular, and they are meant to be confessed together. When a congregation says them, it joins with Christians across two thousand years and across the world in saying what it believes.

# The Apostles' Creed
@ref 1CO 15:3-8; MAT 28:19; ACT 10:36-43
@conf scholars

The Apostles' Creed is the most widely used of all Christian creeds, and it is also the shortest of the three ancient ones. It begins "I believe in God, the Father almighty, creator of heaven and earth," moves to "Jesus Christ, his only Son, our Lord," and continues with his conception by the Holy Spirit, his birth of the virgin Mary, his suffering under Pontius Pilate, his crucifixion, death and burial, his descent to the dead, his resurrection on the third day, his ascension, and his coming to judge the living and the dead. It ends with "the Holy Spirit, the holy catholic church, the communion of saints, the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection of the body, and the life everlasting."

A legend from the fourth century said that the twelve apostles wrote it together after Pentecost, each contributing a clause. It is not true, but the name reflects the belief that it faithfully sums up what the apostles taught. Its actual roots are in a baptismal creed used in the church in Rome by about AD 150 to 200, called the Old Roman Creed. The form we use now took shape slowly in the following centuries, and had settled by about the eighth century.

Several phrases deserve comment. "Catholic" means universal, not Roman: the one church throughout the world. "He descended to the dead" (in older versions "into hell") was added in the fourth century, and it has been understood in several ways, as Christ's entering the realm of the dead, or as the depth of his suffering. "The communion of saints" means the fellowship of all God's people, living and dead. Some churches say "He descended into hell," and others omit the line, but the creed is accepted by Catholic, Orthodox and nearly all Protestant churches.

The creed has a simple structure: Father, Son and Holy Spirit, and the church. It tells the whole story of God's work in a hundred or so words. A believer who has learned it by heart has the outline of the faith always at hand.

# The Nicene Creed
@ref JHN 1:1-14; COL 1:15-20; JHN 15:26
@conf scholars

The Nicene Creed is the one statement of faith that is held in common by the Catholic, Orthodox and nearly all Protestant churches, and so it is the nearest thing there is to a creed for all of Christendom. It is longer than the Apostles' Creed, and more precise, because it was written in the heat of a controversy about who Christ is.

The first version was drawn up at the Council of Nicaea in AD 325, to answer Arius, who taught that the Son was a created being. Its key phrases are about Jesus: "God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God, begotten, not made, of one substance with the Father." The Greek word for "of one substance," homoousios, caused a long fight, but it settled the question that the Son is fully God and not a lesser creature. In AD 381 the Council of Constantinople expanded the creed, especially in what it says about the Holy Spirit: "the Lord, the giver of life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son is worshiped and glorified." This enlarged form is the one that is used today, and it is properly called the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed.

A difference arose in the West. The creed says the Spirit "proceeds from the Father." The Latin churches, beginning in Spain in the sixth century, added "and the Son," in Latin filioque. Rome officially adopted it in 1014. The Eastern churches object, both to the theology, which they believe weakens the Father as the source, and to the fact that it was changed without a council of the whole church. It is one of the main issues dividing East and West, and discussions continue. Many Western Christians now use the original wording in joint services as a sign of unity.

The Eastern church says "We believe," while the Western has traditionally said "I believe." Both express the same faith, spoken as individuals who are also part of a body.

# The Athanasian Creed
@ref DEU 6:4; MAT 28:19; JHN 1:14; COL 2:9
@conf scholars

The third of the great ancient creeds is the least known, but it is one of the most detailed statements of the Trinity and the incarnation. It is called the Athanasian Creed, though it was not written by Athanasius, who died in AD 373. It was composed in Latin, probably in southern Gaul, around AD 500, and is also called by its first words, Quicunque vult, "Whoever wishes."

It begins by saying that "Whoever wishes to be saved must, above all, hold the catholic faith," and then lays out in careful pairs what that faith is. The first half is on the Trinity: "we worship one God in Trinity and Trinity in unity, neither blending the persons nor dividing the essence." The Father is God, the Son is God, the Holy Spirit is God, and yet there are not three Gods but one. Each is uncreated, almighty and eternal. It then describes the incarnation: that Christ is both God and man, equal to the Father in his divinity and less than the Father in his humanity, one person and not two, "not by the conversion of the divine into flesh, but by the taking of the humanity into God."

It is a creed with warning clauses: it says that anyone who does not keep the faith "whole and undefiled" will perish eternally. Those clauses trouble many modern readers, and some churches omit them. They are best understood as a stress on how essential the truth about God and Christ is, and not as a verdict on individuals who have not heard it.

The Athanasian Creed was widely used in medieval worship, especially on Sundays, and the Reformers affirmed it. It is still part of the Anglican Book of Common Prayer and the Lutheran Book of Concord. It represents the fullest ancient statement of what Christians believe about the nature of God.

# Heresies and what they teach us
@ref 1JN 4:1-3; GAL 1:6-9; 2PE 2:1; TIT 1:9
@conf scholars

The word heresy comes from a Greek word meaning choice, and in the church it means a teaching that contradicts the core of the faith. It is a harsh word, and it was often used carelessly, but in its original sense it names the errors that the creeds were written to answer. Looking at them reveals what the church was protecting.

Most heresies are not inventions out of nothing. They are half-truths, one part of the truth blown up at the expense of another. Some make Jesus less than God. Arianism said that the Son was the first and greatest creature, and adoptionism that he was a man whom God adopted. Others make him less than man. Docetism said that he only seemed to have a body, and Gnosticism held that matter is evil and so the Son of God could not really have become flesh. Apollinarianism said that he had no human mind.

Some err about the Trinity. Modalism, or Sabellianism, from Sabellius in the third century, said that the Father, Son and Spirit are not three persons, but three modes or masks of one God, who acts first as Father, then as Son and then as Spirit. Tritheism makes the three into three Gods. Others err on salvation. Pelagius taught that people can obey God by their own power, without grace. Donatists said that the sacraments were invalid if the minister was a sinner.

Each of these was answered by the church because it threatened something at the heart of the gospel. If Jesus is not fully God, he cannot save. If he is not fully man, he cannot represent us. If the Trinity is a mask, then God's love is not eternal. If we can save ourselves, then grace is optional.

The history of heresy is a warning to every age. The old errors return in new forms, and what they have in common is that they make the faith easier to understand and take away its power. As Jude writes, we are to "contend earnestly for the faith entrusted once for all to the saints."

# The seven councils
@ref ACT 15:1-31; GAL 2:1-10; EPH 4:3-6
@conf scholars

Early Christianity held councils, from the meeting of apostles and elders in Jerusalem in Acts 15 onward, to settle disputes. As the church grew, bishops gathered from across the empire to decide matters of doctrine. Seven of these are called ecumenical councils, from the Greek for "the whole inhabited world," because they were meant to speak for the whole church. They are accepted by the Eastern Orthodox churches, Catholics and most Protestants as the basis of orthodox belief in Christ and the Trinity.

The first was the Council of Nicaea in AD 325, which condemned Arianism and began the Nicene Creed. The second was Constantinople in 381, which completed the creed and affirmed the divinity of the Holy Spirit. The third was Ephesus in 431, which condemned Nestorius and affirmed that Mary is Theotokos, "God-bearer," to protect the unity of the person of Christ. The fourth was Chalcedon in 451, which defined Christ as one person in two natures.

The remaining three took up fine points of the same issues. Constantinople II, in 553, tried to bridge the gap with those who rejected Chalcedon. Constantinople III, in 680 and 681, held that Christ has two wills, divine and human, which are in harmony. Nicaea II, in 787, dealt with the use of images, or icons, in worship, which the Orthodox honor and some Protestants reject. The Orthodox accept these seven, and the Catholic Church has recognized later councils, twenty-one in all, down to Vatican II in 1962 to 1965.

Protestants generally honor the first four, and many the first six, as faithful expressions of Scripture. The Reformers did not hold them to have authority equal to Scripture, since councils can err. But they believed that the early councils defined the teaching of the apostles about Christ well. The result is a framework within which most Christian traditions can recognize one another: one God in three persons, and one Lord Jesus Christ, truly God and truly man.

# The Reformation confessions
@ref ROM 3:21-28; EPH 2:8-10; 2TI 3:16-17
@conf scholars

The Reformation of the sixteenth century produced a flood of new statements of faith, called confessions, as the Protestant churches explained what they believed and how they differed from Rome and from each other. Several of them are still in use.

The Augsburg Confession of 1530, written by Philipp Melanchthon, was presented to the emperor Charles V as the statement of the Lutheran princes. It is the basic confession of the Lutheran churches, and it stresses that it teaches nothing new and nothing contrary to the catholic church. The Belgic Confession of 1561, by Guido de Brès, and the Heidelberg Catechism of 1563, by Zacharias Ursinus and Caspar Olevianus, became standards of the Reformed churches of the Netherlands and Germany. The Heidelberg Catechism opens with a question that is still loved, "What is your only comfort in life and in death?" and answers, "That I am not my own, but belong, body and soul, in life and in death, to my faithful Savior Jesus Christ."

The Church of England produced the Thirty-Nine Articles, in 1563 and in final form in 1571, which are still a standard for Anglicans. The Synod of Dort, in the Netherlands in 1618 and 1619, produced the Canons that answered the Arminians. And the Westminster Confession of 1646, with its catechisms, became the standard for English-speaking Presbyterians. The Baptists produced their own in the Second London Confession, issued in 1677 and affirmed in 1689, which was largely based on Westminster.

Five Latin phrases are often used to sum up the central convictions of the Reformation. Sola scriptura, Scripture alone, is the final authority. Sola fide, faith alone, is how a person is justified. Sola gratia, grace alone, is the source of salvation. Solus Christus, Christ alone, is the only mediator. And soli Deo gloria, to God alone be the glory, is the purpose of it all. The grouping as the "five solas" is a modern summary, but the ideas run through all of the confessions.

# Catechisms: teaching the faith by question and answer
@ref DEU 6:6-9; PRO 22:6; LUK 1:4
@conf scholars

A catechism is a way of teaching the faith by question and answer. The word comes from the Greek for "to sound down," or "to teach by word of mouth," and the practice goes back to the early church, which instructed candidates for baptism, called catechumens, over a period that could last years. In the Reformation the form took on new importance, since the Reformers saw that reform would only last if ordinary people, including children, understood what they believed.

Martin Luther wrote his Small Catechism in 1529 after visiting churches in Saxony and finding that many people did not know the Lord's Prayer or the Ten Commandments. He had been shocked, and he wrote the work so that a father could teach it to his household. It takes the Ten Commandments, the Apostles' Creed, the Lord's Prayer, baptism and the Lord's Supper, and gives brief explanations. The Heidelberg Catechism of 1563 was designed to be taught over the fifty-two Sundays of the year. The Westminster Shorter Catechism of 1647 has 107 questions, and begins with the best-known of all: "What is the chief end of man? Man's chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him forever."

The question-and-answer form has strengths. It puts the essentials in memorable shape. It moves from what we believe to why it matters. And it can be learned by heart, which means it is available when it is needed. Many people who learned the catechism as children say that its words stayed with them throughout their lives, and came back in times of grief or temptation.

Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican churches also have catechisms. The Catechism of the Catholic Church was published in 1992, and the Baltimore Catechism taught generations of American Catholic children. The basic idea is the same as the instruction in Deuteronomy: that the words of faith should be taught to children, and spoken of at home.

# Faith and the creeds today
@ref JUD 1:3; 1TI 6:12; 2TI 1:13-14
@conf scholars
@q Is there a line in the Apostles' Creed you find hard to say, and why?

Not every Christian group uses the historic creeds. Some churches, especially in the traditions that grew out of the Restoration movement of the early nineteenth century and in many independent congregations, hold to a principle of "no creed but the Bible" or "no creed but Christ." They fear that written statements can be put above Scripture, or divide believers. Others write their own short statements of faith. Many evangelical churches have a doctrinal statement that sets out in a page or two what they believe about Scripture, the Trinity, salvation and so on.

The strength of the historic creeds is in their age and breadth. They were not written by a single denomination or in a single century. They were refined by the whole church, over centuries, in response to real challenges, and they express what Christians of nearly every tradition hold in common. The English writer C. S. Lewis called it "mere Christianity," meaning the faith that is common ground to all. They also remind us that we have inherited our faith and did not invent it. Paul told Timothy, "Guard the treasure entrusted to you."

They have limits. A creed is not a complete theology, and it does not cover every question. It does not say much about how to live. It was never meant to replace Scripture, and the Reformers held that it was subordinate to it, and could be corrected by it. Reciting a creed without meaning it is also an empty act.

Used well, a creed is a way of saying what you believe and with whom you stand. It is a way for a congregation, in a few moments, to confess the faith it holds in common with the saints, from the apostles to the believer in the next pew. Many Christians find that saying the same words every week gives a steadiness when their own faith is wavering, as they borrow the faith of the church until their own is renewed.
