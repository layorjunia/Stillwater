# Ephesians
@id book-eph
@ref EPH 1:3-6; EPH 2:8-10; EPH 6:10-18
@conf scholars

Ephesians has six chapters and is a letter about the grandeur of the church and the riches of life in Christ. It reads more like a sermon or a meditation than a reply to a problem, and it is often called the crown of Paul's letters. It falls neatly in two: three chapters about what God has done, and three about how to live in light of it.

Paul wrote it from prison, probably in Rome around AD 60 to 62. Some of the oldest manuscripts leave out the words "in Ephesus" in the first verse, which has led many to think it was a circular letter meant for a group of churches in Asia Minor, with Ephesus as one stop. A number of scholars doubt that Paul wrote it, citing its long sentences and vocabulary, but most Christians through history have accepted it as his.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 3: praise for every spiritual blessing in Christ, salvation by grace through faith, the joining of Jews and Gentiles into one body, and a prayer that believers may know the love of Christ.
- Chapters 4 to 6: unity and maturity in the church, putting off the old self, walking in love and light, the marriage and household codes, and the armor of God.

## What to look for
Count how often the letter says "in Christ" or "in him." Chapter 2 gives the best-known summary: "For it is by grace you have been saved through faith, and this not from yourselves; it is the gift of God." The theme that holds it together is the mystery now revealed, that Gentiles are fellow heirs with Jews. The church is a body, a building, and a bride, and its life together is meant to show God's wisdom to the world.

# Philippians
@id book-php
@ref PHP 1:21; PHP 2:5-11; PHP 4:4-7; PHP 4:11-13

Philippians has four chapters and is the warmest of Paul's letters, a thank-you note that turns into a lesson about joy. Philippi was a Roman colony in Macedonia and the first city in Europe where Paul planted a church, on his second journey (Acts 16). The people there had supported him over the years and had just sent a gift by Epaphroditus.

Paul wrote from prison, most likely in Rome around AD 60 to 62, though Ephesus and Caesarea have been proposed. He did not know if he would be freed or executed, and yet the letter is full of joy and rejoicing. He writes to reassure them, to thank them and to urge them to stand together.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: thanksgiving, Paul's circumstances, and the call to live worthy of the gospel. "To live is Christ and to die is gain."
- Chapter 2: unity and humility, supported by the great hymn about Christ, who made himself nothing and was exalted. Timothy and Epaphroditus are commended.
- Chapter 3: a warning against those who trust in the flesh, and Paul's own testimony of counting everything as loss for the sake of knowing Christ.
- Chapter 4: appeals to unity and peace, "Rejoice in the Lord always," and thanks for the gift.

## What to look for
The word that keeps coming back is joy, though the writer is chained to a Roman guard. Paul has learned the secret of being content in plenty and in need, and the source is Christ. The hymn in chapter 2 is among the earliest Christian confessions and says that every knee will bow, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord.

# Colossians
@id book-col
@ref COL 1:15-20; COL 2:6-10; COL 3:1-4
@conf scholars

Colossians has four chapters and is a short letter that sets out the supremacy of Christ over everything. Colossae was a small town in the Lycus Valley, in what is now Turkey, near Laodicea and Hierapolis. Paul had never been there. The church was started by his associate Epaphras, who came to him in prison with a worrying report.

It was written from prison, probably in Rome around AD 60 to 62, and it was carried by Tychicus along with the letters to Philemon and to the Ephesians, which share much of its wording. The false teaching at Colossae is not fully described, but it seems to have mixed Jewish rules about food and festivals with a mystical philosophy, the worship of angels and a belief that Christ was not enough. A few scholars doubt Paul's authorship, though many accept it.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: thanks and prayer, then a great hymn to Christ as the image of the invisible God, the creator and reconciler of all things.
- Chapter 2: warnings against empty philosophy and human rules. In Christ all the fullness of deity lives in bodily form.
- Chapters 3 and 4: new life with Christ. Set your minds on things above, put off the old self, put on love, and live out the new life in households, and in prayer and speech.

## What to look for
The answer to every false teaching is Christ. He is before all things, and in him all things hold together. The believer is not looking for anything more, because "you have been made complete in Christ." The consequence is a new way of life, in which the old habits are stripped off like dirty clothes and a new character put on.

# 1 Thessalonians
@id book-1th
@ref 1TH 1:2-10; 1TH 4:13-18; 1TH 5:16-18

First Thessalonians has five chapters and is probably the earliest of Paul's surviving letters, or very near it. It was written to a young church, which Paul had been forced to leave after only a few weeks, and it is filled with encouragement and affection. Reading it feels like a letter from a father to his children.

Paul, Silas and Timothy planted the church in Thessalonica, a major city in Macedonia, on the second journey (Acts 17), around AD 50. The new believers were harassed by their neighbors. Paul wrote from Corinth about AD 50 or 51 after Timothy returned with good news of their faith and love, together with some questions.

## How it is arranged
- Chapters 1 to 3: thanksgiving for their faith, love and hope, a reminder of how Paul and his friends lived among them, and his joy at Timothy's report.
- Chapter 4: how to please God, with holiness and love, and teaching about those who have died.
- Chapter 5: the day of the Lord, and brief commands about community life.

## What to look for
The three words that begin the letter, faith, love and hope, are the framework. The most famous section is 4:13 to 18, where Paul comforts those who are grieving: Christ will return, the dead in Christ will rise first, and believers will be with the Lord forever. The letter says the church should live in watchfulness and joy, in the expectation of the coming of Jesus. It ends with the short commands "Rejoice at all times. Pray without ceasing. Give thanks in every circumstance."

# 2 Thessalonians
@id book-2th
@ref 2TH 1:3-10; 2TH 2:1-4; 2TH 3:6-13
@conf scholars

Second Thessalonians has three chapters and is a short follow-up to the first letter, written a few months later to the same church. Some of the believers had been unsettled by the claim that the day of the Lord had already come, and others had stopped working and were living off the generosity of others.

Paul, Silas and Timothy are named at the start, and the letter was probably written from Corinth in AD 51. A few scholars have questioned whether Paul wrote it, citing differences of tone from the first letter, but the traditional view remains the majority one.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: thanks for their steadfast faith under persecution, and the promise that Christ will bring justice at his return.
- Chapter 2: the day of the Lord has not yet come. First, the rebellion and the revealing of "the man of lawlessness" must happen.
- Chapter 3: a request for prayer, and a strong word to those who live in idleness.

## What to look for
Chapter 2 is among the most difficult passages in Paul, and Christians have disagreed for centuries about who or what the man of lawlessness and the restrainer are. The main point is clear and practical. Believers should not be shaken by claims that the end has come, and they should keep working. Paul says, "If anyone is unwilling to work, he shall not eat," and he tells them to hold to the teachings they were given.

# 1 Timothy
@id book-1ti
@ref 1TI 2:1-6; 1TI 3:1-13; 1TI 6:6-12
@conf scholars

First Timothy has six chapters and is the first of three letters, with Second Timothy and Titus, known as the Pastoral Letters, because they give guidance to leaders who care for churches. Timothy was Paul's young protégé, whom Paul calls his true child in the faith, and he had been left in Ephesus to deal with false teachers.

The traditional view is that Paul wrote after his release from the imprisonment described at the end of Acts, probably around AD 62 to 64. Some modern scholars think the Pastoral Letters were written in Paul's name by a follower after his death, citing their vocabulary and the church order they assume. Others reply that the letters are personal in a way a forger would hardly have achieved.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: false teaching and Paul's own testimony as a forgiven persecutor.
- Chapter 2: prayer for all, and order in worship.
- Chapter 3: qualifications for overseers and deacons, and the church as the household of God.
- Chapters 4 to 6: a warning about those who forbid marriage and certain foods, advice on caring for widows and elders, and warnings about the love of money.

## What to look for
Paul says that a leader should be above reproach in character, and the long lists in chapter 3 are mostly about behavior and not about gifts. The letter is concerned with sound teaching, and it repeatedly says that godliness is the point. The warning about money has become a classic: "the love of money is the root of all kinds of evil." The letter also holds a short early hymn about Christ in 3:16.

# 2 Timothy
@id book-2ti
@ref 2TI 1:6-8; 2TI 3:14-17; 2TI 4:6-8

Second Timothy has four chapters and is the last letter Paul wrote. It was composed from a Roman prison, where, unlike in his earlier imprisonments, he expected to die. It is a farewell to the younger man whom he loved, and it is among the most moving writings in the New Testament.

Paul was probably executed under the emperor Nero, around AD 64 to 67, according to early church tradition. The letter says that most of his friends in Asia had deserted him, that only Luke was with him, and that he wanted Timothy to come quickly, and to bring his cloak and his books.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: a charge to fan into flame the gift of God, and not to be ashamed of the gospel or of Paul in chains.
- Chapter 2: images of a soldier, an athlete and a farmer, and a call to be faithful with what has been entrusted.
- Chapter 3: difficult times ahead, and a reminder that Scripture is God-breathed and equips for every good work.
- Chapter 4: a solemn charge to preach the word, Paul's own final summary, and personal greetings.

## What to look for
Paul writes as one who is passing the baton: "Guard the treasure entrusted to you." His summary of his life is one of the great sentences of the Bible: "I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith." He is not afraid. The letter says that the word of God is not chained, and that the Lord stood by him and gave him strength.

# Titus
@id book-tit
@ref TIT 1:5-9; TIT 2:11-14; TIT 3:4-7
@conf scholars

Titus has three chapters and is the shortest of the Pastoral Letters. Titus was a Gentile convert and a trusted coworker of Paul, who had served in the difficult church at Corinth, and who was now left on the island of Crete to put things in order. The letter is a brief charge for that task.

The traditional date is about AD 62 to 64, after Paul's first Roman imprisonment, for the same reasons as 1 Timothy, though many scholars question Paul's authorship. The island of Crete had a reputation for dishonesty, and Paul quotes one of its own poets on the subject (1:12).

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: appoint elders in every town, men of proven character who can teach sound doctrine and refute opposition.
- Chapter 2: how different groups in the church should live, and why. The grace of God has appeared, teaching us to say no to ungodliness.
- Chapter 3: how to live in society, as good citizens, and a summary of the gospel.

## What to look for
The letter links belief and behavior. Good doctrine produces good works, and "doing good" is mentioned again and again. The two summaries of the gospel in 2:11 to 14 and 3:4 to 7 are among the clearest in the New Testament, and they say that salvation comes from God's kindness and mercy and not from our righteous deeds, by the washing of regeneration and renewal by the Holy Spirit.

# Philemon
@id book-phm
@ref PHM 1:8-16; PHM 1:17-21

Philemon is one chapter of twenty-five verses and is the shortest of Paul's letters, and the only one in the New Testament that is a purely personal note to a friend about a private matter. It is about a runaway slave and about how a Christian ought to think of him.

Philemon was a wealthy believer in Colossae, in whose home the church met. His slave Onesimus had run away, perhaps with some money, and had somehow met Paul in prison, probably in Rome around AD 60 to 62, and had become a Christian. Paul sends him back with this letter, carried together with the one to the Colossians.

## How it is arranged
- Verses 1 to 7: greeting and thanks for Philemon's love and faith.
- Verses 8 to 16: Paul's appeal for Onesimus, whom he calls his own child, and who is "no longer as a slave, but better than a slave, as a beloved brother."
- Verses 17 to 25: "receive him as you would receive me," with an offer to repay anything he owes, and a hope of visiting.

## What to look for
Paul is gentle and clever. He could command, but he appeals, and he uses the language of partnership and of family to ask Philemon to receive Onesimus. He does not say outright that slavery should end, but he sets a principle that undermines it, since two men are now brothers in Christ. Many readers have noticed the shape of the gospel here: one who owes a debt, and another who steps in and says, "charge it to my account."

# Hebrews
@id book-heb
@ref HEB 1:1-4; HEB 4:14-16; HEB 11:1-3; HEB 12:1-2
@conf scholars

Hebrews has thirteen chapters and is a carefully written sermon about the superiority of Jesus Christ to everything that came before. It is the most polished Greek in the New Testament and one of the most theologically rich books, and it explains the Old Testament sacrifices and priesthood by showing how Christ fulfills them.

The author is unknown. The letter does not name him. Origen, a third-century scholar, said that only God knows who wrote it. Paul, Barnabas, Apollos, Priscilla and Luke have all been suggested. It was written to Jewish Christians who were under pressure and tempted to turn back, probably before AD 70, because it speaks as if the temple services were still going on.

## How it is arranged
- 1:1 to 4:13: Christ is greater than the prophets, angels and Moses, and a warning against unbelief.
- 4:14 to 10:18: Christ is the great high priest, in the order of Melchizedek, with a better covenant and a better sacrifice, offered once for all.
- 10:19 to 12:29: draw near with confidence, hold on, and run with endurance, with the great roll of those who lived by faith in chapter 11.
- Chapter 13: practical exhortations and a blessing.

## What to look for
The word "better" runs through it: a better hope, a better covenant, better promises. The letter contains five stern warnings against drifting away, and Christians have debated how to read them. It also gives the best-known definition of faith in the Bible, in chapter 11. And it offers the picture of Jesus as the one who understands our weakness, so that we may "approach the throne of grace with confidence."

# James
@id book-jas
@ref JAS 1:2-4; JAS 1:22-25; JAS 2:14-17; JAS 3:5-10

James has five chapters and reads like the wisdom literature of the Old Testament in a Christian voice. It is a practical letter, more like Proverbs than Romans, with short, direct commands about speech, money, patience and prayer. Martin Luther disliked it at first, because it seemed to him to contradict Paul, but later readers have seen that the two are addressing different problems.

The author calls himself "a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ," and the tradition identifies him as James, the brother of Jesus, who became the leader of the church in Jerusalem (Acts 15) and was martyred about AD 62. Some think it was written as early as the mid-40s AD, which would make it among the earliest books in the New Testament. It is addressed to Jewish Christians scattered abroad.

## How it is arranged
James moves quickly from topic to topic, but the main themes are these.
- Chapter 1: trials as a means of maturity, asking God for wisdom, and being doers of the word.
- Chapter 2: against showing favoritism to the rich, and the relationship between faith and works.
- Chapter 3: the power of the tongue, and the wisdom that comes from above.
- Chapters 4 and 5: warnings against pride and worldliness, a rebuke to the rich who oppress workers, patience, and prayer for the sick.

## What to look for
The famous passage is 2:14 to 26: "faith without deeds is dead." James is not arguing against justification by faith. He is saying that real faith produces real obedience. The tests he gives are concrete: how you speak, how you treat the poor, whether you control your anger, whether you hear and do.

# 1 Peter
@id book-1pe
@ref 1PE 1:3-9; 1PE 2:9-12; 1PE 3:13-17

First Peter has five chapters and is a letter of encouragement to Christians who were suffering for their faith. It is addressed to believers scattered across five provinces of Asia Minor, whom Peter calls the elect who are exiles of the Dispersion. They did not belong anywhere, and the letter tells them who they really are.

The author is Peter the apostle, writing with the help of Silvanus, who is probably the same as Silas (5:12). He writes from "Babylon," which was a common code word for Rome, probably in the early 60s AD, as the persecution of Christians was beginning under Nero. The tone is hopeful, as of a man who has himself learned how to suffer.

## How it is arranged
- 1:1 to 2:10: the believers' new birth and living hope, and their identity as living stones, a holy priesthood and a chosen people.
- 2:11 to 3:12: living well among unbelievers, in society, at work and in the home, following the example of Christ in suffering.
- 3:13 to 5:14: how to face suffering, prepared to give a reason for hope, and humility and watchfulness for the elders and the young.

## What to look for
Peter's message is that suffering is not strange for those who follow Christ, and that it is not meaningless. The letter points again and again to the example of Jesus, and to the hope of an inheritance that cannot perish. A key verse is 3:15: "Always be prepared to give a defense to everyone who asks you the reason for the hope that is in you." The instruction is to do it with gentleness and respect.

# 2 Peter
@id book-2pe
@ref 2PE 1:3-8; 2PE 1:19-21; 2PE 3:8-13
@conf scholars

Second Peter has three chapters and is a short farewell letter in which the apostle warns against false teachers and encourages growth in the faith. It speaks as the testament of a man who knows he will soon die, and it urges the readers to remember what they have been taught.

The letter names Simon Peter as the author and speaks of his coming death (1:14), which would place it in the mid-60s AD. It is the most disputed letter in the New Testament. It was accepted slowly by the early church, its style differs from 1 Peter, and many scholars think it was written later in Peter's name. Others defend Peter's authorship and the differences of style by the use of a different secretary.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: God's power has given everything for life and godliness, so add to faith goodness, knowledge, self-control, perseverance and love. Peter recalls the transfiguration and the reliability of prophecy.
- Chapter 2: false teachers who will secretly bring in destructive heresies, with examples of judgment from the Old Testament. This chapter is closely parallel to Jude.
- Chapter 3: scoffers who ask where the promised coming is, and the answer that the Lord is patient and the day will come.

## What to look for
The letter says Scripture did not come from human will, but men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit. In chapter 3 Peter calls Paul's letters Scripture, which is an important early note. When people ask why Christ has not yet returned, the answer is that "with the Lord a day is like a thousand years," and that he does not delay but is patient, wanting everyone to come to repentance.

# 1, 2 and 3 John
@id book-1jn
@ref 1JN 1:5-9; 1JN 4:7-12; 1JN 5:11-13

The three letters of John are five chapters, one chapter and one chapter. They are among the simplest in vocabulary and among the deepest in thought. They are written in short sentences, with pairs of opposites: light and darkness, love and hate, truth and lies, life and death.

The letters do not name their author, but the early church was nearly unanimous in saying that they came from the apostle John, in his old age, probably in Ephesus around AD 85 to 95. They share a style and vocabulary with the Gospel of John. The first was written to a community shaken by a split, in which some who had left were denying that Jesus Christ had come in the flesh.

## How it is arranged
First John circles back to the same themes three times, rather than following a strict outline.
- 1:1 to 2:27: walking in the light, confessing sin, obeying the commandments, and warnings about antichrists.
- 2:28 to 4:6: children of God, who practice righteousness and love, and the test of spirits.
- 4:7 to 5:21: God is love, faith overcomes the world, and the assurance of eternal life.
- Second John: a short letter warning against hospitality to teachers who deny that Christ came in the flesh.
- Third John: a note to Gaius, praising his hospitality to traveling missionaries and rebuking Diotrephes.

## What to look for
The author says why he writes: "so that you may know that you have eternal life." The tests of real faith are belief in Jesus as God's Son come in the flesh, obedience, and love for other believers. The letter gives two short definitions of God: "God is light" and "God is love." Love is shown most plainly in the sending of his Son.

# Jude
@id book-jud
@ref JUD 1:3-4; JUD 1:20-21; JUD 1:24-25
@conf scholars

Jude is one chapter of twenty-five verses and is a short, fierce letter about defending the faith. The writer had meant to write about salvation, but felt that he had to urge the readers instead "to contend earnestly for the faith entrusted once for all to the saints."

The author calls himself "a servant of Jesus Christ and a brother of James," and the usual view is that he was the brother of Jesus, who like James had once doubted but later believed. The date is uncertain, from the 60s to the 80s AD. A large part of it closely resembles 2 Peter 2, and it is not clear which came first.

## How it is arranged
- Verses 1 to 4: greeting, and the reason for writing, which is that certain people have slipped into the church and are perverting grace.
- Verses 5 to 16: examples of judgment, from the exodus generation, the angels who left their post, and Sodom and Gomorrah, and a portrait of the false teachers.
- Verses 17 to 23: remember what the apostles said, build yourselves up, and show mercy to those who doubt.
- Verses 24 and 25: one of the finest doxologies in the Bible.

## What to look for
Jude draws on a Jewish book called 1 Enoch (verses 14 and 15) and on other Jewish traditions, which shows that a New Testament writer could quote a source without making all of it Scripture. The tone is urgent, but the end is joy. The closing blessing says that God "is able to keep you from stumbling and to present you unblemished in His glorious presence, with great joy."

# Revelation
@id book-rev
@ref REV 1:17-18; REV 5:9-10; REV 21:3-5; REV 22:20
@conf debated

Revelation has twenty-two chapters and is the last book of the Bible. Its first word in Greek is apokalypsis, "unveiling," which is why it is also called the Apocalypse. It is a letter, a prophecy and a vision, full of symbols drawn from Daniel, Ezekiel and the rest of the Old Testament. It is easy to misread and also one of the most hopeful books in Scripture.

The author calls himself John and says that he was on the island of Patmos "because of the word of God and my testimony about Jesus." Tradition identifies him with the apostle. Most scholars date the book to about AD 95, under the emperor Domitian, when Christians were under pressure to join in the worship of Caesar. Some prefer the late 60s, soon after Nero.

## How it is arranged
- Chapter 1: the vision of the risen Christ among seven lampstands.
- Chapters 2 and 3: seven letters to seven churches in Asia Minor.
- Chapters 4 and 5: the throne of God and the Lamb who was slain.
- Chapters 6 to 16: the seven seals, seven trumpets and seven bowls, with visions in between.
- Chapters 17 to 20: the fall of Babylon, the return of Christ, and the final judgment.
- Chapters 21 and 22: the new heaven and the new earth, and the new Jerusalem.

## How to read it
Christians have understood it in four main ways. Some see it as describing events of the first century. Some see it as a sweep of church history. Some see most of it as still future. Others see it as a set of timeless pictures of the conflict between Christ and evil. The book itself says it was written to encourage believers who were suffering: Christ is alive, he holds history in his hand, and he will win. The last prayer is the church's own: "Come, Lord Jesus."
