# The brain, the senses and the body: how it is built and how it works.
@series brain
@src Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science (5th ed.)
@conf solid

How many neurons? | The human brain has about 86 billion neurons. | For a long time, textbooks said the human brain has 100 billion neurons, a round number nobody had really counted. In 2009 the Brazilian scientist Suzana Herculano-Houzel and her colleagues did it a different way: they dissolved brain tissue into a uniform soup of cell nuclei and counted samples under the microscope. They arrived at about 86 billion neurons, and roughly the same number of other cells. It's a smaller number than the legend, and it's still more than enough to be astonishing.|86 billion|Azevedo et al., 2009
The synapse | A tiny gap where one neuron sends a chemical message to the next. | Neurons don't touch. At the junction, called a synapse, there's a tiny gap, and when a signal arrives, the first cell releases chemical messengers that cross the gap and tell the next cell whether to fire. A single neuron can receive thousands of these connections. Learning works partly by strengthening some synapses and letting others fade, so what you do with your brain changes its physical wiring. That's how a skill you practice turns into something your body just knows.||Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science
The action potential | A neuron's signal is an all-or-nothing electrical spike. | A neuron's signal is a spike of electricity that either happens fully or doesn't happen at all. In 1952 Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley worked out how it travels, using the huge nerve fiber of a squid, which was big enough to put electrodes inside. A stronger stimulus doesn't make a bigger spike. It makes more spikes per second. So the brain's code for "louder" or "brighter" is "faster," like a Geiger counter clicking more rapidly.||Hodgkin & Huxley, 1952
Myelin | A fatty sheath around nerve fibers that makes signals travel much faster. | Many of your nerve fibers are wrapped in a fatty coating called myelin, the way electrical wire has insulation. It makes signals travel dramatically faster, over 100 meters per second in the fastest fibers, compared with about a meter per second in bare ones. In multiple sclerosis, the immune system attacks the myelin, and messages slow down or stop getting through. It's why the disease can cause weakness, numbness and vision trouble.||Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science
Glial cells | Brain cells that are not neurons but do essential work. | Neurons get the attention, but the brain has about as many other cells, called glia, and they do vital work. Astrocytes feed neurons and tidy up their chemical surroundings. Oligodendrocytes make myelin. Microglia act as the brain's cleaners and defenders. "Glia" comes from the Greek for glue, since early anatomists thought that's all they were. It's now known they help shape how circuits work, and they're a very active area of research.||Azevedo et al., 2009
Brain energy | The brain is about 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of the body's energy. | Your brain is only about 2 percent of your body's weight, but it uses roughly 20 percent of its energy, whether you're thinking hard or lying on the couch. Much of that goes to keeping neurons ready to fire, like an engine idling. Hard concentration adds only a small amount to the constant bill. So "thinking burns lots of calories" is mostly a myth, since your brain is expensive just for being on.|20%|Raichle & Gusnard, 2002
Glutamate and GABA | The brain's main accelerator and brake. | Most of the brain's signaling comes down to a push and a pull. Glutamate is the main excitatory messenger, telling neurons to fire. GABA is the main inhibitory one, telling them to calm down. The balance between them matters a great deal, and a lot of medicines work by shifting it. Alcohol and many anti-anxiety drugs, for instance, partly work by boosting the effect of GABA, which is why they feel calming.||Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science
Serotonin | A chemical messenger linked to mood, sleep and digestion, made mostly in the gut. | Serotonin is often called the mood chemical, but most of it, about 90 percent, is actually in your gut, where it helps regulate digestion. It doesn't cross from the gut into the brain, which makes its own supply. For decades people said depression comes from low serotonin. A big review in 2022 found no consistent evidence for that simple idea. Serotonin matters for mood, but the story is much more complicated than a single chemical running low.|About 90%|Moncrieff et al., 2022|mixed
Dopamine and movement | Dopamine is also needed to start movement. | Dopamine has a reputation as the pleasure chemical, but one of its most important jobs is helping you move. In Parkinson's disease, the cells that make dopamine in a deep part of the brain gradually die. People develop tremors and slowness, and find it hard to start movements. A drug called L-dopa, which the brain turns into dopamine, can restore movement for a time, and it was one of the first great successes of brain chemistry.||Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science
Acetylcholine | A messenger used at the muscles and for attention and memory. | Acetylcholine is the messenger a nerve uses to tell a muscle to contract, so every step you take depends on it. It also plays a role in attention and memory in the brain. Some medicines for Alzheimer's disease try to keep its levels up. And botulinum toxin, the active ingredient in Botox, works by blocking its release, which is why it relaxes muscles and smooths wrinkles.||Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science
Endorphins | The body's own painkillers. | In the early 1970s scientists found that the brain has receptors that fit opium, which seemed odd. Why would we have receptors for a plant drug? The answer came in 1975, when John Hughes and Hans Kosterlitz isolated the body's own opioid-like molecules, which they named enkephalins. Endorphins are the broader family. They're released during hard exercise, laughter and pain, and they may be part of why a good run can feel wonderful.||Hughes et al., 1975
Oxytocin | A hormone that affects bonding and trust, but not as simply as its "love hormone" nickname suggests. | The "love hormone" or "cuddle hormone" has a lot of press. Oxytocin does help with childbirth and breastfeeding, and it plays a role in bonding. But the claims about nasal sprays making people more trusting have been hard to repeat, and some studies found that it can also increase favoritism toward your own group. Its effects depend heavily on the situation and the person, so the nickname oversimplifies.||Nave, Camerer & McCullough, 2015|mixed
Cortisol | The body's main stress hormone, which also follows a daily rhythm. | Cortisol is the body's main stress hormone, but it's not just for emergencies. It follows a daily rhythm, peaking about 30 minutes after you wake up, which helps you get going, and dropping to its lowest at night. Short bursts of it are helpful. When levels stay high for a long time, it can harm memory, immune function and sleep. Both the spike and the recovery matter.||Clow et al., 2010
Adrenaline | The surge hormone behind a pounding heart and sharp focus. | When you sense danger, your adrenal glands, which sit on top of your kidneys, release adrenaline within seconds. It makes your heart pound, widens your airways, sends blood to your muscles and sharpens your senses, preparing you to fight or flee. In the United States, it's often called epinephrine, which is the same chemical. It's the same one that is in an auto-injector for severe allergic reactions.||Cannon, 1929
The prefrontal cortex | The front part of the brain handles planning, impulse control and weighing consequences. | The front part of your brain, behind your forehead, is where planning, impulse control, and weighing consequences largely happen. It's among the last areas to mature, continuing to develop into your mid-twenties, which helps explain teenage risk-taking. Damage to it can leave a person's knowledge and intelligence intact, but make it very hard to plan, to hold back impulses and to carry things through.||Gogtay et al., 2004
The amygdala | An almond-shaped region that tags things as important, especially threats. | The amygdala is a pair of almond-shaped clusters deep in the brain that tag things as important, especially possible threats. A woman with damage to hers, known in the research as S.M., felt almost no fear and had trouble recognizing fear on faces. But it isn't only about fear. The amygdala also responds to what's new, surprising or emotionally charged, whether it's good or bad.||Adolphs et al., 1994
The hippocampus | A seahorse-shaped region needed to form new memories and navigate. | The hippocampus, named for its seahorse shape, is crucial for forming new memories and for finding your way around. In 2000 Eleanor Maguire scanned London taxi drivers, who have to memorize thousands of streets, and found that the rear part of their hippocampus was larger than in other people. The more years they'd driven, the bigger it was. It's also one of the first regions affected in Alzheimer's disease.||Maguire et al., 2000
The cerebellum | The "little brain" at the back, with most of the brain's neurons. | The cerebellum sits at the back of the brain and means "little brain." It's small, about 10 percent of the brain's volume, but it contains about 80 percent of its neurons. It smooths and times your movements, keeps your balance and helps you learn skills like riding a bike. Researchers have also found it takes part in language and thinking, so it does more than coordinate muscles.|About 80%|Azevedo et al., 2009
The thalamus | The brain's relay station for the senses. | Think of the thalamus as a switchboard in the middle of your brain. Almost all of the signals from your senses, except smell, stop there on the way to the outer cortex. It helps decide what gets through. It also plays a part in regulating attention and sleep, so when it's damaged, people can have trouble staying alert or may lose awareness altogether.||Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science
The hypothalamus | A tiny region that keeps the body's internal balance. | The hypothalamus is only about the size of an almond, but it keeps your body in balance. It monitors and adjusts body temperature, thirst, hunger, sleep cycles and the release of many hormones. It's also the link between the brain and the hormone system, directing the pituitary gland. When you shiver, feel hungry, or get sleepy in the evening, the hypothalamus is involved.||Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science
The basal ganglia | Deep brain structures that help select actions and build habits. | Deep in the brain, the basal ganglia help you choose which action to take and when. They're involved in starting and stopping movements, and in turning repeated behavior into habit. Ann Graybiel's work showed that as a routine becomes automatic, these structures change their activity. They're affected in Parkinson's disease, where movement is hard to start, and in Huntington's disease, which causes unwanted movements.||Graybiel, 2008
The corpus callosum | A bundle of about 200 million fibers connecting the two hemispheres. | The corpus callosum is a thick bundle of about 200 million nerve fibers that connects the left and right halves of the brain and lets them share information. In the mid-twentieth century, doctors began cutting it in some people with severe epilepsy, to stop seizures from spreading across the brain. These patients became the subjects of the famous split-brain studies, which taught us a lot about how the two sides work.|~200 million|Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science
The insula | A fold deep in the cortex that tracks signals from inside the body. | The insula is a fold of cortex tucked deep inside, and it listens to your body. It's active when you notice your heartbeat, feel nauseated, sense pain or experience disgust. Many scientists think it helps turn raw signals from your body into emotions. A gut feeling, in a literal sense, may involve your insula.||Craig, 2002
The anterior cingulate | A region that notices conflict and errors. | This region sits in the middle of the brain, curving around the front of the corpus callosum. It's a kind of conflict monitor. It becomes active when two responses compete, as when you have to name the ink color of the word "red" printed in blue. It also lights up when you realize you've made a mistake, and when you feel social pain, such as being excluded. It seems to signal that something needs attention.||Botvinick et al., 2004
The brainstem | The stalk that keeps breathing, heartbeat and alertness going. | The brainstem connects the brain to the spinal cord, and it runs the basics: breathing, heartbeat, blood pressure, and the cycle of sleeping and waking. You don't have to think about any of it. That's also why damage to the brainstem is so often fatal. A small injury here can be more serious than a much larger injury in the outer parts of the brain.||Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science
The motor homunculus | A map of the body along the surface of the brain's motor strip. | In the 1930s the neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield, operating on patients who were awake, stimulated the surface of the brain with a small electrode and noted which part of the body moved. He mapped a strip of cortex where each body part has its own patch. When drawn as a figure, it looks like a distorted little person with huge hands, lips and tongue, because those parts need the most precise control.||Penfield & Boldrey, 1937
Broca's area | A region in the left frontal lobe linked to producing speech. | In 1861 the French doctor Paul Broca treated a man nicknamed "Tan," because that was nearly the only word he could say, although he could understand speech. After the man died, Broca examined his brain and found damage in a spot in the left frontal lobe. People with damage there still understand language but speak slowly and with effort. It was one of the first clear links between a brain region and a mental function.||Broca, 1861
Wernicke's area | A region in the left temporal lobe linked to understanding language. | In 1874 the German doctor Carl Wernicke described patients who had a different problem from Broca's. They could speak fluently, but what they said made little sense, and they had trouble understanding others. Their damage was in a part of the left temporal lobe. The two language areas are linked by a bundle of fibers, and the pair showed that language depends on a network, not on one spot.||Wernicke, 1874
Split-brain patients | When the two halves are disconnected, each can act without the other knowing. | When the connection between the two hemispheres is cut, strange things happen. Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga tested these patients in the 1960s. If a word was flashed to the right hemisphere, which can't speak, the patient said they'd seen nothing, yet their left hand could pick out the matching object. Each half seemed to have its own knowledge. Sperry won a Nobel Prize in 1981 for the work.||Sperry, 1981; Gazzaniga, 2005
Phineas Gage | A railroad worker survived an iron rod through his frontal lobe in 1848. | In 1848 a railroad worker named Phineas Gage was packing explosive powder when it went off and drove an iron rod through the front of his skull. He survived, and a doctor reported that he changed from a responsible man into an impulsive, profane one. Later historians found the story had been exaggerated over time. Gage recovered much of his ability, and spent years working as a stagecoach driver, so the full tale is more complicated.||Macmillan, 2000|mixed
Adult neurogenesis | Do adults grow new neurons? | In some animals, the hippocampus keeps making new neurons through adulthood. Whether people do is hotly debated. In 2018 one team reported that new neurons in the human hippocampus stop forming in childhood, and another team in the same year found evidence that they continue into old age. The different results may come from how brain samples were handled. So the answer, for now, is that nobody is sure.||Sorrells et al., 2018; Boldrini et al., 2018|contested
Synaptic pruning | The brain trims connections it uses less. | A baby's brain has a burst of connection-building, and the number of synapses peaks in early childhood. Then, through adolescence and into early adulthood, the brain trims many of them, keeping the ones that are used. It may sound like a loss, but it makes the circuits more efficient. Pruning is a bit like trimming a hedge. What you're left with is shaped by experience.||Huttenlocher, 1979
Sleep stages | A night of sleep cycles through light, deep and REM sleep about every 90 minutes. | A night of sleep isn't one steady state. Over about 90 minutes you go from light sleep into deep sleep and then into REM sleep, and then the cycle repeats four or five times. Deep sleep is concentrated in the first half of the night, and REM sleep gets longer toward morning. That's why cutting your sleep short on the end loses most of your REM, and it may be one reason you feel foggy.|About 90 minutes|Carskadon & Dement, 2011
REM sleep | The stage where most vivid dreaming happens. | In 1953 Eugene Aserinsky, a graduate student, and his professor Nathaniel Kleitman noticed that sleepers' eyes darted back and forth under their lids at certain times in the night. When they woke people during that phase, they often reported vivid dreams. It's called rapid eye movement, or REM, sleep. In REM, your body's muscles are mostly paralyzed, which keeps you from acting out your dreams.||Aserinsky & Kleitman, 1953
The glymphatic system | The brain clears waste more during sleep. | In 2013 Maiken Nedergaard's lab at the University of Rochester reported that in mice, the spaces between brain cells expanded by about 60 percent during sleep, letting cerebrospinal fluid wash through and carry away waste, including proteins linked to Alzheimer's. It suggested that one function of sleep is cleaning house. The evidence in people is still developing, and scientists are debating the details.||Xie et al., 2013|mixed
How much sleep? | Most adults need seven or more hours a night. | In 2015 the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society issued a consensus statement based on a panel of experts reviewing the research. It said adults should regularly sleep at least seven hours a night for good health. Needs vary a little from person to person, and a few people truly do well on less. But if you rely on caffeine to get through the day, you're probably not getting enough.|7+ hours|Watson et al., 2015
Sleep loss feels like being drunk | Going without sleep impairs you like alcohol. | In studies from the late 1990s, researchers compared the effects of staying awake with the effects of alcohol. After 17 to 19 hours without sleep, people's performance on tests of reaction time and coordination was as poor as at a blood alcohol level of 0.05 percent. After 24 hours it was like 0.10 percent, over the legal driving limit in the United States. Drowsy driving is a real danger.||Williamson & Feyer, 2000; Dawson & Reid, 1997
Social jetlag | The mismatch between your body clock and the schedule you keep. | Till Roenneberg, a German chronobiologist, found that many people sleep much later on weekends than on workdays. He called the mismatch "social jetlag," because it's like flying a few time zones west on Friday night and back east on Monday morning. People with a bigger gap tend to be more tired and in a worse mood, and to have more health problems. Keeping a regular wake-up time helps.||Roenneberg et al., 2006
The body clock is not exactly 24 hours | Most people's internal day is about 24 hours and 10 minutes. | Your internal clock runs slightly longer than a day. In 1999 Charles Czeisler and colleagues kept volunteers in dim light for weeks, with no clocks and no daylight cues, and measured an average internal day of 24 hours and 11 minutes. Each morning, light resets the clock to the 24-hour day. That's why a bright morning can make you feel better, and why jet lag takes days to clear.|24.18 hours|Czeisler et al., 1999
Teens' clocks shift | During puberty the body clock moves later. | During puberty, the body clock moves later. Mary Carskadon at Brown University found that teenagers don't get sleepy until later in the evening, and naturally want to wake later in the morning, and it isn't just laziness. When schools have started later, studies have found that students got more sleep and had better attendance. A 7:30 start asks a teenager to wake at what feels like the middle of their night.||Carskadon, 2011
Rods and cones | The eye has two kinds of light sensors: rods for dim light and cones for color. | The retina of each eye has two kinds of light sensors. Rods, about 120 million of them, work in dim light but can't see color, which is why the world looks gray at night. Cones, about 6 million, work in bright light and give you color and fine detail. They're concentrated in the center of your vision, where you focus. The two systems together let us see in both starlight and sunshine.|120M and 6M|Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science
The blind spot | Each eye has a spot with no sensors where the nerve leaves the eye. | Each eye has a place where the optic nerve leaves the back of the eye, and there are no light sensors there. So each of us has a small hole in our vision. We don't notice it, because the brain fills it in using the surroundings and the other eye. You can find yours: close your left eye, look at a mark on the left side of a page with your right eye, and move the page slowly toward you until a second mark to its right disappears.||Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science
Saccades | The eyes do not glide; they jump about three times a second. | Your eyes don't glide smoothly across a scene. They hop about three times a second, in quick jumps called saccades, and stop to take in information between them. During each jump, your vision is briefly suppressed, so you don't see the blur. What feels like a smooth, continuous view is in fact built from many snapshots stitched together by your brain.|About 3 a second|Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science
Color vision | Color comes from three types of cone cells, each tuned to a range of light. | Thomas Young proposed in 1802 that the eye has three kinds of color receptors, and later experiments confirmed it. Each type of cone responds best to a different range of light, roughly red, green and blue, and your brain compares their signals to give you the full range of colors. About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have a form of color blindness, because the genes involved are on the X chromosome.|1 in 12 men|Young, 1802; Helmholtz
The cochlea | A snail-shaped organ where sound becomes nerve signals. | The cochlea, a coiled tube in the inner ear that looks like a snail shell, is where sound turns into nerve signals. It's lined with thousands of tiny hair cells, each tuned to a certain pitch. In humans, once those cells are destroyed, they don't grow back, which is why damage from loud noise is permanent. Young people can hear from about 20 to 20,000 hertz, and the upper range shrinks with age.||Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science
Protecting your hearing | Sounds at 85 decibels or above can harm hearing over hours. | Sounds at 85 decibels, about as loud as heavy city traffic, can damage hearing if you hear them for hours. For every 3 decibels louder, the safe time is cut in half. So a rock concert at 100 decibels is safe for only about 15 minutes without protection. Hearing loss from noise builds up silently and permanently, and a pair of cheap foam earplugs makes a real difference.|85 dB|NIOSH, 1998
The five tastes | The tongue detects sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. | Your tongue detects five basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. The Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda identified umami in 1908, when he worked out what made seaweed broth so savory. It's the taste of glutamate, found in broth, cheese and ripe tomatoes. You may have seen a "tongue map" with different zones for each taste. It's a myth, and every area of the tongue can detect all five.||Ikeda, 1908; Chandrashekar et al., 2006
Smell and memory | Smell has a quick route to the brain's memory and emotion centers. | The sense of smell has a more direct route to the brain's emotional and memory areas than other senses. Scent signals reach the amygdala and hippocampus after only a couple of connections. That may be why a smell, like a perfume or a kitchen, can suddenly bring back a vivid scene from childhood. Studies find that odor-evoked memories tend to be more emotional, though they aren't necessarily more accurate.||Herz & Engen, 1996|mixed
The one-trillion-smells claim | A 2014 study said people can tell apart over a trillion odors. | In 2014 a study in Science claimed that humans can tell apart more than a trillion different odors, a number that got a lot of press. Other scientists looked at the math and said the conclusion didn't follow from the data, and the true number could be far lower. We still don't know how many smells people can really distinguish.||Bushdid et al., 2014; Gerkin & Castro, 2015|contested
The gate control theory of pain | The spinal cord can turn pain signals up or down before they reach the brain. | In 1965 Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall proposed that the spinal cord has a kind of gate that can let pain signals through or block them. Signals from touch, for example, can close the gate, which is why rubbing a bumped elbow helps. Signals from the brain can also open or close it, so your attention, mood and expectations change how much a given injury hurts.||Melzack & Wall, 1965
Phantom limbs | People who lose a limb can still feel it. | After an amputation, many people go on feeling the missing limb, sometimes with pain. V. S. Ramachandran invented a mirror box in the 1990s. A patient puts the intact hand in one side so that its reflection looks like the missing one, then moves it, and some felt relief from the pain. Later trials have had mixed results, so it's an interesting idea that works for some people and not others.||Ramachandran & Rogers-Ramachandran, 1996|mixed
Proprioception | Your sense of where your body is without looking. | Close your eyes and touch your nose. You can do it because of a sense you rarely notice, proprioception, which tells you where your limbs are. In 1906 the physiologist Charles Sherrington gave it the name. Stretch sensors in your muscles, tendons and joints send constant information to the brain. People who lose it, from nerve disease, have to watch their limbs to move them at all.||Sherrington, 1906
The vestibular system | The inner-ear sensors for balance and head motion. | Deep in each inner ear are three fluid-filled loops that sense rotation of the head, and two small organs that sense tilt and acceleration. Together they're the vestibular system, which keeps you balanced and your gaze steady as you move. Motion sickness happens when the signals from this system don't match what your eyes see, as when you read in a moving car, so your brain gets conflicting reports.||Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science
The heart | It beats about 100,000 times a day. | Your heart beats about 100,000 times a day without a break. At rest it pumps about five liters of blood around the body each minute, and that's roughly all the blood you have, so a drop of blood makes a complete trip in about a minute. Over a long lifetime it will beat more than 2.5 billion times. It's a muscle, and regular exercise makes it stronger and more efficient.|About 100,000|Guyton & Hall, Textbook of Medical Physiology
The skin | The body's largest organ. | Skin is the body's largest organ, covering about two square meters in an adult. It keeps water in and germs out, helps regulate your temperature, makes vitamin D when exposed to sunlight, and gives you the sense of touch. The outer layer constantly sheds and replaces itself, about once a month, so some of the dust in a home is shed skin.|About 2 m²|Guyton & Hall, Textbook of Medical Physiology
The liver | The only internal organ that can regrow. | The liver can do something no other internal organ does: regrow. If part of it is removed, as in surgery or in a living donation, the remainder can grow back to nearly the original size within weeks. It's also a chemical workhorse that filters the blood, makes bile for digesting fat, stores sugar and breaks down drugs and toxins. It's why living-donor liver transplants are possible.||Michalopoulos & DeFrances, 1997
The kidneys | Two organs that filter about 180 liters of blood fluid a day. | Your two kidneys filter about 180 liters of fluid from your blood each day, far more than the volume of fluid in the body. Almost all of it is reclaimed, leaving about 1.5 liters to leave as urine. The kidneys keep the balance of salts and water just right, and help control blood pressure. People can live a healthy life with just one, which is why living kidney donation works.|About 180 L|Guyton & Hall, Textbook of Medical Physiology
The skeleton | An adult has 206 bones; a baby has about 300. | An adult has 206 bones, but a newborn has around 300. Many of a baby's bones fuse together as it grows, such as the separate plates of the skull and the bones at the base of the spine. Bone isn't dead. It's living tissue that constantly rebuilds itself, with old bone absorbed and new bone laid down, and it gets stronger with weight-bearing exercise.|206|Gray's Anatomy
Muscles | A muscle can only pull, never push. | A muscle can only contract. It can pull, but it can never push. That's why muscles work in pairs on either side of a joint: one bends your elbow and its partner straightens it. The body has more than 600 muscles. The heart is a special kind, which works without ever stopping. Moving the body well is a matter of timing and coordination between them.||Gray's Anatomy
The gut microbiome | The body holds about as many bacteria as human cells. | The human body hosts trillions of microbes, mostly bacteria in the gut. For years, it was said that they outnumbered our own cells ten to one. In 2016 Ron Sender, Shai Fuchs and Ron Milo recalculated and found about 38 trillion bacteria and about 30 trillion human cells, closer to even. They help digest food, make some vitamins and train the immune system, and scientists are still learning how much they affect health.|38 trillion|Sender, Fuchs & Milo, 2016
The gut-brain axis | The gut and brain send signals both ways, mainly through the vagus nerve. | Your gut and your brain are in constant conversation, mostly along the vagus nerve, a long nerve that runs between them. It's why stress can upset your stomach and why anxiety can make your gut feel tight. In animal studies, changes in gut bacteria change behavior. How much this affects human mood is still being worked out, and claims made for probiotics are ahead of the evidence.||Mayer, 2011|mixed
Innate and adaptive immunity | The body has a fast, general defense and a slower, specific one. | The immune system has two parts. The innate part is fast and general: within hours, cells such as macrophages attack anything that looks foreign. The adaptive part is slower but precise. T cells and B cells learn the particular shape of a germ over days, and make antibodies. Afterward, memory cells remember it, sometimes for decades, which is the principle behind vaccines.||Janeway's Immunobiology
Fever | A raised temperature is a defense, not just a symptom. | A fever feels miserable, but it's part of the body's defense. A higher temperature slows the growth of many germs and helps your immune cells work faster. Treating a mild fever is mainly for comfort, and it isn't always necessary. A very high fever, or any fever in a young infant, is a reason to see a doctor, since it can signal a serious infection.||Evans et al., 2015
Boosting your immune system | "Boosting" immunity is not a scientific idea. | You see "immune boosting" on supplement labels, but scientifically it isn't a meaningful idea. The immune system isn't a muscle that gets stronger the more it's stimulated. It's a balance, and an overactive one is what causes allergies and autoimmune diseases. What actually supports it is plain: enough sleep, regular exercise, a balanced diet, vaccination, not smoking and managing stress.||Harvard Health, 2020
Allergies | The immune system reacts to a harmless thing as if it were dangerous. | An allergy is when the immune system treats something harmless, such as pollen or peanut, as a threat and produces antibodies called IgE against it. The next exposure triggers sneezing, itching or, in serious cases, anaphylaxis. For years parents were told to keep peanuts away from babies. In 2015 the LEAP trial showed the opposite: giving peanut foods early to infants at high risk cut their chance of a peanut allergy by about 80 percent.||Du Toit et al., 2015
Insulin | The hormone that moves sugar from blood into cells. | Insulin is the hormone that tells your cells to take up sugar from the blood. In 1921 Frederick Banting and Charles Best, working in a Toronto lab, isolated it, and in January 1922 a 14-year-old boy named Leonard Thompson, who was dying of type 1 diabetes, got the first injection. Within a year, people who would have died within months were being kept alive. It's one of the great stories of modern medicine.|1921|Banting & Best, 1922
Leptin | A hormone from fat cells that tells the brain how much energy is stored. | In 1994 Jeffrey Friedman's team at Rockefeller University discovered leptin, a hormone made by fat cells that signals to the brain how much energy is stored. Mice that lack it eat constantly and become very obese, and so do rare people with the same genetic fault. Most people with obesity, however, have high levels of leptin and the brain has become less responsive to it. It showed that body weight is regulated by hormones.||Zhang et al., Nature, 1994
Your DNA | About 3 billion letters long, and 99.9% the same as anyone else's. | The human genome is about 3 billion letters long, and any two people share about 99.9 percent of them. There are around 20,000 genes that code for proteins, but they make up only 1 or 2 percent of the sequence. The rest includes regulatory regions and a lot we're still learning about. Small differences in those letters help explain why people differ in traits and in disease risk.|99.9%|Human Genome Project, 2003
