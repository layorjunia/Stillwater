# World history: turning points, with the dates that are well established.

The first writing | Writing began in Sumer, in what is now Iraq, around 3400 to 3200 BCE. | Writing began in Sumer, in what is now southern Iraq, around 3400 to 3200 BCE. It seems to have started out as a bookkeeping tool, with little clay tokens and marks pressed into clay to keep track of grain, sheep and beer. Over the centuries, the marks became wedge-shaped signs, called cuneiform, that could stand for sounds. Then people could write names, laws, letters and stories, and the oldest known literature was born.|c. 3300 BCE|Schmandt-Besserat; Britannica
The wheel | The earliest known wheels turned pots, then carts, about 5,500 years ago. | The first wheels weren't for carts. They turned the potter's wheel, and wheeled vehicles appear a little later, around 3500 BCE. A wheel and axle found in the Ljubljana marshes in Slovenia has been dated to about 3150 BCE, among the oldest known. It seems odd that the wheel came well after boats and plows, but it's actually hard: the axle and the hole have to fit very closely, and that takes skilled work.|c. 3500 BCE|Gasser & Velušček, 2003
The start of farming | People began planting crops about 11,000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent. | About 11,000 years ago, in a region of the Middle East called the Fertile Crescent, people began to plant seeds on purpose. Wheat, barley, lentils and peas were among the first crops. Farming let people stay in one place, store food and support larger numbers, and that led to villages, then cities, kings, and writing. It also brought new problems, such as crowded living and disease.|c. 9500 BCE|Standard archaeology text
Göbekli Tepe | A temple of carved stone pillars, built about 11,500 years ago, before farming. | In 1994 the German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt began to dig on a hilltop in southeastern Turkey, and found huge T-shaped stone pillars carved with animals, arranged in circles. It was built about 11,500 years ago, before farming, and before pottery. It overturned the idea that people first settled down in towns and only then built monuments. Perhaps the need to gather for such a place helped to start settled life.|c. 9500 BCE|Schmidt, 2006
The Great Pyramid | Built around 2560 BCE for King Khufu, it was the tallest human-made structure for nearly 4,000 years. | The Great Pyramid of Giza was built around 2560 BCE as the tomb of the Egyptian king Khufu. It stood about 146 meters high, and it was the tallest human-made structure in the world for nearly 4,000 years, until the spire of Lincoln Cathedral in England surpassed it in the 1300s. The workers weren't slaves, as people used to think. Archaeologists found their town nearby, with bakeries and graves, which shows they were paid and fed.|c. 2560 BCE|Lehner, 1997
The Code of Hammurabi | A Babylonian king set out 282 laws on a stone pillar around 1754 BCE. | Around 1754 BCE the king of Babylon, Hammurabi, had 282 laws carved on a tall black stone pillar, so that everyone could see them. It covered trade, property, family and crime. Punishments depended on the social rank of the victim and the offender, and many were based on "an eye for an eye." It's one of the oldest long written law codes, and it reflects the idea that the king's role is to ensure justice.|c. 1754 BCE|Roth, 1995
The alphabet | Most of the world's alphabets descend from one invented about 3,800 years ago. | Most of the world's alphabets descend from a single invention. Around 1800 BCE, workers from Canaan in Egypt's Sinai turquoise mines adapted a few Egyptian hieroglyphs into a simple script, with a sign for each sound. The Phoenicians spread it around the Mediterranean, the Greeks added vowels, and the Romans passed their version on to us. A system of about two dozen signs was far easier to learn than hundreds.|c. 1800 BCE|Standard history of writing
The Rosetta Stone | A slab with the same decree in three scripts unlocked Egyptian hieroglyphs. | For 1,400 years, nobody could read Egyptian hieroglyphs. In 1799 French soldiers digging near the town of Rashid, or Rosetta, found a stone slab carved with the same decree in three scripts: hieroglyphs, a cursive Egyptian script and ancient Greek. It dates from 196 BCE. Using the Greek as a key, the French scholar Jean-François Champollion announced in 1822 that he could read hieroglyphs.|1799|Champollion, 1822
The first Olympic Games | The ancient Games began in 776 BCE, by tradition, in Olympia, Greece. | By tradition, the first Olympic Games were held in 776 BCE at Olympia in Greece, in honor of Zeus. They took place every four years, and included foot races, wrestling, boxing and chariot races. A sacred truce was declared so that athletes could travel safely, even when cities were at war. Only free Greek men were allowed to compete, and the Games continued for more than a thousand years.|776 BCE|Standard classical history
Athenian democracy | In 508 BCE Cleisthenes gave Athenian citizens a vote in an assembly. | In 508 BCE the Athenian leader Cleisthenes reformed the government, giving citizens a vote in an assembly that made the laws. Any citizen could speak and vote. But citizens were only free adult men born in Athens, perhaps a fifth of the population. Women, slaves and foreigners were left out. It was a limited democracy, but it introduced the idea that ordinary people should decide.|508 BCE|Aristotle, Constitution of the Athenians
Marathon | The Athenians beat a larger Persian army at Marathon in 490 BCE. | In 490 BCE the Athenians defeated a much larger Persian army at the plain of Marathon, one of the most famous battles in history. The popular story that a runner raced 26 miles to Athens with the news, shouting "Victory!" and then dropped dead, is a much later legend. What's better documented is that a runner named Pheidippides ran about 150 miles to Sparta to ask for help. The modern marathon race comes from the legend.|490 BCE|Herodotus, Histories|mixed
Alexander the Great | He built an empire from Greece to India by age 32. | Alexander became king of Macedon at the age of 20, in 336 BCE, after his father was murdered. In just over a decade he conquered the Persian Empire, defeating King Darius, and led his army as far as the Indus River in India by 326 BCE. His soldiers refused to go further. He died in Babylon in 323 BCE, at 32, and his generals divided up the empire. It spread Greek culture across much of Asia.|356–323 BCE|Arrian, Anabasis
The First Emperor of China | Qin Shi Huang unified China in 221 BCE. | In 221 BCE King Zheng of Qin finished conquering the other states of China and declared himself Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor. He standardized writing, weights and measures, and even the width of cart axles. His tomb is guarded by thousands of life-size clay soldiers, the Terracotta Army, which was discovered in 1974 by farmers digging a well.|221 BCE|Sima Qian, Records of the Grand Historian
The Great Wall and space | The Great Wall of China cannot be seen from the Moon. | One of the most popular facts is that the Great Wall of China is visible from the Moon, and it's not true. Astronauts say it's barely visible, if at all, even from low Earth orbit, because it's narrow and is similar in color to the land around it. The claim appeared in print in the 1930s, decades before anyone went into space.||Standard reference
The Roman Republic | Rome threw out its last king in 509 BCE and was governed by elected officials. | According to tradition, the Romans overthrew their last king in 509 BCE and set up a republic, in which leaders were elected. Two consuls shared power for a year, and the Senate advised them. The Republic lasted nearly 500 years, as Rome grew from a city into the ruler of the Mediterranean. Then a series of civil wars gave power to one man, Augustus, in 27 BCE.|509–27 BCE|Livy, Polybius
The Ides of March | Julius Caesar was killed by senators on March 15, 44 BCE. | On March 15, 44 BCE, the Ides of March in the Roman calendar, Julius Caesar was stabbed to death in the Senate by a group of about 60 senators. They hoped to save the Republic from a man who'd had himself made dictator for life. But the killing led to another civil war, and within 17 years to the rule of Caesar's adopted heir, Augustus, as the first emperor.|44 BCE|Plutarch, Suetonius
The Pax Romana | About 200 years of relative peace within the Roman Empire. | The "Roman Peace" was a period of about 200 years, from Augustus in 27 BCE to the death of Marcus Aurelius in 180 CE, when the Roman Empire was relatively stable. Trade and travel flourished, with good roads and shipping across the Mediterranean. The peace was kept by large armies on the borders, and the people conquered by Rome didn't always feel peaceful. But it was a time of prosperity.|27 BCE–180 CE|Standard Roman history
The Silk Road | A network of trade routes linking China, India, Persia and Rome. | The Silk Road wasn't one road but a network of trade routes linking China, India, Persia and Rome. The Han emperor sent an envoy, Zhang Qian, west around 138 BCE to find allies, and that opened up the routes. Silk, paper, spices and ideas traveled along them, as did religions. So did diseases. Most traders only carried goods part of the way, passing them to others.|c. 130 BCE|Sima Qian
The Library of Alexandria | It did not burn down in one fire. | The popular story is that the great Library of Alexandria in Egypt was destroyed in a single great fire. In fact, it declined over centuries. Funding was cut, scholars were driven out in political purges, and a number of fires damaged parts of it, including one set by Julius Caesar's troops in 48 BCE that burned some storage warehouses. Historians reject the idea of one dramatic loss.||Bagnall, 2002|mixed
Paper | China had paper by the 2nd century CE, and it took 1,000 years to reach Europe. | The Chinese had paper by the 2nd century CE, and it was a thousand years before Europe took it up. The court official Cai Lun is traditionally credited with inventing it around 105 CE, but archaeologists have found older fragments. Arab papermakers learned the craft after 751, at the Battle of Talas, according to tradition, and it reached Europe in the 1100s, through Spain. Paper made printing and widespread literacy affordable.|c. 105 CE|Tsien, 1985
Vesuvius | Mount Vesuvius erupted in 79 CE and buried Pompeii and Herculaneum. | In 79 CE Mount Vesuvius erupted and buried the Roman towns of Pompeii and Herculaneum. A young man named Pliny the Younger watched from across the bay and described the towering cloud in two letters to the historian Tacitus. The ash preserved homes, shops, loaves of bread, paintings and graffiti so well that we can read about daily life in a Roman town.|79 CE|Pliny the Younger, Letters
The Council of Nicaea | In 325 CE, bishops gathered in Nicaea to settle a dispute about the nature of Christ. | In 325 CE the Roman emperor Constantine called bishops from across the empire to Nicaea, in what's now Turkey, to settle a dispute over the nature of Christ: whether the Son was created or eternal. The council produced an early form of the Nicene Creed, which affirms that the Son is of one substance with the Father. A revised version is still recited in many churches today.|325|Eusebius; Nicene Creed
The fall of Western Rome | The last Western Roman emperor was deposed in 476. | In 476 the Germanic general Odoacer removed the teenage emperor Romulus Augustulus, and didn't bother to replace him. That date is traditionally given as the end of the Western Roman Empire. Historians now see it as a slow change over about a century, not a sudden collapse, as barbarian kingdoms took over provinces. The Eastern Roman Empire, centered on Constantinople, continued until 1453.|476|Standard Roman history
Hagia Sophia | The great church in Constantinople was built in only five years. | The great church of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople, now Istanbul, was built in only five years, from 532 to 537, for the emperor Justinian. Its enormous dome, about 55 meters high, seems to float on a ring of windows. For almost a thousand years it was one of the largest enclosed spaces in the world. It was later a mosque, then a museum, and in 2020 became a mosque again.|537|Procopius
The Hijra | The year 622 is year 1 of the Islamic calendar. | The Islamic calendar counts years from 622 CE, the year of the Hijra, when the Prophet Muhammad and his followers moved from Mecca to Medina after persecution. The calendar is lunar, with twelve months of 29 or 30 days, so the year is about 11 days shorter than the solar year. This means the Islamic months, like Ramadan, move through the seasons.|622|Standard Islamic history
The House of Wisdom | A center of scholarship in Baghdad in the 800s and 900s. | In ninth-century Baghdad, the caliphs supported a center of scholarship known as the House of Wisdom. Scholars there translated Greek, Persian and Indian works into Arabic, and added their own discoveries in mathematics, astronomy and medicine. Al-Khwarizmi, who gave us the word algebra, worked there. Many ancient texts, including some by Aristotle and Galen, survived because they were translated.|9th century|Gutas, 1998
Charlemagne | The Frankish king was crowned Emperor in Rome on Christmas Day, 800. | On Christmas Day in the year 800, Pope Leo III crowned the Frankish king Charlemagne as Emperor in Rome. He ruled much of western Europe, and promoted education, setting up schools in monasteries. His scribes also developed a clear and legible handwriting style, Carolingian minuscule, which later became the model for our lowercase letters.|800|Einhard, Life of Charlemagne
The Vikings in America | Norse explorers reached Newfoundland around the year 1000. | About 500 years before Columbus, Norse explorers reached North America. At L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, archaeologists found the remains of buildings, and wood that had been cut with metal tools, which the locals didn't have. In 2021 scientists used a solar storm recorded in tree rings to date the cutting precisely, to the year 1021. It's the earliest known European presence in the Americas.|1021|Kuitems et al., Nature, 2021
The Norman Conquest | William of Normandy won the Battle of Hastings on October 14, 1066. | On October 14, 1066, William, Duke of Normandy, defeated the English King Harold at the Battle of Hastings. Harold was killed, perhaps by an arrow in the eye, as the Bayeux Tapestry seems to show, though that's debated. French became the language of England's rulers for around 300 years, and English absorbed thousands of French words, which is why we have both "cow" and "beef."|1066|Bayeux Tapestry; Anglo-Saxon Chronicle
Magna Carta | In 1215 English barons forced King John to accept limits on his power. | In 1215 a group of English barons forced King John to put his seal to a charter limiting his power. It promised, among other things, that no free man could be punished except by the lawful judgment of his peers or the law of the land. Most clauses later became obsolete. But the idea that even a king is under the law influenced many later constitutions, including the American one.|1215|Holt, 1992
Genghis Khan | He founded the Mongol Empire in 1206, the largest connected land empire in history. | Temüjin united the Mongol tribes and in 1206 took the title Genghis Khan, "universal ruler." He and his successors built the largest connected land empire in history. Under his grandsons, it stretched from Korea to Hungary. It also protected trade along the Silk Road and spread ideas and technologies, though it was founded on terrible violence, and may have helped to spread the plague.|1206|Weatherford, 2004
Mansa Musa | The ruler of Mali whose pilgrimage in 1324 made gold cheaper in Cairo. | Mansa Musa ruled the West African empire of Mali in the early 1300s, and was famously wealthy. In 1324 he traveled to Mecca with thousands of attendants and camels laden with gold. He gave away so much in Cairo that the price of gold there fell for years. Back in Mali, the city of Timbuktu became a renowned center of learning, with libraries and a university.|1324|Ibn Khaldun; al-Umari
The Black Death | A plague that killed perhaps a third or more of Europe between 1347 and 1351. | The plague that swept through Europe between 1347 and 1351 killed perhaps a third or more of the population. It came along trade routes from Asia, spread by fleas on rats, and by people. With so many dead, workers were scarce, wages rose and the old feudal order, in which peasants were tied to the land, weakened. It took Europe about two centuries to recover its population.|1347–1351|Benedictow, 2004
The printing press | Johannes Gutenberg's movable type, around 1450, made books cheap. | Around 1450 the German goldsmith Johannes Gutenberg developed a way to print with movable metal type. Before that, books had to be copied by hand, and a Bible might take a scribe months. His famous Bible appeared around 1455. By 1500, more than 20 million books had been printed in Europe. Books became cheap, and ideas, from science to religion, could spread quickly.|c. 1450|Eisenstein, 1979
The fall of Constantinople | The Ottoman Turks took the capital of the Byzantine Empire on May 29, 1453. | On May 29, 1453, the Ottoman Sultan Mehmed II captured Constantinople, the capital of the Byzantine Empire, after a siege of 53 days. His army used huge cannons to batter the ancient walls. The city became the Ottoman capital, Istanbul. Greek scholars who fled west carried manuscripts with them, which helped to feed the Italian Renaissance.|1453|Runciman, 1965
Columbus | In 1492 Christopher Columbus crossed the Atlantic and reached the Caribbean. | In 1492 the Italian navigator Christopher Columbus, sailing for Spain, crossed the Atlantic and landed in the Caribbean. He believed he'd reached the edge of Asia. Millions of people already lived in the Americas, and his arrival began lasting contact between the hemispheres, which brought exchanges of crops and ideas, but also disease, conquest and enslavement for many Indigenous peoples.|1492|Standard history
Magellan's voyage | The first trip around the world, 1519 to 1522. | In 1519 five ships under Ferdinand Magellan left Spain to find a westward route to the Spice Islands. Magellan was killed in a battle in the Philippines in 1521. Only one ship, the Victoria, under Juan Sebastián Elcano, completed the journey around the world in 1522, with 18 men of the original crew of about 270. It proved that the world could be circumnavigated.|1519–1522|Pigafetta, journal
The Columbian exchange | After 1492, plants, animals and diseases crossed the Atlantic and changed both sides. | After 1492, plants, animals and diseases crossed the Atlantic in both directions, and changed both sides of the ocean. Potatoes, tomatoes, maize and chocolate went to the Old World, and horses, wheat and smallpox went to the New. The historian Alfred Crosby named the process in 1972. Many of the foods that we think of as typical of European countries, like Italian tomatoes, came from the Americas.||Crosby, 1972
The fall of the Aztecs | Hernán Cortés and his allies captured Tenochtitlan in 1521. | In 1521 Hernán Cortés and his forces captured Tenochtitlan, the Aztec capital, now Mexico City. The Spaniards didn't do it alone. Thousands of Indigenous allies, who resented Aztec rule, fought with them, and smallpox, which the Europeans had brought, killed a huge part of the city's people. The conquest had many causes, not just Spanish weapons and horses.|1521|Restall, Seven Myths of the Spanish Conquest (2003)
Luther's 95 Theses | In 1517 Martin Luther challenged the sale of indulgences. | In 1517 Martin Luther, a German monk and professor, wrote a list of 95 arguments against the sale of indulgences, which were pardons that people bought to reduce punishment for sin. According to tradition, he posted them on the church door at Wittenberg. The printing press carried his ideas across Germany within weeks. The Reformation that followed split Western Christianity, and Luther later translated the Bible into German.|1517|Luther, 1517
The King James Bible | An English translation completed in 1611 that shaped the language. | The King James Version of the Bible was completed in 1611 by about 47 scholars, working from Hebrew and Greek texts. It built heavily on the earlier English translation by William Tyndale, who was executed in 1536. Its rhythmic language shaped English literature and speech, giving us phrases like "the powers that be" and "the skin of my teeth."|1611|Daniell, The Bible in English (2003)
Machu Picchu | The Inca citadel in Peru was built around 1450 and abandoned about a century later. | The Inca citadel of Machu Picchu, high in the Andes of Peru, was probably built around 1450 as an estate for the emperor Pachacuti. It was abandoned about a century later, around the time of the Spanish conquest, and the Spaniards apparently never found it. In 1911 the American explorer Hiram Bingham brought it to world attention, although local farmers had known about it all along.|c. 1450|Burger & Salazar, 2004
The Peace of Westphalia | The treaties of 1648 ended the Thirty Years' War and set up the idea of sovereign states. | The treaties signed in 1648 in the German region of Westphalia ended the Thirty Years' War, one of the most destructive wars in European history, which killed perhaps a quarter of the people in the German lands. The agreements said that each ruler would control his own territory, without outside interference, an idea that's often seen as the beginning of the modern system of sovereign states.|1648|Standard European history
The Great Fire of London | The 1666 fire destroyed about 13,000 homes, but only six deaths were officially recorded. | In September 1666 a fire that began in a baker's shop in Pudding Lane burned for four days and destroyed about 13,000 houses and 87 churches, including St Paul's Cathedral. Only six deaths were officially recorded, though the true number may have been higher, since the poor weren't always counted. The disaster led to new building rules, and to the first fire insurance companies.|1666|Porter, 2008
Social contract thinkers | Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau asked why we obey governments. | Why should we obey a government? Three philosophers gave different answers. In 1651 Thomas Hobbes argued that people give up some of their freedom in return for safety. In 1689 John Locke said that government exists to protect people's rights, and can be replaced if it fails to. In 1762 Jean-Jacques Rousseau said that legitimate government must express the general will of the people.|1651–1762|Hobbes, Leviathan; Locke, Two Treatises
Montesquieu's separation of powers | He argued in 1748 that power should be divided among separate branches. | In 1748 the French thinker Montesquieu published The Spirit of the Laws, in which he argued that liberty is safest when government power is divided among separate branches, so that each can check the others. His idea strongly influenced the framers of the US Constitution, who divided the government into executive, legislative and judicial branches.|1748|Montesquieu, 1748
What is Enlightenment? | Immanuel Kant wrote in 1784: "Dare to know." | In 1784 the German philosopher Immanuel Kant answered the question in a short essay. He said that enlightenment is people's emergence from their self-imposed immaturity, which he defined as the inability to use one's own understanding without someone else's guidance. His motto was "Sapere aude," or "Dare to know," a phrase that he took from the Roman poet Horace.|1784|Kant, 1784
The Declaration of Independence | Adopted on July 4, 1776, it said "all men are created equal." | On July 4, 1776, the Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence, which stated that "all men are created equal." Thomas Jefferson wrote most of the text, and Congress edited it. At the time, the words didn't extend to enslaved people or to women. Later movements, from abolitionists to suffragists to civil rights leaders, used them to demand that the promise be kept.|1776|Declaration of Independence
The US Constitution | Written in Philadelphia in the summer of 1787. | In the summer of 1787 delegates met in Philadelphia, in secret, to write a new framework of government for the United States. They debated for about four months, and the final document is only about 4,500 words long. It has been amended 27 times, with the first ten amendments, known as the Bill of Rights, added in 1791. It's the oldest written national constitution still in use.|1787|Standard US history
The French Revolution | In 1789 the Bastille fell and the Declaration of the Rights of Man was adopted. | On July 14, 1789, a Paris crowd stormed the Bastille, a royal prison and a symbol of tyranny, and the French Revolution began. Later that summer, the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen set out ideas of equality and liberty. The revolution overthrew the monarchy and later descended into the Terror, and then the rule of Napoleon. It spread ideas of equal citizenship and national sovereignty across Europe.|1789|Standard European history
The Haitian Revolution | Enslaved people in Saint-Domingue overthrew French rule and founded Haiti in 1804. | In 1791 enslaved people in the French colony of Saint-Domingue rose up, in what became the only successful slave revolt in history. Led first by Toussaint Louverture, and after his capture in 1802 by Jean-Jacques Dessalines, they defeated French, Spanish and British forces. Haiti declared independence on January 1, 1804, becoming the first Black-led republic and the second independent nation in the Americas.|1791–1804|James, The Black Jacobins (1938)
Waterloo | Napoleon was defeated on June 18, 1815 in present-day Belgium. | On June 18, 1815, near the village of Waterloo in what is now Belgium, Napoleon's army was defeated by British and allied forces under the Duke of Wellington and by Prussian forces under Marshal Blücher. It ended 23 years of nearly constant war in Europe. Napoleon was exiled to the remote island of St. Helena, in the South Atlantic, where he died in 1821.|1815|Standard European history
Watt's steam engine | James Watt's 1769 improvements made steam practical for factories. | Steam engines existed before James Watt, but they wasted most of their heat. In 1769 Watt, a Scottish instrument maker, patented a design with a separate condenser, which made the engine far more efficient. With the help of his business partner Matthew Boulton, steam power spread to mills, mines and later to railways and ships, and powered the Industrial Revolution.|1769|Watt, 1769
The first public railway | The Stockton and Darlington Railway opened in 1825. | The Stockton and Darlington Railway in northern England opened in 1825, using George Stephenson's steam locomotive to haul coal and passengers. Within a few decades, railways spread across the world. By 1850 Britain had about 6,000 miles of track. Trains made it necessary to standardize time, since each town had previously kept its own local time.|1825|Standard history of technology
Ending the slave trade | Britain banned its slave trade in 1807 and slavery in its empire in 1833. | In 1807 Britain banned its involvement in the transatlantic slave trade, and in 1833 it abolished slavery throughout its empire. The United States abolished it with the 13th Amendment in 1865. Campaigners like William Wilberforce, Olaudah Equiano and Thomas Clarkson used pamphlets, petitions and a boycott of slave-grown sugar to turn public opinion, and the enslaved people themselves resisted constantly.|1807–1865|Hochschild, Bury the Chains (2005)
Mill on liberty | John Stuart Mill argued in 1859 that society may limit your freedom only to stop you harming others. | In his 1859 book On Liberty, the English philosopher John Stuart Mill argued that society may justly limit a person's freedom only to prevent harm to others. A person's own good, he said, isn't enough reason to force them. He defended free speech even for opinions that are wrong, because a false idea may contain some truth, and because truth that's never challenged becomes a dead dogma.|1859|Mill, On Liberty (1859)
Wollstonecraft | Mary Wollstonecraft argued in 1792 that women should have the same education as men. | In 1792 Mary Wollstonecraft published A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, in which she argued that women appear to be less rational than men only because they're denied an education. She called for girls and boys to be taught the same things. It was widely criticized at the time, and later became one of the founding texts of feminism.|1792|Wollstonecraft, 1792
The telegraph | The first message, "What hath God wrought," was sent in 1844. | On May 24, 1844, Samuel Morse sent a message from the Capitol in Washington to Baltimore, 40 miles away: "What hath God wrought." Until then, news could travel only as fast as a horse, a train or a ship. The telegraph made communication almost instantaneous. By 1866, a cable laid under the Atlantic connected America and Europe.|1844|Morse, 1844
The telephone and the light bulb | Bell's patent was granted in 1876, and Edison's practical bulb in 1879. | Alexander Graham Bell was granted a patent for the telephone in 1876, on the same day that another inventor, Elisha Gray, filed a notice for a similar device. In 1879 Thomas Edison perfected a practical electric light bulb with a long-lasting filament, and the British inventor Joseph Swan had made a working bulb at about the same time. Inventions often arrive in more than one place at once.|1876, 1879|US Patent 174,465
The Red Cross | A Swiss businessman, Henry Dunant, inspired it after seeing a battlefield in 1859. | In 1859 a Swiss businessman, Henry Dunant, happened to be near the battlefield of Solferino in Italy, and was horrified by the thousands of wounded left without help. He wrote a book about it, and in 1863 helped to found the Red Cross. The first Geneva Convention in 1864 agreed that the wounded and those who care for them should be protected in war.|1863|Dunant, 1862
Women's right to vote | New Zealand was the first country to give women the vote, in 1893. | In 1893 New Zealand became the first self-governing country to give women the vote in national elections. Others followed slowly. In the United States, the 19th Amendment gave women the vote in 1920, and in France, women could not vote until 1944. Suffragists campaigned with petitions, marches and, sometimes, hunger strikes in prison.|1893|Standard history
The Wright brothers | On December 17, 1903, the first powered flight lasted 12 seconds. | On December 17, 1903, at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, Orville Wright made the first powered, controlled flight in a heavier-than-air machine. It lasted 12 seconds and covered 120 feet. Later that day, Wilbur flew 852 feet in 59 seconds. Just 66 years afterward, humans walked on the Moon. The brothers were bicycle makers from Ohio.|12 seconds|Library of Congress
The Titanic | The "unsinkable" liner struck an iceberg on April 14, 1912. | The luxury liner Titanic, widely described as unsinkable, hit an iceberg on the night of April 14, 1912, on its first voyage, and sank in under three hours. About 1,500 of the 2,200 people aboard died, partly because there were lifeboats for only about half of them. After the disaster, new rules required ships to carry enough lifeboats for everyone and to keep radios staffed around the clock.|1912|British Wreck Commissioner's inquiry
World War I | The war of 1914 to 1918 killed about 20 million people. | The Great War lasted from 1914 to 1918 and killed about 20 million people, soldiers and civilians. It began after the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria in Sarajevo on June 28, 1914, and drew in the great powers through a web of alliances. It saw the first large-scale use of tanks, poison gas and aircraft in war, and trench warfare that bogged down fronts for years.|1914–1918|Standard history
The Russian Revolution | In 1917 two revolutions ended the tsar's rule and brought the Bolsheviks to power. | In 1917 two revolutions overthrew Russia's government. In February, strikes and protests forced Tsar Nicholas II to abdicate. In October, the Bolsheviks under Vladimir Lenin seized power from a weak provisional government. A bitter civil war followed, and in 1922 the Soviet Union was formally founded. It would last nearly 70 years and shape the 20th century.|1917|Standard history
The 1918 flu pandemic | The flu of 1918 to 1920 killed at least 50 million people worldwide. | The influenza pandemic of 1918 to 1920 killed at least 50 million people worldwide, more than the First World War, and about a third of the world's population caught it. It's called the Spanish flu only because Spain, neutral in the war, reported freely on it, while other countries censored the news. Unusually, it killed many healthy young adults.|50 million+|CDC; Johnson & Mueller, 2002
The Great Depression | The US stock market crashed in October 1929 and a decade of hardship followed. | In October 1929 the US stock market crashed, and the world sank into the worst economic downturn of the 20th century. By 1933 about a quarter of American workers were unemployed, and thousands of banks failed. President Franklin Roosevelt's New Deal brought programs such as Social Security, bank deposit insurance and new labor laws, many of which remain in place.|1929|Standard US history
World War II | The deadliest war in history, 1939 to 1945, killed about 70 to 85 million people. | The war that ran from 1939 to 1945 was the deadliest in history, killing about 70 to 85 million people. It began when Germany invaded Poland on September 1, 1939, and eventually involved most of the world's nations. More than half of those who died were civilians, many of them in the Soviet Union and China. It ended with the surrender of Germany in May 1945 and Japan in August.|1939–1945|Standard history
The Holocaust | The Nazi regime murdered about six million Jews, and millions of others. | Between 1941 and 1945 Nazi Germany and its collaborators murdered about six million Jews, along with millions of others, including Roma, disabled people, Poles and Soviet prisoners of war. They used shooting squads, ghettos and extermination camps such as Auschwitz. The Nuremberg trials of 1945 and 1946 held leading Nazis to account, and helped to establish the idea of crimes against humanity.|1941–1945|US Holocaust Memorial Museum
D-Day | On June 6, 1944, the Allies landed on the beaches of Normandy. | On June 6, 1944, Allied forces landed on five beaches in Normandy, France, in the largest seaborne invasion in history. About 156,000 troops came ashore on the first day, supported by thousands of ships and aircraft. It opened the Western Front against Nazi Germany, and the Allies pushed on to liberate Paris in August.|June 6, 1944|National WWII Museum
Hiroshima and Nagasaki | The only use of nuclear weapons in war, in August 1945. | In August 1945 the United States dropped atomic bombs on the Japanese cities of Hiroshima, on August 6, and Nagasaki, on August 9. They remain the only use of nuclear weapons in war. Japan announced its surrender on August 15. Estimates of the number killed in both cities by the end of 1945 range from about 150,000 to 250,000.|1945|Standard history
The United Nations | Founded in October 1945 by 51 countries to prevent another world war. | The United Nations was founded in October 1945, after the Second World War, by 51 countries, to prevent another such war. It now has 193 member states. In 1948 its General Assembly adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, drafted by a committee chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt. It set out, for the first time, rights that all people should enjoy.|1945|UN Charter
Gandhi's Salt March | In 1930 Mohandas Gandhi led a 240-mile walk to the sea to make salt in defiance of British law. | In March 1930 Mohandas Gandhi led a 240-mile march to the sea at Dandi to make salt, in defiance of the British law that gave the government a monopoly on it. The walk lasted 24 days, and thousands joined along the way. The salt tax hurt the poorest most. The march drew world attention to India's independence movement. India became independent in 1947.|1930|Standard history
Brown v. Board of Education | In 1954 the US Supreme Court ruled that segregated public schools were unconstitutional. | In 1954 the US Supreme Court ruled unanimously that racial segregation in public schools was unconstitutional, overturning the doctrine of "separate but equal." The case combined several lawsuits, led by the NAACP lawyer Thurgood Marshall, who later became the first Black Supreme Court justice. Real integration took many years, and in many places it's still incomplete.|1954|Brown v. Board, 347 U.S. 483
Rosa Parks | On December 1, 1955, she refused to give up her bus seat in Montgomery, Alabama. | On December 1, 1955, in Montgomery, Alabama, Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat on a bus to a white passenger, and was arrested. Her act led to a boycott of the city's buses by Black residents that lasted 381 days. It brought a young minister, Martin Luther King Jr., to national leadership, and in 1956 the Supreme Court ruled that bus segregation was unconstitutional.|1955|Standard US history
The March on Washington | About 250,000 people gathered on August 28, 1963, where King gave the "I Have a Dream" speech. | On August 28, 1963, about 250,000 people gathered at the Lincoln Memorial in Washington for the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. Martin Luther King Jr. gave his "I Have a Dream" speech there. The march helped build support for the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which banned discrimination, and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.|1963|National Archives
The Cuban Missile Crisis | In October 1962 the US and Soviet Union came close to nuclear war. | In October 1962 an American spy plane found Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba, 90 miles from Florida. For 13 days, the world held its breath as American ships blockaded the island and leaders threatened war. It ended when the Soviets agreed to remove the missiles in return for an American pledge not to invade Cuba and a secret promise to remove US missiles from Turkey.|1962|Allison & Zelikow, 1999
The Berlin Wall | Built in August 1961, it came down on November 9, 1989. | In August 1961 East Germany began building a wall to stop its citizens from fleeing to the West through Berlin. At least 140 people died trying to cross it. On November 9, 1989, after huge protests, East Germany announced that its borders were open, and crowds began to tear the wall down. Germany was reunified in October 1990.|1961–1989|Standard history
The end of the Soviet Union | The USSR dissolved on December 26, 1991. | On December 25, 1991, Mikhail Gorbachev resigned as president of the Soviet Union, and the next day the Soviet parliament voted the country out of existence. Fifteen new countries emerged, including Russia, Ukraine and Kazakhstan. It ended 69 years of Soviet rule and the Cold War that had dominated world politics since the late 1940s.|1991|Standard history
The first message on the internet | On October 29, 1969, computers at UCLA and Stanford exchanged their first message. | On October 29, 1969, a student programmer at UCLA, Charley Kline, tried to send the word "LOGIN" to a computer at Stanford, over the new ARPANET. The system crashed after the first two letters, so the first message was "LO." An hour later the whole word got through. The network started with four computers, and has grown into billions of devices.|1969|Kleinrock
The personal computer | The Altair 8800 of 1975, then the Apple II in 1977 and the IBM PC in 1981. | In 1975 the Altair 8800 kit let hobbyists build their own computers. In 1977 the Apple II, a ready-made machine with color graphics, became a hit, and in 1981 IBM introduced its PC. Together, they moved computers from air-conditioned rooms to ordinary desks and homes. By the 1990s, the World Wide Web gave those computers a reason to be connected.|1975–1981|Standard history of computing
The iPhone | Steve Jobs introduced it on January 9, 2007, and it went on sale on June 29. | Steve Jobs unveiled the iPhone on January 9, 2007, describing it as three products in one: a phone, a music player and an internet device. It went on sale on June 29. Its multi-touch screen replaced the keypad. The App Store opened in 2008, and changed how software is made and sold. Within a few years, smartphones had become part of daily life for billions of people.|2007|Apple, 2007
Smallpox is gone | Smallpox is the only human disease completely wiped out. | Smallpox is the only human disease that has been completely eradicated. It killed about 300 million people in the 20th century alone. A global vaccination campaign led by the World Health Organization reduced cases until the last natural case was reported in Somalia in 1977. In 1980 the WHO declared the world free of smallpox. It's regarded as one of the greatest achievements in public health.|1980|WHO, 1980
Extreme poverty is falling | The share of people in extreme poverty fell from over a third in 1990 to under a tenth by 2019. | In 1990 about 36 percent of the world's population lived in extreme poverty, according to World Bank estimates. By 2019 that had fallen to about 9 percent. The World Bank defines it as living on less than about $2 a day in today's prices. Progress has been uneven, much of it in China and India, and the COVID-19 pandemic reversed some of the gains.|36% to 9%|World Bank
We live twice as long | Global life expectancy has more than doubled since 1900. | In 1900 the average person in the world could expect to live to around 32. By 2019 it was about 73. Much of the improvement came from fewer children dying young, thanks to clean water, vaccines and antibiotics, along with better nutrition. It's one of the biggest changes in human history, though we tend to forget it because it happened gradually.|32 to 73|Our World in Data; UN
Literacy | The share of adults who can read rose from about 12% in 1820 to about 87% in 2016. | In 1820 only about 12 percent of the world's adults could read and write. By 2016 about 87 percent could. Most of that rise happened in the last century, as schooling spread across the world. Still, around 770 million adults can't read, according to UNESCO, and about two-thirds of them are women.|12% to 87%|Our World in Data; UNESCO
COVID-19 and vaccines | The first mRNA vaccines were authorized in December 2020, less than a year after the virus was identified. | The first vaccines against COVID-19 to use messenger RNA were authorized in December 2020, less than a year after the virus was identified, faster than any vaccine before. That speed was made possible by decades of earlier research, by scientists such as Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman, who found how to make mRNA work without triggering harmful inflammation. They received the 2023 Nobel Prize in Medicine.|2020|Nobel Prize in Medicine, 2023
