# Logical fallacies and the basics of arguing well.

@src Copi & Cohen, Introduction to Logic (14th ed.)
@conf solid

Ad hominem | Attacking the person instead of the argument. | You hear it all the time: "You can't trust his budget plan, he went bankrupt once." Maybe his history is relevant, but whether the plan adds up is a separate question, and you can check the numbers without knowing a thing about the man. The Latin means "to the person." There's one exception worth keeping in mind. If someone has a real conflict of interest, pointing it out is fair, because it gives you a reason to look more closely at what they say.
Straw man | Misrepresenting an argument so it is easier to knock down. | Someone says the schools in town need more funding, and the reply is, "So you want to bankrupt the country?" That's a straw man: a weaker, sillier version of the claim that's easy to knock over, set up in place of what was actually said. It works because the listener sees an argument being won and doesn't notice it was a different argument. If you catch yourself rebutting a position in its worst form, stop and restate theirs in its best.
Steelmanning | Restating the other side's view in its strongest form before you answer it. | If a straw man is an easy version of someone's view, a steelman is the strongest one you can build. The philosopher Daniel Dennett, drawing on the game theorist Anatol Rapoport, suggests rules for criticism. First, restate their position so clearly that they say "I wish I'd put it that way." Second, list what you agree with. Only then say where you disagree. It takes effort, and it tends to make the argument that follows more honest and often shorter.||Dennett, Intuition Pumps (2013)
False dilemma | Treating two options as the only ones when more exist. | "You're either with us or against us" is a famous example of a trap in which a complicated situation is squeezed into two options. In real life there's usually a third: you might agree with some of it, want to wait, or object to the way the choice is framed. When someone offers you an either/or, the useful question is what's missing from the list. Sometimes the best answer is "neither," or "it depends on something you haven't mentioned yet."
Slippery slope | Claiming one step will inevitably lead to a disaster without showing the steps between. | The claim goes: if we allow this small thing, the next step will happen, and then the next, until we reach a disaster. Sometimes that's true, and chains of events do happen. It becomes a fallacy when the links are just asserted rather than shown. A fair slope argument has to explain why each step would really lead to the next one, and why nothing could stop it along the way. Without that, it's a worry dressed up as a proof.
Appeal to authority | Accepting a claim because someone important said it, not because of the evidence. | We can't check everything ourselves, so relying on experts is normal and usually wise. The fallacy lies in leaning on someone's fame instead of their expertise. A celebrated physicist's opinion on nutrition doesn't count for much, and neither does a doctor's on geology. What does carry weight is the agreement of people who study the subject, since several independent experts agreeing is hard to dismiss. Even then, it's strong evidence, not proof, and experts do sometimes revise their views.
Circular reasoning | The conclusion is already hidden in the premise. | Here's a classic: "The book is true because it says so, and what it says is always true." The conclusion is hidden in the premise, so the argument goes in a circle and never gets anywhere new. It can be hard to spot when the circle is large, with a few steps in between. The older name is "begging the question," which in this use means assuming the thing you're supposed to prove. A good test is to ask whether someone who doubted the conclusion would accept your premises.
Post hoc ergo propter hoc | "After this, therefore because of this": assuming what came first caused what followed. | The Latin means "after this, therefore because of this." A rooster crows just before sunrise every morning, but nobody thinks the rooster raises the Sun. Yet we make the same mistake all the time. We start a new vitamin and the cold goes away, or we change a setting and the problem stops, so we credit the change. Something happening first is a clue about cause, but only a clue. It could be coincidence, or something else could be causing both.
No true Scotsman | Redefining a group after a counterexample so the generalization stays safe. | The philosopher Antony Flew gave an example in 1975. A man reads in the paper that a Scotsman did something terrible and says, "No Scotsman would do that." The next day he reads about a Scotsman who did, and answers, "Well, no true Scotsman would do that." The group has been redefined to keep the claim safe. Whenever a generalization faces a counterexample and someone changes the definition instead of the claim, you're probably seeing this move.||Flew, Thinking About Thinking (1975)
Tu quoque | Dismissing a criticism because the critic does the same thing. | The Latin is "you too." A doctor who smokes tells a patient to quit, and the patient says, "You smoke, so why should I listen?" The doctor may be a hypocrite, and that may be worth saying. But smoking's dangers don't depend on who delivers the message. The charge of hypocrisy is about the speaker, not the claim. If you notice you're dismissing advice because the person giving it doesn't follow it, ask whether the advice is still correct.
Red herring | Introducing an irrelevant topic to pull attention away from the point. | You ask a colleague why the report is late, and the answer is a long account of how hard the team has been working this month. All of it may be true, but it doesn't answer the question you asked. A red herring changes the subject to something nearby that feels related and is easier to talk about. You can often spot it by comparing the question with the reply. If the reply wouldn't change when the question did, you're being steered.
Equivocation | Using one word in two different senses within an argument. | A word can have more than one meaning, and an argument can quietly switch between them. "A feather is light. What is light cannot be dark. So a feather cannot be dark." The first "light" means "not heavy" and the second means "not dark." The argument sounds valid until you notice the word has changed under your feet. It's common in debates over words like "theory," "natural" or "freedom." Asking "what do you mean by that word here?" can unravel it.
The burden of proof | The one making the claim must support it; "prove me wrong" shifts the work to the wrong person. | Whoever makes a claim is responsible for supporting it. If a friend says there's a hidden ninth planet in our solar system, it's up to them to show the evidence. It's not up to everyone else to prove there isn't one. "Prove me wrong" quietly flips the burden, and it's a common tactic. It doesn't mean every claim needs a lab study. But the more surprising the claim, the more the person making it has to bring.
Russell's teapot | You cannot disprove a tiny teapot orbiting the Sun, and that is no reason to believe in it. | In 1952 the philosopher Bertrand Russell wrote that if he said a small china teapot orbits the Sun, between Earth and Mars, too small for any telescope to see, nobody could disprove it. But it would be absurd for him to expect people to believe it on that basis. His point was about the burden of proof. You can't disprove a claim that's built to be unfalsifiable, and that fact doesn't make the claim likely.||Russell, "Is There a God?" (written 1952)
The Texas sharpshooter | Firing at a barn wall, then painting the target around the bullet holes. | Imagine a man who fires shots at the side of a barn, then paints a bullseye around the tightest cluster and calls himself a marksman. That's the fallacy: looking at a lot of data, finding a pattern, and then presenting it as if you'd predicted it. With enough numbers, patterns appear by chance. A pattern is much more convincing if it was named before looking, or if it shows up again in fresh data that wasn't used to find it.
Appeal to nature | Assuming that natural means good or safe, and artificial means bad. | The idea that natural things are good for you and artificial things are bad sounds sensible, and it sells a lot of products. But arsenic, hemlock and tobacco are all perfectly natural. Vaccines, seat belts and most modern medicines were made by people. Where something comes from tells you little about whether it's safe. The question to ask is what evidence there is for this particular thing, at this dose, for this purpose.
Appeal to tradition | Assuming something is right because it has always been done. | For roughly two thousand years, doctors treated many illnesses by draining blood from patients, and the practice only faded in the nineteenth century. It lasted because it was traditional and because people sometimes got better anyway. That a practice has been done for a long time shows that it's survived, not that it works. Tradition can carry real wisdom, but it needs testing the same way a new idea does.
Bandwagon appeal | Treating a claim as true because many people believe it. | For most of history, most people believed the Sun circled the Earth. They were wrong, and the number of believers had nothing to do with the truth. Popular opinion can be a useful clue, because sometimes many people really do know something. But that's the case only when they're independently informed, not just copying each other. How many people believe something can tell you about people. It doesn't tell you about the world.
Appeal to emotion | Using feelings in place of reasons. | A charity commercial with a crying child can move you, and there's nothing wrong with that. But the commercial can't tell you whether the charity actually helps children, how much goes to overhead, or whether the money would do more good elsewhere. Feelings are a fine reason to care and to act. The mistake comes when a feeling stands in for evidence on a question that evidence could settle.
The genetic fallacy | Judging an idea by where it came from instead of whether it is true. | This fallacy is about origins. It judges an idea by where it came from instead of by whether it holds up. A sound argument doesn't become unsound because someone disreputable made it, and a poor one isn't rescued by a prestigious source. The history of an idea can be interesting, and sometimes it points to a reason for caution. But when you want to know if it's true, you have to look at the idea itself.
Fallacy of composition | Assuming what is true of the parts is true of the whole. | Just because every part has a property doesn't mean the whole does. A team of the best players in the league may still play badly together, because what matters is how they fit. Or think of a parade: you can see better by standing up, but if everyone stands, nobody sees any better. What's true for one person acting alone can fail when everyone does it. Check the leap from parts to whole.
Hasty generalization | Drawing a broad conclusion from a few cases. | You meet two rude people from a small town on one trip and decide the town is full of rude people. Two is too few to say much about a town of thousands. We all do this, because we have to form impressions from limited experience. The remedy is to hold those impressions loosely and to ask how many cases you've seen, and whether they were typical or just the ones that stood out.
The anecdotal fallacy | Using a personal story to dismiss data. | "My grandfather smoked a pack a day and lived to 95." It's a true story, and it may even be dear to you. But one case can't outweigh studies of millions, because there are always some people who beat the odds. Personal stories are vivid and memorable, and that's exactly why they can feel stronger than they are. Stories are a good way to start asking a question, but a poor way to answer it.
The middle ground fallacy | Assuming the truth must lie halfway between two positions. | If one person says the Earth is round and another says it's flat, the truth isn't that it's slightly curved. The middle ground fallacy assumes that when two sides disagree, the right answer must sit halfway between them. Sometimes a compromise is wise, as with splitting a bill. But on factual questions, one side can simply be right, and splitting the difference then just means being half wrong.
The nirvana fallacy | Rejecting a solution because it is not perfect. | A seat belt doesn't save everyone in a crash. That's true, and it's no reason to skip it. The nirvana fallacy rejects a good-enough solution because it's not perfect, comparing it to an imaginary ideal instead of to the real alternatives. The useful comparison is between the options you actually have. A policy, a diet or a plan doesn't need to solve everything to be worth doing, only to be better than the alternative.
The loaded question | A question with an assumption built into it. | "Have you stopped cheating on your taxes?" Whether you answer yes or no, you've admitted to cheating. The question has an assumption built into it, and it traps the answerer. A courtroom lawyer might use one on purpose, and it can happen in everyday conversation by accident. The best response is to refuse the premise. You might say, "I haven't cheated on my taxes, so the question doesn't apply," before answering anything else.
Special pleading | Applying a rule to everyone except yourself without a good reason. | Someone says that rules about being on time are important, except in their own case, where they have good reasons. Maybe they do. But special pleading skips the step of showing why this case is different. It's easy to spot in others and hard to spot in ourselves, because our own circumstances always feel unusual to us. A fair test is to ask whether you'd accept the same excuse from someone else.
Moving the goalposts | Changing the standard of proof after it has been met. | Someone says, "Show me one study that proves it." You find one. "Well, I meant a large study." You find a large one. "I meant a randomized trial." Each time the evidence is delivered, the standard shifts. If nothing could satisfy the person, then the question was never really about evidence. A good-faith discussion starts by agreeing on what would change each person's mind, before looking for it.
Poisoning the well | Discrediting a source in advance so nothing it says will be heard. | Before you've heard a word from a speaker, someone says, "Don't listen to him, he's a liar." That can make everything the person says afterwards sound suspect. Even if the charge were true, though, what he says next still has to be judged on its own. The harm is done in advance, which is why it works. When someone tries to discredit a source before you've heard it, ask for the specific points to check.
Cherry-picking | Showing only the evidence that supports your side. | Say there are thirty studies on a question and three found an effect. A person who wants you to believe in it cites just those three. Each citation is accurate, but the picture is misleading. Cherry-picking can hide inside honest facts, so it's hard to catch by checking individual claims. The better question is "What does all the evidence say?" That's the reason reviews that pool many studies are worth more than any single one.
The ecological fallacy | Using group-level data to draw conclusions about individuals. | Looking at countries, you might find that places with higher average income also have higher rates of some disease. It's tempting to conclude that richer people get the disease more. But within each country the wealthy could actually be healthier. A pattern across groups doesn't have to hold for the individuals in them. Statistics about averages describe populations, and they can mislead when we apply them to a single person.
The prosecutor's fallacy | Confusing "how likely is this evidence if innocent" with "how likely is innocence given this evidence". | In 1999 Sally Clark, a British solicitor, was convicted of murdering her two baby sons, who had died suddenly. A pediatrician told the court that the chance of two such deaths in one family was 1 in 73 million. The number was wrongly calculated, and it also confused two different questions: how rare the evidence is if she were innocent, and how likely she is to be innocent given the evidence. Her conviction was overturned in 2003.|1 in 73 million|Royal Statistical Society statement, 2001; Court of Appeal, 2003
Argument from ignorance | Claiming something is true because it has not been proven false, or the reverse. | "Nobody has shown that ghosts don't exist, so they must." The lack of proof against something isn't proof for it. You can reverse it as well: "No one has proven it's real, so it isn't." There's a subtle exception. If you've searched properly in the places where evidence would show up, and found none, that absence does count for something. A thorough search that finds nothing is informative. An absence of looking isn't.
Argument from incredulity | "I can't imagine how this could be true, so it isn't." | "I just can't imagine how that could be true, so it can't be." The limit of one person's imagination isn't a law of nature. Quantum mechanics is hard to picture, and so is the size of the universe, or how a single cell becomes a human. They are all true anyway. Finding something hard to believe is a signal to learn more, and to check, but it isn't a reason to conclude that the claim is wrong.
Whataboutism | Answering a criticism with a different one. | Someone criticizes one action, and the reply is "But what about when your side did it?" It shifts the discussion from the topic to the other person's record. Both things might be wrong, or one might be worse, or they might be quite different. Pointing at someone else's fault doesn't make the first complaint go away. If you hear it, you can say, "Maybe so, and we can talk about that too. Right now, we're discussing this."
False equivalence | Treating two things as equal when they differ greatly in size or kind. | Two things are described as the same because they share one feature, though they differ enormously in scale or kind. Treating a small slip and a deliberate fraud as equivalent because both are "mistakes" is an example. The comparison hides the difference that matters most. A good check is to ask how they compare in size, intent and consequences, not just whether the same word applies.
The fallacy fallacy | Deciding a claim is false because someone argued for it badly. | Imagine someone argues for a true conclusion with a bad argument. You point out the flaw. Did you just prove that the conclusion is false? No. A bad argument for something doesn't make it untrue. It only means that this particular reasoning doesn't establish it. Someone can be right for the wrong reasons. The fair response is to say the argument fails, then look for better evidence on both sides.
Appeal to novelty | Assuming something is better because it is new. | A new treatment, gadget or idea is sometimes sold on being new alone, as if newer meant better. Newness tells you nothing about quality. Many new things are improvements and many aren't. The old way might be old because it works. The right test is the same one used for anything else: what's the evidence that it works, and compared with what?
Appeal to consequences | Deciding what is true by what you wish were true. | "There has to be an afterlife, or life would be pointless." That moves from how we'd like things to be to how they are. Wanting an answer to be true, or fearing what follows if it's false, tells us nothing about whether it's correct. The world doesn't owe us comfortable facts. It can be a tough habit to notice, since the wish feels like a reason, and it's worth asking what the evidence says if you set the wish aside.
Affirming the consequent | "If P then Q. Q. Therefore P." | The pattern goes: if it rained, the street is wet. The street is wet. Therefore it rained. It sounds right, but a sprinkler, a street-cleaning truck or a burst pipe would also leave a wet street. The "if" gave one route to a result, and the result can have many causes. Doctors, detectives and scientists all have to watch for this one. Seeing the effect doesn't tell you which cause produced it.
Denying the antecedent | "If P then Q. Not P. Therefore not Q." | "If you study, you'll pass. You didn't study, so you won't pass." That doesn't follow, because you might pass anyway: the test could be easy, or you could already know the material. The "if" described one way to reach the outcome, not the only way. It's the mirror image of affirming the consequent, and it's just as easy to slip into when you're thinking fast.
Modus ponens | If P then Q, and P, so Q: the basic valid pattern. | This is the basic pattern of valid reasoning. "If it's raining, the ground is wet. It's raining. Therefore the ground is wet." If both of the first two statements are true, the conclusion has to be true. Logic calls an argument valid when its conclusion follows from its premises, whether or not the premises are actually true. The Latin means "the way that affirms." Nearly all everyday deduction runs on it.
Modus tollens | If P then Q, and not Q, so not P: the logic of disproof. | The pattern here runs backward: "If the theory is right, the planet will appear at this spot. It didn't. So the theory is wrong." It's the logic of disproof, and Karl Popper built his picture of science on it. A theory can never be completely proven by confirming cases, but one clear failure can sink it. It explains why scientists look hard for results that would contradict their ideas.||Popper, The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934)
Valid versus sound | Valid: the conclusion follows from the premises. Sound: it is valid and the premises are actually true. | "All birds can fly. Penguins are birds. Therefore penguins can fly." The reasoning is valid, meaning the conclusion follows if the premises are true. But the first premise is false, so the argument isn't sound. A sound argument is valid and also has true premises. When you check an argument, you have to ask two questions: does the conclusion follow, and are the starting facts correct?
Deduction and induction | Deduction guarantees its conclusion; induction only makes it likely. | Deduction guarantees its conclusion if the premises are true: all men are mortal, Socrates is a man, so Socrates is mortal. Induction only makes a conclusion likely: the Sun has risen every day so far, so it will probably rise tomorrow. Most of science and daily life runs on induction, because we can rarely be certain. It's the reason that we talk about evidence building up, not proof. And one surprising case can overturn an inductive rule.
Falsifiability | A claim is scientific only if some possible observation could show it to be wrong. | In 1934 the philosopher Karl Popper suggested that what makes a claim scientific is that it could, in principle, be shown wrong by some observation. A theory that fits whatever happens, like a horoscope, doesn't say much. Philosophers still argue about exactly where to draw the line, but the habit it teaches is useful. Ask yourself what result would change your mind, and if the answer is nothing, then notice that.||Popper, The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934)
Hitchens's razor | What can be asserted without evidence can be dismissed without evidence. | Christopher Hitchens, the writer, put it this way in 2007: what can be asserted without evidence can be dismissed without evidence. It places the burden on the person making a claim, and it gives you permission not to take a bare assertion seriously. It's a rule of thumb, though. Dismissing something doesn't prove it false. It just means you've no reason yet to believe it, which is a different thing.||Hitchens, God Is Not Great (2007)
The Sagan standard | Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. | Carl Sagan popularized the idea that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. The more a claim conflicts with what we already know, the stronger the evidence needs to be. If your neighbor says he has a dog, you'd accept that easily. If he says he has a dragon in the garage, you'd want to see it. It isn't a bar set to be impossible. It's a way of matching your trust to how surprising the claim is.||Sagan, Cosmos (1980)
Appeal to ridicule | Making a position look silly instead of answering it. | Someone describes an opposing idea in a cartoonish way, and the audience laughs. A laugh isn't a rebuttal, though, and a funny version of an idea isn't the idea. Many true ideas were laughed at at first, including continental drift, which was mocked for decades before the evidence won. Be cautious when mockery has replaced an argument, especially when you find yourself enjoying it.
The association fallacy | Rejecting an idea because a disliked person or group also holds it. | "Hitler liked dogs, so liking dogs is bad." It sounds absurd, but the same move is made in subtler ways all the time: rejecting an idea because a disliked person or group believes it. A claim stands or falls on its own evidence, no matter who else agrees with it. A stopped clock is right twice a day, and even a villain can be right about the weather.
Bulverism | Assuming someone is wrong and then explaining their mistake by their motives. | C. S. Lewis named this one, after an invented person called Ezekiel Bulver. Bulver, at the age of five, hears his mother say to his father, "Oh, you say that because you are a man." Bulver realizes that you can dismiss anything by explaining why the person says it. Lewis's point was that you must first show a claim is wrong. Only then does it become interesting to ask why someone believed it.||C. S. Lewis, God in the Dock (1970)
Weasel words | Vague phrases that sound like evidence but commit to nothing. | "Studies show," "many experts agree," "some people say." These phrases sound like evidence while committing to nothing. Which studies? How many experts, out of how many? Who are the "some people"? If the writer can't name them, then nothing has really been claimed that you can check. They're common in advertising and in news headlines, and a good habit is to ask who exactly is being referred to.
The motte-and-bailey | Defending a modest claim when challenged, then acting as if a bolder one was proven. | In medieval castles, the motte was a fortified hill you could retreat to, and the bailey was the more comfortable but harder-to-defend courtyard. In arguments, the bailey is the bold claim someone really wants to make, and the motte is a modest, easy-to-defend claim they fall back on when challenged. Once the challenge passes, they go back to the bold one. If someone keeps switching, ask which claim they're actually defending.||Shackel, 2005
The Gish gallop | Overwhelming an opponent with so many weak points that none can be answered in time. | It's named for the debater Duane Gish, who would fire off a rapid string of weak arguments, so many that his opponent couldn't answer them all in the time available. It works because it takes much longer to refute a claim than to make one. In a long list, unanswered points can look like points conceded. A sensible response is to pick the two or three strongest and handle those properly, or to say the list can't be fairly answered at that speed.|
The ludic fallacy | Treating real life as a casino where the odds are known. | Nassim Taleb coined this term, from the Latin for "game," in his 2007 book The Black Swan. It's the mistake of treating real life as if it were a casino, where the odds are known and the rules are fixed. Neat probability models are built on assumptions, and the real world can break them. The risks that hurt most are often the ones nobody put in the model. A model is a good tool for thinking, and a poor guarantee.||Taleb, The Black Swan (2007)
The narrative fallacy | We turn random events into a neat story with causes. | After a company succeeds, we tell a story about the founder's grit and vision, and it all seems to fit together. The story is tidy, and it feels like an explanation. But luck may have done much of the work, and the same story could be told about the founders who failed. Taleb warned us to be careful of stories that explain too neatly. A good one makes you feel you understand, and that feeling isn't evidence.||Taleb, The Black Swan (2007)|mixed
Spurious correlation | Two numbers can move together by pure coincidence. | The author Tyler Vigen collects charts of numbers that move together by pure chance. One of them shows that per-person cheese consumption in America tracked, over ten years, the number of people who died tangled in their bedsheets. The match is striking and it means nothing. If you compare enough data sets, some will line up by accident. A correlation needs a plausible reason, and ideally a repeat on new data, before it means anything.||Vigen, Spurious Correlations (2015)
