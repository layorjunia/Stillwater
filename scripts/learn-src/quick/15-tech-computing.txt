The bit | The smallest unit of information: a 0 or a 1. | A bit is the smallest piece of information there is: a single choice between two possibilities, a zero or a one. In 1948 the engineer Claude Shannon published a paper that used the bit as the basic unit for measuring information, and his colleague John Tukey is credited with coining the word, from "binary digit." Eight bits make a byte, which can hold 256 different values, enough for one letter of text.|1948|Shannon, 1948
Unicode | A standard that gives every character in every language a number. | Computers store text as numbers, and for years, different systems used different codes, which made text turn to garbage when moved between them. ASCII, from 1963, had only 128 characters, enough for plain English. Unicode, begun in 1991, set out to give every character in every writing system its own number. It now covers more than 150,000 characters, from Chinese and Arabic to ancient scripts and emoji.|150,000+|Unicode Consortium
Emoji | The first set of 176 emoji was drawn in Japan in 1999. | The first set of emoji, 176 of them, was created in 1999 by Shigetaka Kurita, a designer working on a mobile internet service in Japan. Each was drawn on a tiny grid of 12 by 12 pixels. They were meant to convey things like weather and traffic quickly on small screens. The original set is now in the permanent collection of the Museum of Modern Art in New York.|1999|Kurita, 1999
Algorithm | A precise, step-by-step recipe for solving a problem. | An algorithm is a precise, step-by-step method for solving a problem, one that's clear enough that it can be followed without any guesswork. The word comes from the name of the Persian mathematician al-Khwarizmi. A cooking recipe, a method of sorting names alphabetically and the way your phone finds a route all count. What matters is that each step is unambiguous and that the procedure ends.||Knuth, The Art of Computer Programming
Big O | A way to describe how a program's running time grows as the input grows. | Programmers use "Big O" notation to describe how a program's running time grows as the amount of data grows. Imagine sorting a million items. A simple method that compares every item with every other takes time proportional to n squared, about a trillion steps. A cleverer method, taking time proportional to n times the logarithm of n, needs only about 20 million. At scale, the choice of method matters far more than the speed of the computer.||Standard computer science text
Compilers and interpreters | A compiler translates a whole program into machine code in advance; an interpreter runs it line by line. | Computers can only run machine code, which is long strings of numbers, so a human-friendly program has to be translated. A compiler translates the whole program in advance and produces a file that the computer can run directly. An interpreter reads and carries out the program line by line as it goes. Grace Hopper wrote an early compiler in 1952. C is typically compiled, Python is usually interpreted, and many systems use a mixture.||Hopper, 1952
The operating system | The program that manages a computer's hardware and runs the other programs. | The operating system is the program that sits between your applications and the hardware. It decides which program gets the processor, manages memory, files and devices, and gives each application a safe place to run. Unix appeared at Bell Labs in 1969. In 1991 a Finnish student, Linus Torvalds, began writing Linux, whose kernel now runs most of the world's servers and Android phones.|1991|Ritchie & Thompson, 1974
The CPU and clock speed | A processor carries out instructions, billions of times a second. | The central processor is the part of a computer that carries out instructions, and its speed is measured in how many cycles it completes each second. A 3 gigahertz chip ticks three billion times a second. In one tick, light travels only about 10 centimeters. That's one reason that processors have to be tiny: a signal can't cross a large machine in less time than that.|3 billion/sec|Standard architecture text
GPUs | Graphics chips do many simple calculations at once, which is why they power AI. | A graphics processing unit, or GPU, was designed to draw video game images, which involves doing the same simple calculation for millions of pixels at once. A CPU has a few powerful cores that do one thing after another. A GPU has thousands of small cores that work at the same time. Training an artificial neural network involves huge numbers of identical multiplications, and it's an ideal job for a GPU.||Standard computer architecture text
Memory and storage | RAM is fast and forgets when the power is off; storage is slower and keeps its data. | Computers have several layers of memory. The fastest is the processor's own cache, which takes about a nanosecond to read. Then there's RAM, about a hundred times slower, which loses its contents when the power goes off. Then storage, such as a solid-state drive, which keeps data permanently and is about a thousand times slower again. It's a trade-off between speed, size and cost.||Standard architecture text
The transistor | A tiny switch with no moving parts, the building block of every chip. | A transistor is a tiny electronic switch with no moving parts. John Bardeen and Walter Brattain built the first one at Bell Labs in December 1947, and William Shockley improved the design, and the three shared a Nobel Prize in 1956. It replaced the bulky, hot and unreliable vacuum tube. A modern chip contains tens of billions of them, and the largest hold over 100 billion.|1947|Bell Labs, 1947
Moore's law | The number of transistors on a chip doubled about every two years. | In 1965 Gordon Moore, who later cofounded Intel, noticed that the number of transistors that could be put on a chip was doubling regularly, and predicted that it would continue. In 1975 he revised the pace to about every two years. It held for decades, and made computers ever faster and cheaper. It has been slowing in recent years, as the parts get close to the size of only a few atoms.||Moore, 1965|mixed
The internet and the web | They are not the same thing. | People often use the two words as if they meant the same, but they don't. The internet is the global network of connected computers, which began as a standard way for networks to talk when the TCP/IP protocol was adopted on January 1, 1983. The World Wide Web is a system of linked pages and sites that runs on top of the internet. Email and online games use the internet but not the web.|1983|Cerf & Kahn, 1974
Packet switching | Data is cut into small packets that travel separately and are reassembled. | The internet doesn't send a message as one continuous stream. It breaks it into small packets, each of which finds its own way across the network and is reassembled at the other end. The idea was developed in the 1960s by Paul Baran in the US and Donald Davies in Britain. It makes the network robust, because if one route fails, the packets just take another.||Baran, 1964; Davies, 1966
IP addresses and DNS | Every device has a number, and DNS turns names into numbers. | Every device on the internet has a numerical address, like 142.250.80.46, but nobody wants to remember those. The Domain Name System, or DNS, works like a phone book: when you type a website's name, a DNS server looks up the matching number and your computer connects to it. Paul Mockapetris invented it in 1983. If DNS went down, you'd be able to reach websites only by typing their numbers.||Mockapetris, 1983
The padlock in your browser | It means your connection is private, not that the site is honest. | The padlock in the address bar means that the connection between your browser and the website is encrypted, so that nobody in between can read or alter what you send. It doesn't mean the site is trustworthy. Scammers can get the padlock for fake sites, too. It's wise to check the address itself, and to be careful about links in messages, whether or not you see a padlock.||Standard web security guidance
Undersea cables | About 99% of international internet traffic travels along cables on the ocean floor. | It feels like the internet is in the air, but about 99 percent of international data travels through cables lying on the ocean floor. There are hundreds of them, adding up to well over a million kilometers, and they link all the continents except Antarctica. Each carries many fibers and is only about as thick as a garden hose. Ships' anchors and fishing nets are the main hazards.|99%|ITU; TeleGeography
Public-key encryption | A way to send secrets to someone you have never met. | How can you send a secret to someone you've never met, without first sharing a secret key? Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published an answer in 1976, and in 1977 Rivest, Shamir and Adleman created the RSA method. Each person has two keys, a public one to lock messages and a private one to unlock them. British codebreakers at GCHQ had found the idea earlier, but it was kept secret until 1997.|1976|Diffie & Hellman, 1976
Hashing | A one-way fingerprint of data: easy to compute, practically impossible to reverse. | A hash function turns any piece of data into a short, fixed-length fingerprint. It's easy to compute, and practically impossible to reverse. Change even one letter of the input, and the hash changes completely. Websites should store hashes of your password, not the password itself, so that even if the database is stolen, the passwords aren't directly revealed. Hashes are also used to check that a file hasn't been altered.||Standard cryptography text
Two-factor authentication | A second proof of identity, besides your password. | A password is something you know. A second factor is something you have, like your phone, or something you are, like a fingerprint. A 2019 study by Google found that even a simple text-message code blocked 100 percent of automated bot attacks, 96 percent of bulk phishing attacks and 76 percent of targeted attacks. App prompts and security keys did even better. It's one of the best things you can do to protect an account.|76-100%|Thomas et al., Google, 2019
Passwords: length beats complexity | A long passphrase is stronger than a short string full of symbols. | For years we were told to use short passwords with symbols and numbers, and to change them often. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology revised its advice in 2017. It recommended against forced regular changes and strange character requirements, since they push people into predictable patterns. Instead, long passphrases are better, and passwords should be checked against lists of known leaked ones.|2017|NIST SP 800-63B
Phishing | Fake messages that trick people into giving up logins or money. | Phishing is when an attacker sends a message that looks like it's from a bank, a delivery company or a coworker, to trick you into giving up a password or sending money. In Verizon's 2023 report on data breaches, about 74 percent involved a human element, such as a mistake, stolen credentials or social engineering. The best defense is to slow down, and to go to the real website yourself, instead of clicking the link.|74%|Verizon DBIR, 2023
The first spam | The first mass unsolicited email went out in 1978. | On May 3, 1978, Gary Thuerk, a marketer at Digital Equipment Corporation, sent an advertisement for a new computer to about 400 people on ARPANET, the forerunner of the internet. Many recipients were annoyed. The word "spam" for unwanted messages came later, from a Monty Python sketch in which the word is repeated over and over until it drowns out everything else.|1978|Brunton, Spam (2013)
The cloud | Someone else's computers, rented by the hour. | "The cloud" is a friendly name for other people's computers. Instead of buying and running their own servers, companies and individuals rent computing power and storage over the internet from big data centers, paying for what they use. Amazon launched its cloud services in 2006. When you store photos online or stream a movie, they're sitting in a data center somewhere, probably far from you.|2006|Standard computing text
The API | A set of rules that lets one program ask another for something. | An API, or application programming interface, is a set of rules that allows one program to ask another for something. It's like a waiter in a restaurant: you order from a menu, the waiter takes your request to the kitchen, and brings back the food. You never have to go into the kitchen yourself. When a weather app shows a forecast, it probably asks a weather service's API for the data.||Fielding, 2000
Relational databases | Data is stored in tables that can be linked. | In 1970 Edgar Codd, a researcher at IBM, proposed storing data in simple tables of rows and columns that can be linked to each other by shared values. It was a big change from earlier, more tangled systems. A query language called SQL grew from his idea, and it still underlies most business software today, from bank records to online shopping.|1970|Codd, 1970
Version control | Software that records every change and who made it. | Version control software keeps a record of every change made to a set of files, who made it, and when, so that you can go back to any earlier version. Linus Torvalds created Git in 2005, in about two weeks, after a dispute over the tool that the Linux project had been using. Git lets thousands of people work on the same project at once, and merge their changes.|2005|Torvalds, 2005
Open source | Software whose code anyone can read, use and improve. | Open-source software is software whose code is publicly available for anyone to read, use, modify and share. The term was chosen in 1998. Much of the internet runs on it: Linux powers most web servers, and the Android phone system is built on it. Wikipedia, begun in 2001, applied a similar spirit of open, collaborative work to encyclopedias.|1998|Open Source Initiative
Ariane 5 | A rocket blew up 37 seconds after launch in 1996, from a number that did not fit. | On June 4, 1996, the first Ariane 5 rocket, built by the European Space Agency, veered off course and blew up 37 seconds after launch. The cause was a software bug. A program reused from the older Ariane 4 tried to squeeze a large number into a space that was too small for it. That caused a shutdown of the guidance system. The rocket and its cargo were worth about $370 million.|$370 million|Lions, ESA inquiry, 1996
Y2K and 2038 | Computers stored years as two digits, and later, seconds in a 32-bit number. | Many old computer programs stored years as two digits, so that 2000 would look like 1900. It set off a worldwide effort to repair them, which cost hundreds of billions of dollars, and when the new year arrived in 2000, most systems worked. A similar problem looms for systems that count seconds from 1970 in a 32-bit number. It will run out on January 19, 2038, and older systems will need to be fixed.|2038|Standard computing history
Therac-25 | A radiation machine overdosed patients in the 1980s because of a software flaw. | The Therac-25 was a radiation therapy machine used in hospitals in the 1980s. Between 1985 and 1987 it delivered massive overdoses to at least six patients, and at least three of them died. The software had a flaw called a race condition, which occurred when an operator typed quickly, and the older hardware safety locks, which would have stopped it, had been removed. It's a classic case study in software safety.|1985-87|Leveson & Turner, 1993
Knight Capital | A firm lost $440 million in 45 minutes in 2012 because of a software mistake. | On August 1, 2012, the trading firm Knight Capital installed new software on its servers but, by mistake, put it on only seven of its eight machines. The eighth still had old code, which started buying and selling stocks wildly. In 45 minutes the firm lost about $440 million, nearly all of its capital, and it nearly went out of business. The SEC later fined it.|$440 million|SEC, 2013
Machine learning | Programs that learn patterns from examples instead of following written rules. | Traditionally, programmers write rules telling a computer what to do. In machine learning, you show the program many examples and let it find the patterns itself. To make a spam filter, for instance, you show it thousands of spam and non-spam emails. Arthur Samuel used the term in 1959 for a checkers program that got better by playing against itself.|1959|Samuel, 1959
Neural networks | Layers of simple units whose connections are adjusted by training. | An artificial neural network is made up of layers of simple units, loosely inspired by brain cells, whose connections are adjusted during training. Frank Rosenblatt built an early one, the perceptron, in 1958. A 1986 paper by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams showed how to train deeper networks. In 2012 a network called AlexNet cut the error rate in an image-recognition contest from 26 percent to 15 percent, and launched the modern deep-learning boom.|26% to 15%|Krizhevsky et al., 2012
Overfitting | A model that memorizes its training examples fails on new ones. | An overfitted model has memorized its training examples, including their quirks and errors, and so it does badly on new data. It's like a student who memorizes the answers to past exams without understanding the subject, and then fails when the questions change. The remedy is to test the model on data it has never seen, and to keep the model from being more complicated than it needs to be.||Standard machine learning text
Training and inference | Training is the long process of learning; inference is using what was learned. | There are two phases in an AI model's life. Training is the long, expensive process in which it learns from data, which for a large model can take weeks on thousands of specialized chips. Inference is using the finished model, such as when you ask it a question. That takes a fraction of a second. Most of the cost of running a popular system comes from many people using it, rather than from the one-time training.||Standard machine learning text
Bias in AI | A system trained on unrepresentative data can work much worse for some groups. | An AI system learns from its training data, and if that data doesn't represent everyone, it can work much worse for some groups. In 2018 the researchers Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru tested commercial face-analysis programs in the Gender Shades study. The error rate was as high as 34.7 percent for darker-skinned women, compared with 0.8 percent for lighter-skinned men. The study led several companies to improve their systems.|34.7% vs 0.8%|Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018
Large language models | Programs that predict the next word, trained on huge amounts of text. | A large language model is a program trained on enormous amounts of text to predict the next word, again and again, which lets it produce fluent writing, answer questions and carry on a conversation. Most are built on the transformer design introduced in the 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need." They can be astonishingly capable, but fluent writing isn't the same as accurate writing.|2017|Vaswani et al., 2017
Hallucination | When an AI states something false with total confidence. | When an AI language model states something false with complete confidence, such as a made-up quotation or a nonexistent book, it's called a hallucination. The reason is that these models produce text that's likely, given their training, rather than text that's been checked against facts. It's wise to verify any names, numbers, quotes and citations that an AI gives you, particularly when it matters.||Standard AI reference
Tokens and the context window | A language model reads chunks of words, and can only consider so many at a time. | A language model doesn't read letters or whole words. It reads chunks called tokens, and a token is roughly three-quarters of an English word. The model can only consider a certain amount of text at once, known as its context window. It's like the model's working memory: if something falls outside the window, the model can't see it, however important it was earlier in the conversation.||Standard AI reference
The Turing test | Can a machine's conversation be told apart from a human's? | In 1950 Alan Turing proposed an "imitation game": if a person chatting by text can't tell whether they're talking to a machine or a human, the machine could be said to think. In 1966 Joseph Weizenbaum wrote ELIZA, a very simple program that rephrased what people typed, like a therapist. Some users became convinced that it understood them, an effect now known as the ELIZA effect.|1950|Turing, 1950; Weizenbaum, 1966
Deep Blue and AlphaGo | Computers beat the best human chess player in 1997 and the best Go player in 2016. | In 1997 IBM's Deep Blue beat the world chess champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match, the first time a computer beat a reigning champion under tournament conditions. The ancient game of Go was thought to be much harder for computers, because there are so many possible moves. But in 2016 Google DeepMind's AlphaGo beat the top player Lee Sedol four games to one.|1997, 2016|Campbell et al., 2002; Silver et al., 2016
AlphaFold | An AI that predicts the shape of proteins from their chemical sequence. | A protein's function depends on its three-dimensional shape, which comes from how a chain of amino acids folds, and working it out by experiment can take months or years. Biologists had tried to predict it from the sequence for 50 years. In 2020 DeepMind's AlphaFold matched experimental accuracy for many proteins. Demis Hassabis and John Jumper shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, with David Baker, for this work.|2020|Jumper et al., 2021
Blockchain | A shared record that many computers keep and no one can quietly edit. | A blockchain is a shared record, kept by many computers, in which new entries are linked to the earlier ones so that no one can quietly change history. It was invented for Bitcoin, which was described in 2008 by a person or group using the name Satoshi Nakamoto. Beyond digital currencies, few uses have proved better than an ordinary database, so its broader value is still debated.|2008|Nakamoto, 2008|mixed
Quantum computing | Computers that use quantum effects to solve certain problems much faster. | A quantum computer uses the strange effects of quantum physics, such as superposition, to tackle certain problems far faster than ordinary computers. In 1994 Peter Shor showed that a large enough quantum computer could break the public-key encryption that protects much of the internet. Today's machines are small and error-prone, and such a computer doesn't exist yet. Researchers are already developing encryption that would resist it.|1994|Shor, 1994|mixed
GPS | Satellites broadcast the time, and your phone works out where you are. | The Global Positioning System works by satellites that broadcast signals with the exact time. Your phone listens to several of them and, from the tiny differences in arrival times, works out how far it is from each. To fix its position in three dimensions and to correct its own clock error, it needs signals from at least four satellites. The system is run by the US Space Force and is free for anyone to use.|4 satellites|US Space Force
QR codes | A square barcode invented for tracking car parts. | The QR code was invented in 1994 by Masahiro Hara at the Japanese company Denso Wave, to track car parts in factories. It can hold much more than a traditional barcode, and it can be read from any angle. Built-in error correction means that a code can still be read even if up to about 30 percent of it is damaged or covered, which is why a code with a logo in the middle still works.|1994|Denso Wave
Bluetooth | The wireless standard is named for a Danish king. | The wireless technology is named for Harald Bluetooth Gormsson, a Danish king who ruled around 960 and is said to have united Denmark and Norway. Engineers who were working on a standard to connect different devices chose the name, first as a code name, because the technology was meant to unite devices. The Bluetooth logo is a combination of the runic letters for H and B, his initials.|c. 960|Kardach, 2001
Wi-Fi | The name is not an abbreviation. | You might assume that Wi-Fi stands for "wireless fidelity," but it doesn't. In 1999 the industry group that certifies the technology hired a branding agency, Interbrand, to come up with a catchy name for what was technically called IEEE 802.11. Wi-Fi was chosen because it sounded like "hi-fi." The invented tagline "wireless fidelity" was used for a while, but it was never what the name meant.|1999|Wi-Fi Alliance
The first barcode scan | A pack of chewing gum was the first item scanned, on June 26, 1974. | At 8:01 a.m. on June 26, 1974, at a Marsh supermarket in Troy, Ohio, a cashier scanned the first product with a barcode: a ten-pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit chewing gum. The pack is now in the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History. Barcodes were slow to catch on, but they went on to change how stores and warehouses track goods.|1974|Smithsonian
Email and the @ sign | Ray Tomlinson sent the first network email in 1971 and chose "@" to separate the user from the computer. | In 1971 Ray Tomlinson, an engineer working on ARPANET, sent the first email between two computers, and chose the @ sign to separate the user's name from the computer's name. He picked it because it was on the keyboard and wasn't used in anyone's name. He later said the test message was probably something like "QWERTYUIOP," and that he couldn't remember exactly.|1971|Tomlinson
Hick's law | The more choices you give, the longer it takes to decide. | The more options you offer, the longer it takes a person to decide. In 1952 William Hick showed that decision time rises with the logarithm of the number of choices. It's a reason to keep menus short, and to group options into categories. A screen with twenty buttons will slow down most users compared with one that shows a few and tucks the rest behind a clear label.|1952|Hick, 1952
Fitts's law | The time to reach a target depends on how far it is and how big it is. | The time it takes to reach a target depends on how far away it is and how big it is. Paul Fitts described the rule in 1954, while studying pilots' movements. It explains why buttons should be large, and why items at the edge or corner of a screen are easy to hit: the cursor can't go past the edge, so you can just fling it there.|1954|Fitts, 1954
Dark patterns | Interface tricks that push you into things you didn't mean to do. | A dark pattern is a design trick that pushes people into doing something they didn't intend. The designer Harry Brignull coined the term in 2010. Examples include an unsubscribe link hidden in tiny gray type, boxes that are already ticked to add extras to your order, and countdown timers that reset when they reach zero. Some regulators have begun to treat them as illegal.|2010|Brignull, 2010
Conway's law | Systems end up mirroring the organization that built them. | In 1968 the computer scientist Melvin Conway observed that organizations tend to produce systems that mirror their own communication structure. His example: if four groups work on a compiler, you'll get a compiler with four passes. If departments in a company don't talk to each other, the software they build won't work smoothly together either. To fix the software, you may have to fix the organization.|1968|Conway, 1968
Technical debt | Quick fixes now that cost extra effort later. | In 1992 the programmer Ward Cunningham used a financial metaphor to describe the cost of shortcuts in software. Writing a quick fix now is like taking out a loan: you get speed today, but you pay interest later, in the form of code that's harder to change. That can be a smart trade-off if you pay it back soon. If you never do, the interest builds up until it's hard to make any progress.|1992|Cunningham, 1992
Premature optimization | Making code fast before knowing it matters wastes time. | Donald Knuth wrote in 1974 that "premature optimization is the root of all evil," and it's become a famous warning to programmers. Making code fast before you know where the bottleneck is wastes time and often makes the code harder to read. But the full quote is more balanced: he said that we should forget about small efficiencies 97 percent of the time, yet not pass up our opportunities in the critical 3 percent.||Knuth, 1974
Rubber duck debugging | Explaining your code line by line to a rubber duck helps you find the bug. | Programmers have a trick for finding bugs: explain your code, line by line, to a rubber duck on your desk. Saying the problem out loud forces you to state your assumptions, and often you notice the mistake in the middle of the explanation. The practice was described in the 1999 book The Pragmatic Programmer, and a duck isn't required. A patient colleague or a pet also works.|1999|Hunt & Thomas, 1999
The Swiss cheese model | Accidents happen when holes in several layers of defense line up. | In 1990 the psychologist James Reason proposed picturing the defenses against accidents as slices of Swiss cheese, each with holes. A single hole doesn't lead to disaster, because the next slice blocks it. But sometimes the holes in several layers line up, and a hazard passes straight through. The lesson is to add more layers of defense, and to keep fixing the holes. It's used in aviation, medicine and many other fields.|1990|Reason, 1990
Normal accidents | In very complex, tightly linked systems, some accidents are inevitable. | After the Three Mile Island nuclear accident in 1979, the sociologist Charles Perrow argued in his 1984 book that in systems that are both very complex and tightly coupled, with no slack, some accidents are inevitable. Parts interact in ways that no one expected, and failures spread faster than anyone can respond. Not everyone agrees that this means accidents can't be reduced, but the idea has been very influential.|1984|Perrow, 1984|mixed
Blameless postmortems | After a failure, ask what allowed it, not who to blame. | When something goes wrong, the natural reaction is to ask who was at fault. Engineering teams at companies such as Google and Etsy found that punishing people for mistakes made them hide problems, which made failures worse. Many now hold blameless postmortems, asking "how did our system allow this to happen?" instead of "whose fault was it?" People report problems earlier, and the system gets safer.||Beyer et al., Site Reliability Engineering (2016)
The Hyatt Regency walkway | In 1981 two hanging walkways collapsed in Kansas City, killing 114 people. | On July 17, 1981, two suspended walkways in the lobby of the Hyatt Regency hotel in Kansas City collapsed during a dance, killing 114 people. The cause was a change to the design that was made during construction, in how the walkway rods were attached, which doubled the load on a critical connection. No one rechecked the calculations. It's a standard example in engineering ethics classes.|114 deaths|Moncarz & Taylor, 2000
Lithium-ion batteries | Rechargeable batteries that power phones, laptops and electric cars. | The rechargeable batteries in phones, laptops and electric cars were developed over several decades. Stanley Whittingham made the first working lithium battery in the 1970s, John Goodenough doubled its voltage in 1980, and Akira Yoshino made a safe, practical version in 1985. Sony began selling them in 1991. The three shared the 2019 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.|2019|Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 2019
The cost of solar power | The average cost of electricity from new large solar farms fell about 85% between 2010 and 2020. | According to the International Renewable Energy Agency, the average cost of electricity from new utility-scale solar power fell by about 85 percent between 2010 and 2020. A mix of larger production, learning by doing and better manufacturing brought the cost down. In many places, solar is now among the cheapest sources of new electricity, cheaper than building new coal or gas plants.|About -85%|IRENA, 2021
Apollo's computer | The guidance computer for the Moon landings had about 4 kilobytes of working memory. | The guidance computer that helped land astronauts on the Moon in 1969 had 2,048 words of erasable memory and 36,864 words of fixed memory, about 4 kilobytes of working memory in modern terms. It weighed about 32 kilograms. A modern phone has millions of times more memory. It was reliable, and it famously handled a series of alarms during the Apollo 11 landing without crashing.|1969|MIT Instrumentation Laboratory
A nanosecond is a foot | Light travels about 30 centimeters in one billionth of a second. | Light travels about 30 centimeters, roughly a foot, in one billionth of a second. The computer scientist Grace Hopper used to hand out pieces of wire cut to that length when she gave lectures, to show people why speed is limited. A signal can't cross a large machine in less time than that. It's why smaller, closer components make faster computers.||Hopper, lectures
Stuxnet | The first known computer worm built to cause physical damage. | Stuxnet was a computer worm discovered in 2010 and designed to attack industrial equipment. It targeted centrifuges used to enrich uranium at an Iranian nuclear plant, making them spin out of control while showing normal readings to the operators. It's widely believed to have been created by the United States and Israel, though neither has officially confirmed it. It was the first known digital weapon to cause physical damage.|2010|Langner, 2011; Zetter, 2014
ENIAC | One of the first electronic general-purpose computers, unveiled in 1946. | The Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer, unveiled at the University of Pennsylvania in 1946, was one of the first general-purpose electronic computers. It filled a large room, weighed about 30 tons and used about 18,000 vacuum tubes. Its first programmers were six women, including Jean Bartik and Betty Holberton, who had to figure out how to program it by wiring and switching, with little documentation.|1946|Penn, 1946
