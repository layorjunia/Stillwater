The invention of zero | Zero as a number, not just a placeholder, came from India. | Many ancient peoples used a mark to hold an empty place in a number, but treating zero as a number you can add and multiply came from India. The Bakhshali manuscript, a birch-bark text carbon-dated to somewhere between the 3rd and 4th centuries, uses a dot for zero. In 628 the astronomer Brahmagupta wrote down rules for arithmetic with zero, including what happens when you add it to or subtract it from other numbers.|628|Brahmagupta, 628; Oxford Bodleian, 2017
Negative numbers | They were used in China 2,000 years ago and resisted in Europe until the 1600s. | Chinese mathematicians were working with negative numbers around 2,000 years ago. The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art used red counting rods for positive numbers and black ones for negative. European mathematicians were much slower to accept them, and for centuries many called negative answers "absurd" or "fictitious," since you can't have less than nothing of something. They only became standard in the 1600s and later.||Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art
Pi | The ratio of any circle's circumference to its diameter, about 3.14159, never ending and never repeating. | Pi is the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter, about 3.14159, and the digits go on forever with no repeating pattern. In 1761 Johann Lambert proved that it can't be written as a fraction. In 1882 Ferdinand von Lindemann proved the stronger result that it's transcendental, not the solution of any ordinary algebraic equation. That showed that the ancient puzzle of "squaring the circle" with a compass and straightedge is impossible.|3.14159|Lambert, 1761; Lindemann, 1882
How many digits of pi do we need? | NASA's navigation uses 15 digits. | You might think space navigation would need thousands of digits, but NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory says that it uses 15 for its most exacting calculations. They've calculated that even for a circle with a radius of about 15 billion miles, farther than Voyager 1 has traveled, 15 digits would put the circumference off by only about an inch. Computers have calculated 100 trillion digits, for the sheer fun of it.|15 digits|NASA/JPL
The number e | About 2.71828, the base of natural growth. | The number e, about 2.71828, appears wherever things grow or decay continuously. Jacob Bernoulli found it in 1683 while asking a question about money: if a bank pays 100 percent interest, what happens if it's compounded more and more often, every month, every day, every second? The amount doesn't grow forever, it approaches e. The number turns up in population growth, radioactive decay and the bell curve.|2.71828|Bernoulli, 1683
Euler's identity | e to the power i times pi, plus 1, equals 0. | Euler's identity is e raised to the power of i times pi, plus one, equals zero. It joins five of the most fundamental numbers in mathematics, e, i, pi, 1 and 0, in a single short equation using addition, multiplication and exponents. It comes from a formula discovered by Leonhard Euler in 1748. Many mathematicians consider it the most beautiful equation, and in a survey of readers it was voted that.|e^(iπ) + 1 = 0|Euler, 1748
Imaginary numbers | The square root of minus one, written i, turns out to be useful. | The square root of minus one can't be an ordinary number, since any number times itself is positive. Yet in 1545 the Italian Girolamo Cardano found that using such "impossible" numbers in the middle of a calculation could give correct, real answers, though he thought they were useless. Today, mathematicians write the square root of minus one as i, and use such numbers for alternating current, signal processing and quantum mechanics.||Cardano, 1545; Bombelli, 1572
There are infinitely many primes | Euclid proved around 300 BCE that the primes never run out. | A prime number is divisible only by one and itself. Around 300 BCE the Greek mathematician Euclid proved that there are infinitely many. His argument is short: suppose you had a complete list of all the primes. Multiply them together and add one. The result isn't divisible by any prime on your list, so there must be another prime, and the list wasn't complete after all. It's still a model of an elegant proof.|c. 300 BCE|Euclid, Elements
The largest known prime | A number with more than 41 million digits. | The largest known prime number has more than 41 million digits. In October 2024 a former NVIDIA engineer, Luke Durant, found it by running software on graphics processors in cloud computers across many countries. It's two raised to the power 136,279,841, minus one, which is the kind of number called a Mersenne prime. The record is usually broken every few years, with the help of volunteer computing.|41M digits|GIMPS, 2024
Goldbach's conjecture | Every even number above 2 is the sum of two primes. | In 1742 the mathematician Christian Goldbach wrote to Leonhard Euler suggesting that every even number greater than two is the sum of two primes. For example, 10 is 3 plus 7, and 28 is 5 plus 23. Computers have checked it for every even number up to about four quintillion, and it's never failed. But nobody has found a proof, and it remains one of the oldest unsolved problems in mathematics.|1742|Goldbach, 1742
Fibonacci numbers | 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13: each is the sum of the two before. | In 1202 Leonardo of Pisa, known as Fibonacci, published a book that included a puzzle about how many pairs of rabbits there would be after a year, if each pair has a new pair every month. The answer gave the sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, where each number is the sum of the two before it. Indian mathematicians had noticed the sequence earlier. Pine cones, sunflower heads and pineapples often show spirals that count in Fibonacci numbers.|1202|Fibonacci, Liber Abaci
The golden ratio | About 1.618, the number where the whole is to the larger part as the larger part is to the smaller. | The golden ratio, about 1.618, is the number you get when a line is cut so that the whole is to the longer part as the longer is to the shorter. It's the limit of the ratio of consecutive Fibonacci numbers. Many people say it's the secret behind the Parthenon, the Mona Lisa and beauty itself, but those claims are mostly myth and wishful measuring. It's a lovely number without needing the legend.||Livio, The Golden Ratio (2002)|mixed
The Pythagorean theorem | In a right triangle, the squares on the two shorter sides add up to the square on the longest. | In any right-angled triangle, the square of the longest side equals the sum of the squares of the other two. A Babylonian clay tablet from about 1800 BCE, known as Plimpton 322, lists number sets that fit the rule, so people knew it a thousand years before Pythagoras. Pythagoras's name is attached to it because the first proofs known to us came from the Greek tradition. It's the basis of how we measure distance.|a² + b² = c²|Neugebauer & Sachs, 1945
The irrational square root of 2 | It cannot be written as a fraction. | The diagonal of a square with sides of length one is the square root of two, and it can't be written as a fraction. The ancient proof is a clever argument by contradiction. Suppose it were a fraction p over q, in lowest terms. Then you can show that both p and q would have to be even, which contradicts "lowest terms." A legend says the Pythagoreans, who believed all numbers were ratios, were deeply disturbed by the discovery.||Euclid, Elements
Some infinities are bigger | There are more real numbers than whole numbers. | It sounds absurd, but there are different sizes of infinity. In 1874 and again in 1891, the German mathematician Georg Cantor showed that you can't make a complete list of all the real numbers, the decimals, even an infinite one. Whatever list you offer, you can build a new number by changing one digit in each entry, and it can't be on the list. So the real numbers are a bigger infinity than the whole numbers. Some contemporaries thought he was mad.|1891|Cantor, 1891
Hilbert's hotel | A hotel with infinitely many rooms can fit new guests even when full. | The German mathematician David Hilbert used a thought experiment in 1924 to illustrate infinity. Imagine a hotel with infinitely many rooms, all full. A new guest arrives. No problem: ask each guest to move to the room with double their number, which leaves all the odd-numbered rooms empty. It shows that with infinite sets, a part can be as big as the whole, which is bizarre.||Hilbert, 1924
Zeno's paradoxes | In a race, the fast Achilles can never catch the tortoise, said Zeno. | Around 450 BCE the Greek philosopher Zeno of Elea argued that the fast runner Achilles could never catch a tortoise with a head start. By the time Achilles reaches where the tortoise was, it's moved on a bit, and so on forever. The puzzle bothered thinkers for two thousand years, until mathematicians showed that an infinite series of ever smaller steps can add up to a finite total, so that Achilles does catch up.|c. 450 BCE|Aristotle, Physics
Calculus | The mathematics of change, built from two ideas: slopes and areas. | Calculus is the mathematics of change. It rests on two ideas: finding the slope of a curve at a point, which tells you the rate of change, and finding the area under a curve, which adds up many small pieces. Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz developed it independently, between the 1660s and 1680s, and their followers carried on a bitter dispute over who deserved the credit. It underlies physics, engineering and economics.||Newton; Leibniz
The seven bridges of Königsberg | Is there a walk that crosses each of the city's seven bridges exactly once? | The city of Königsberg, now Kaliningrad, had seven bridges that connected two islands and the banks of a river. Residents wondered whether you could walk through the city and cross each bridge exactly once. In 1736 Leonhard Euler showed that you could not. He simplified the map to dots and lines, which created graph theory. It now helps describe networks of all kinds, from roads to the internet to friendships.|1736|Euler, 1736
The Möbius strip | A loop with a half twist has only one side and one edge. | Take a long strip of paper, give it a half twist and tape the ends together. You get a Möbius strip, a loop with only one side and one edge. August Möbius and Johann Listing described it independently in 1858. If you draw a line along the middle until you return to the start, you'll find you've covered what looked like both sides. It's a classic example of a surface with surprising properties.||Möbius & Listing, 1858
Topology | In topology a coffee cup and a doughnut are the same shape. | Topology is a branch of mathematics that studies shapes in terms of what stays the same when you stretch, bend or squash them, without tearing. To a topologist, a coffee cup and a doughnut are the same shape, because each has exactly one hole, and one can be smoothly deformed into the other. It might sound like a game, but topology has real uses in physics, biology and data analysis.||Standard topology text
The four color theorem | Any map can be colored with four colors so no neighbors match. | In 1852 a mapmaker's observation raised a question: can any map be colored with just four colors, so that no neighboring regions share a color? For over a century, nobody could prove it. In 1976 Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken did, with the help of a computer that checked nearly 2,000 cases, using over 1,200 hours of computer time. It was the first major theorem proved with a computer, and some mathematicians were uneasy about that.|1976|Appel & Haken, 1976
Fermat's last theorem | No whole numbers satisfy a^n + b^n = c^n for n above 2. | Around 1637 the French lawyer and mathematician Pierre de Fermat scribbled in a book margin that no whole numbers satisfy a to the n plus b to the n equals c to the n for n above two. He added that he had a marvelous proof, but the margin was too small. Nobody found a proof for 357 years. Andrew Wiles finally proved it in 1994, after seven years of working in secret in his attic, using mathematics that didn't exist in Fermat's day.|1637 to 1994|Wiles, 1995
Gödel's incompleteness theorems | In any consistent system of arithmetic, some true statements cannot be proved inside it. | In 1931 the young logician Kurt Gödel proved something startling. In any consistent system of rules rich enough to include arithmetic, there are true statements that can't be proved within the system. It ended the dream of David Hilbert and others of finding a complete and consistent set of rules for all of mathematics. It showed that there are built-in limits to what proof can do.|1931|Gödel, 1931
The halting problem | No program can decide, for every program, whether it will finish or run forever. | In 1936 Alan Turing asked whether it's possible to write a program that can look at any other program and tell, in advance, whether it will eventually stop or run forever. He proved it can't be done, using a clever self-referring argument. It's a real limit on what computers can do, not just a limit of today's technology, and it was one of the first such results in computer science.|1936|Turing, 1936
P versus NP | Can every problem whose answer is quickly checkable also be quickly solved? | Some problems, like solving a big Sudoku or finding the best route through many cities, are hard to solve but easy to check once someone shows you an answer. The question of whether every problem whose answer can be checked quickly can also be solved quickly is called P versus NP. It's one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, with a million-dollar prize. Most experts believe the answer is no, but nobody has proved it.||Clay Mathematics Institute
The Millennium Prize Problems | Seven great problems, with $1 million each; only one is solved. | In 2000 the Clay Mathematics Institute named seven of the deepest unsolved problems in mathematics and offered $1 million for the solution of each. Only one has been solved so far: the Poincaré conjecture, by the Russian mathematician Grigori Perelman, in 2002 and 2003. He declined the prize and the other honors. The rest, including the Riemann hypothesis and P versus NP, remain open.||Clay Mathematics Institute
Benford's law | In many real data sets, about 30% of numbers start with the digit 1. | In many real collections of numbers, like river lengths, populations and stock prices, about 30 percent of them begin with the digit 1, and fewer than 5 percent begin with 9. Simon Newcomb noticed in 1881 that the early pages of logarithm tables were more worn than the later ones, and Frank Benford rediscovered it in 1938. Auditors use it to catch fraud, since invented numbers tend not to follow the pattern.|30%|Newcomb, 1881; Benford, 1938
Zipf's law | The second most common word appears half as often as the first, the third a third as often. | In nearly any large body of text, the most common word appears about twice as often as the second, three times as often as the third, and so on. The linguist George Zipf described it in 1935. In English, "the" makes up about 7 percent of words. Strikingly similar patterns appear in the sizes of cities and the traffic to websites, though no one fully understands why.||Zipf, 1935
The bell curve | Many measurements cluster around an average with symmetric tails. | Many measurements, such as heights, test scores and measurement errors, cluster around an average and thin out symmetrically on either side, in what's called the normal distribution. About 68 percent of values fall within one standard deviation of the average, 95 percent within two, and 99.7 percent within three. Carl Friedrich Gauss used it in the early 1800s to handle errors in astronomical measurements.|68-95-99.7|Gauss, 1809
The central limit theorem | Averages of many independent things tend to form a bell curve, whatever the shape of the originals. | Here's a remarkable fact: if you take the average of many independent random things, the result tends to form a bell curve, whatever the shape of the original distribution. It explains why so many things in nature look bell-shaped. A general version of the theorem was proved by Pierre-Simon Laplace in 1810. It's one of the main reasons that statistics works.||Laplace, 1810
The law of large numbers | With enough trials, averages settle close to the true value. | If you flip a fair coin only a few times, you might get seven heads in ten. But as the number of flips grows, the share of heads gets closer and closer to a half. This is the law of large numbers, proved by Jacob Bernoulli, published in 1713. It's why casinos always win in the long run, and it's also a warning that small samples can easily mislead.||Bernoulli, 1713
The birth of probability | Two letters in 1654 started the mathematics of chance. | In 1654 the French mathematicians Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat exchanged letters about a gambling problem: how should the stakes be divided fairly if a game of chance is interrupted before it's finished? Their solutions laid the foundations of probability theory. Before then, people had played games of chance for thousands of years but had no mathematics to describe them.|1654|Pascal & Fermat, 1654
Shuffling a deck | There are about 8 times 10 to the 67th ways to order 52 cards. | The number of ways to arrange a deck of 52 cards is 52 factorial, which is about 8 followed by 67 zeros. That's so large that every time you properly shuffle a deck, you almost certainly create an order that has never occurred before in the history of the world. If every person on Earth shuffled a deck every second since the start of the universe, they'd barely scratch the surface.|8 × 10^67|Standard combinatorics
The infinite monkey theorem | A monkey typing at random would eventually produce Shakespeare. | The idea that a monkey hitting keys at random on a typewriter would eventually type the complete works of Shakespeare goes back to Émile Borel in 1913. It's true in theory, if given infinite time. But it would take far longer than the age of the universe for any realistic number of monkeys. The theorem is really about the nature of large numbers and chance, not about literature.||Borel, 1913
Logarithms | A way to turn multiplying into adding. | A logarithm turns multiplication into addition. In 1614 the Scottish mathematician John Napier published tables of them, which saved astronomers and navigators years of tedious arithmetic. Slide rules, used by engineers for 350 years, were based on them. Many quantities are measured on logarithmic scales, including earthquakes on the Richter scale, sound in decibels and acidity in pH.|1614|Napier, 1614
The chessboard and the rice | Doubling grains on each square gives more than 18 quintillion on the last. | A legend says the inventor of chess asked a king for a reward of one grain of rice on the first square of the board, two on the second, four on the third, doubling each time. The king agreed, thinking it modest. By the 64th square, the amount would be about 18 quintillion grains, more rice than has ever been grown. It's a classic illustration of exponential growth.|2^64 − 1|Standard exponential growth example
The Collatz conjecture | Take any number; if it's even halve it, if odd triple it and add 1. You always reach 1. | Pick any positive whole number. If it's even, halve it. If it's odd, triple it and add one. Keep going. The conjecture is that you'll always eventually reach 1. Lothar Collatz posed it in 1937, and computers have tested it for every number up to nearly 300 quintillion, but there's no proof. The mathematician Paul Erdős said that mathematics may not be ready for such problems.|1937|Lagarias, 2010
Binary | Computers count in base 2, with just 0 and 1. | Computers use the binary system, where there are only two digits, 0 and 1, instead of our ten. In 1703 the German mathematician Gottfried Leibniz wrote about it, and was fascinated that all numbers could be made from just those two symbols. Each digit is a bit, and eight bits make a byte. Any number, letter, picture or sound can be written in binary, which suits machines that have switches that are either on or off.|1703|Leibniz, 1703
Why 60 minutes in an hour? | The Babylonians counted in base 60. | We divide hours into 60 minutes and minutes into 60 seconds because the ancient Babylonians counted in base 60. It's a handy number: 60 can be divided evenly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30, so fractions are easy. We still use it for time, and the 360 degrees of a circle also come from the Babylonians, from base 60 and the length of the year.||Standard history of mathematics
Euclid's Elements | A 300 BCE textbook that taught rigorous proof for over 2,000 years. | Around 300 BCE the Greek mathematician Euclid wrote the Elements, a textbook that built all of geometry from a handful of simple assumptions, called axioms, by logical proof. It was used for over 2,000 years and became one of the most influential books ever written. Abraham Lincoln is said to have studied it as a young lawyer, to learn how to argue and prove a point.|c. 300 BCE|Euclid, Elements
Non-Euclidean geometry | On a curved surface the angles of a triangle add up to more or less than 180 degrees. | Euclid assumed that, given a line and a point not on it, there's exactly one line through the point that never meets the first. For centuries, mathematicians tried to prove this, until in the 1820s and 30s, Gauss, János Bolyai and Nikolai Lobachevsky showed that you can build consistent geometries without it. On a curved surface, the angles of a triangle add up to more or less than 180 degrees. A century later Einstein used it to describe curved spacetime.||Gauss; Bolyai; Lobachevsky
Fractals | Shapes that look similar at every scale, like a coastline or a fern. | A fractal is a shape that looks similar at every scale, like a coastline, a fern, or a snowflake edge. The mathematician Benoit Mandelbrot named them in 1975. He asked a famous question in 1967, "How long is the coast of Britain?" and showed that there's no single answer: the more finely you measure, the longer it gets. Fractals help describe many natural shapes.|1975|Mandelbrot, 1967, 1975
The butterfly effect | Tiny changes in the starting conditions can lead to huge differences later. | In 1961 the meteorologist Edward Lorenz was running a weather simulation on a computer. To save time, he restarted a run in the middle, typing in 0.506 instead of the full figure, 0.506127. The tiny difference grew until the forecast was completely different. It showed that small changes can lead to large differences later, and it's one reason that weather can only be forecast about ten days ahead.|1961|Lorenz, 1963
Proof by contradiction | Assume the opposite of what you want to prove and show it leads to nonsense. | In this method, you assume the opposite of what you want to prove and show that it leads to an absurdity, so the assumption must be false. It's used to prove that the square root of 2 is irrational, and that there are infinitely many primes. The English mathematician G. H. Hardy wrote that it "is one of a mathematician's finest weapons." It's like checking a door by assuming it's open and finding you'd walk through a wall.||Hardy, A Mathematician's Apology (1940)
Mathematical induction | Prove it for the first case, then prove that each case leads to the next, and it holds for all. | Induction is a way of proving that something is true for all whole numbers. First show that it's true for the first case. Then show that, if it's true for any case, it must be true for the next one. It's like a line of dominoes: if the first falls, and each one knocks over the next, then they all fall. It can prove, for example, that the sum of the first n numbers is n times n plus one, divided by two.||Standard mathematics text
The Gauss story | A teacher asked a young Gauss to add the numbers 1 to 100 to keep him busy. | According to a well-known story, when Carl Friedrich Gauss was about ten, his teacher asked the class to add up the numbers from 1 to 100, hoping to keep them busy. Gauss gave the answer in moments. He saw that 1 plus 100 is 101, 2 plus 99 is 101, and so on, making 50 pairs of 101, which is 5,050. The tale may have been embellished in the retelling, but the method is real and useful.|5,050|Standard mathematics history|mixed
The Mars Climate Orbiter | A 1999 NASA probe was lost because of a units mix-up. | In September 1999 NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter, which had traveled nine months to reach Mars, approached the planet too low and was lost. The cause was a mix-up in units. Software built by one team gave the thrust in pound-force seconds, which is the English system, and the navigation team expected newton-seconds, which is metric. The $125 million spacecraft probably burned up in the atmosphere.|$125 million|NASA, 1999
Honeycomb | Bees build hexagons because they use the least wax to cover a flat area. | Honeybees build their wax cells as hexagons, and a mathematician explains why: it's the most efficient shape. The honeycomb conjecture, which goes back to the ancient Greeks, says that dividing a flat surface into equal-area cells with the least total boundary is best done with a hexagonal grid. Thomas Hales proved it in 1999. Bees use the least wax to store the most honey.|1999|Hales, 2001
Noether's theorem | Every symmetry in nature corresponds to a conservation law. | In 1918 the mathematician Emmy Noether proved a result that physicists consider one of the most beautiful and important. Every symmetry in nature leads to a conservation law. If the laws of physics are the same at all times, energy is conserved. If they're the same everywhere in space, momentum is conserved. When she died, Einstein wrote that she was the most significant creative mathematical genius since women were admitted to higher education.|1918|Noether, 1918
Periodical cicadas | Some cicadas emerge every 13 or 17 years, both prime numbers. | In the eastern United States, some cicadas spend 13 or 17 years underground and then emerge together in huge numbers. Both numbers are primes. One hypothesis is that prime-numbered cycles make it hard for predators or other cicada broods with shorter cycles to line up with them. It's an elegant idea, but it's a hypothesis, and not all biologists are persuaded.||Goles et al., 2001|mixed
The slide rule | For 350 years engineers did calculations by sliding two scales. | Before pocket calculators, engineers used slide rules, two sliding scales marked with logarithms, which turn multiplication into addition by sliding. The English minister William Oughtred invented it around 1622. For about 350 years, it was a standard tool. Apollo astronauts carried slide rules as a backup. Pocket calculators replaced them in the early 1970s.|c. 1622|Standard history of computing
Pi Day | March 14, because 3.14 is the start of pi. | Pi Day is celebrated on March 14, because 3.14 are the first digits of pi. The physicist Larry Shaw organized the first celebration at the Exploratorium museum in San Francisco in 1988, with a march around a circular space, followed by fruit pie. March 14 is also Albert Einstein's birthday. In 2019 UNESCO proclaimed it the International Day of Mathematics.|3/14|Exploratorium, 1988
