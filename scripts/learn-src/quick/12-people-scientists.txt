# People who changed what we know: scientists, doctors, engineers and thinkers, with one fact each worth remembering.

Marie Curie | The first person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences. | Marie Sklodowska was born in Warsaw and moved to Paris to study, and she went on to a career that nobody has matched. In 1903 she shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with her husband Pierre and Henri Becquerel for their work on radioactivity, a word she coined. In 1911 she won the Chemistry prize for discovering polonium and radium. In the First World War she helped equip vehicles with X-ray machines, which soldiers called "little Curies."|1867–1934|Nobel Prize records
Galileo Galilei | He pointed a telescope at the sky and found that the heavens were not perfect. | In 1609 Galileo heard of a Dutch invention and built a better telescope himself, then turned it to the sky. He saw mountains and craters on the Moon, four small moons circling Jupiter and the phases of Venus, all evidence that not everything in the heavens circled the Earth. In 1633 the Inquisition forced him to renounce the Sun-centered view, and he spent the rest of his life under house arrest.|1564–1642|Galileo, Sidereus Nuncius (1610)
Nicolaus Copernicus | He proposed in 1543 that the Earth moves around the Sun. | For more than a thousand years most people believed that the Sun and planets circled the Earth. Copernicus, a Polish astronomer and church official, worked out in detail a system with the Sun at the center. His book On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres was printed in 1543, and according to tradition he was handed a copy on his deathbed. The idea wasn't entirely new, but he supplied the mathematics.|1473–1543|Copernicus, 1543
Johannes Kepler | He found that planets move in ellipses, not circles. | Kepler, a German astronomer, had access to the careful naked-eye observations of Tycho Brahe, and spent years trying to fit Mars's orbit to a circle. It wouldn't fit. Eventually he realized the orbit was an ellipse. He published three laws of planetary motion between 1609 and 1619, which Isaac Newton later used to arrive at his theory of gravity. Kepler also wrote an early work of science fiction about a trip to the Moon.|1571–1630|Kepler, 1609, 1619
Charles Darwin | He spent 20 years gathering evidence before publishing his theory. | Darwin returned from his five-year voyage on HMS Beagle in 1836 with the beginnings of an idea. For more than twenty years, he gathered evidence and told only a few friends. In 1858 he received a letter from the naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace, who had independently come to the same idea. A joint paper was read to the Linnean Society, and Darwin's book On the Origin of Species came out in 1859.|1809–1882|Darwin, 1859
Alfred Russel Wallace | He came up with natural selection independently, while ill with fever on an island. | Wallace was a self-taught naturalist who made a living collecting specimens in the Amazon and in Southeast Asia. In 1858, lying sick with a fever on an island in Indonesia, he thought of natural selection, wrote it up in a few days and sent it to Darwin. Darwin was alarmed to see his own theory, and the two agreed to publish together. Wallace also found that animals on either side of a line through Indonesia are strikingly different, now called Wallace's Line.|1823–1913|Wallace, 1858
Louis Pasteur | He proved that germs cause disease and fermentation. | In the 1860s the French chemist Louis Pasteur showed that microbes, not "spontaneous generation," were responsible for fermentation and spoilage. He boiled broth in flasks with long swan-shaped necks, which let in air but trapped dust and germs, and the broth stayed clear. That supported germ theory, the idea that germs cause disease. In 1885 he gave a boy named Joseph Meister, who'd been bitten by a rabid dog, the first rabies vaccine, and the boy lived.|1822–1895|Pasteur, 1861, 1885
Joseph Lister | He made surgery much safer by cleaning wounds and tools with carbolic acid. | In the 1860s surgeons in hospitals operated in street clothes and wiped their instruments on their aprons, and many patients died of infection afterward. The British surgeon Joseph Lister read about Pasteur's work on germs and began to clean wounds and tools with carbolic acid. In 1867 he reported that deaths among his amputation patients fell from about 45 percent to about 15 percent. The mouthwash Listerine was named after him.|1827–1912|Lister, 1867
Ignaz Semmelweis | He found that doctors' unwashed hands were killing new mothers. | In 1847 the Hungarian doctor Ignaz Semmelweis noticed that women giving birth in the hospital ward where doctors worked were dying of fever at a much higher rate than in the ward staffed by midwives. He realized that doctors were coming straight from autopsies, and he told them to wash their hands in chlorine solution. Deaths fell from about 18 percent to about 2 percent. His colleagues rejected the idea, and he ended his life in an asylum.|1818–1865|Semmelweis, 1861
Edward Jenner | He tested the first smallpox vaccine using cowpox in 1796. | In the 1790s the English country doctor Edward Jenner heard that milkmaids who'd caught cowpox seemed not to get smallpox. In 1796 he took matter from a cowpox sore and scratched it into the arm of an eight-year-old boy, James Phipps. Later he exposed the boy to smallpox, and the boy didn't catch it. The word "vaccine" comes from the Latin word for cow, vacca.|1749–1823|Jenner, 1798
Florence Nightingale | The nurse who was also a pioneering statistician. | Florence Nightingale is remembered as the "Lady with the Lamp," a nurse in the Crimean War, but she was also a brilliant statistician. She gathered data and drew striking charts, including a polar-area diagram, to show that far more soldiers died of disease than of battle wounds. After sanitary reforms at the hospital at Scutari, the death rate dropped from over 40 percent to about 2 percent. In 1858 she became the first woman elected to the Royal Statistical Society.|1820–1910|Nightingale, 1858
Ada Lovelace | She wrote what is considered the first computer program, in 1843. | Ada Lovelace was the daughter of the poet Lord Byron, and she studied mathematics. She translated an article about Charles Babbage's proposed Analytical Engine, a mechanical computer that was never completed, and added her own notes in 1843. They included a step-by-step method for the machine to calculate Bernoulli numbers, which is regarded as the first computer program. She also saw that such a machine could work with symbols and even music, not just numbers.|1815–1852|Lovelace, 1843
Alan Turing | He defined what a computer can do, helped break Enigma and asked whether machines can think. | In 1936 the young British mathematician Alan Turing described an imaginary machine that could carry out any calculation, a model of what we now call a computer. In the Second World War, he worked at Bletchley Park and helped break the German naval Enigma code, work that is thought to have shortened the war. In 1950 he asked whether machines can think, and proposed a test. He was convicted in 1952 for being gay, and the British government pardoned him in 2013.|1912–1954|Turing, 1936, 1950
Grace Hopper | A rear admiral who wrote one of the first compilers, which turns human-readable code into machine code. | Grace Hopper was a mathematician who became a rear admiral in the US Navy. In 1952 she created one of the first compilers, a program that translates instructions into machine code, and she argued that programming should be done in words close to English, which led to the language COBOL. In 1947, when a moth was found stuck in a relay of the Harvard Mark II computer, she taped it into the logbook as the "first actual case of bug being found."|1906–1992|Hopper, 1952
Katherine Johnson | A NASA mathematician whose calculations took Americans to space. | Katherine Johnson was a mathematician at NASA who worked as a human "computer" at a time when Black women were segregated at the agency. She calculated the flight path for Alan Shepard's 1961 mission. In 1962, before John Glenn's orbital flight, the astronaut asked for "the girl" to check the computer's numbers by hand, and he wouldn't fly until she had. President Obama gave her the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2015.|1918–2020|NASA
Rosalind Franklin | Her X-ray image of DNA, Photo 51, revealed its helical structure. | The British chemist Rosalind Franklin was an expert in X-ray crystallography. In 1952 she and her student Raymond Gosling produced "Photo 51," a sharp X-ray image of DNA that showed a helical pattern. Without her knowledge, a colleague showed it to James Watson, and it gave him and Francis Crick crucial information for their 1953 model. She died of ovarian cancer in 1958, at 37, and the Nobel Prize isn't awarded posthumously.|1920–1958|Franklin & Gosling, 1953
Barbara McClintock | She discovered that genes can move around in a chromosome, decades before others believed her. | Barbara McClintock studied corn, and from the 1940s she noticed that certain genes could move from one place on a chromosome to another, changing the color patterns of the kernels. Most scientists didn't believe her, and she stopped publishing about it for years. Decades later, other researchers found similar "jumping genes" in many organisms. She won the 1983 Nobel Prize in Medicine, alone, at the age of 81.|1902–1992|McClintock, 1950; Nobel, 1983
Henrietta Leavitt | A "computer" at Harvard who found a way to measure the distance to stars. | In the early 1900s, Henrietta Leavitt worked at Harvard College Observatory as one of the women hired to measure photographs of stars, for low pay. Studying pulsing stars in the Magellanic Clouds, she noticed that the brighter ones took longer to pulse. That gave astronomers a way of measuring distances to faraway stars and galaxies. Edwin Hubble later used it to show that the Andromeda nebula is a separate galaxy.|1868–1921|Leavitt & Pickering, 1912
Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin | She showed in 1925 that stars are made mostly of hydrogen. | In 1925 Cecilia Payne, then a graduate student at Harvard, concluded in her PhD thesis that stars are made mostly of hydrogen and helium, not of the same mix as the Earth, as scientists had assumed. A prominent astronomer, Henry Norris Russell, persuaded her to describe the result as probably not real. Four years later, he reached the same conclusion by other means and published it, giving her credit. The astronomer Otto Struve called her thesis the most brilliant ever in astronomy.|1900–1979|Payne, 1925
Chien-Shiung Wu | She proved that nature can tell left from right, and was left out of the Nobel Prize. | Physicists believed that nature doesn't distinguish left from right. In 1956 Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang suggested that it might not hold for the weak nuclear force and needed someone to test it. Chien-Shiung Wu, a Chinese-American experimentalist at Columbia, ran a delicate experiment on cobalt-60 at very low temperatures and found that it did break the symmetry. Lee and Yang won the 1957 Nobel Prize in Physics, but she did not.|1912–1997|Wu et al., 1957
Albert Einstein | In 1905, at 26, he published four papers that changed physics. | In 1905, while working as a patent clerk in Bern, Switzerland, the 26-year-old Albert Einstein published four papers in a single year, now called his miracle year. They explained the photoelectric effect, which showed that light comes in packets, proved the existence of atoms by explaining Brownian motion, presented the special theory of relativity, and gave the equation E equals mc squared.|1879–1955|Einstein, 1905
Max Planck | He started quantum theory in 1900 as a reluctant fix. | In 1900 Max Planck was trying to explain the glow of hot objects, and found that the numbers worked only if energy came in tiny packets, which he called quanta. He thought of it as a mathematical trick and was uneasy about it. It took years for physicists, including Einstein, to realize it described something real, and Planck's constant became a foundation of quantum theory. He won the 1918 Nobel Prize in Physics.|1858–1947|Planck, 1900
Niels Bohr | He proposed in 1913 that electrons in atoms occupy fixed energy levels. | In 1913 the Danish physicist Niels Bohr proposed that electrons in atoms can only occupy certain fixed energy levels and give off light when they jump between them. It explained why hydrogen glows in specific colors. He later led the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics. During the Second World War, he helped Jewish scientists escape from Nazi-occupied Europe, and he escaped to Sweden himself in 1943.|1885–1962|Bohr, 1913
Paul Dirac | He predicted antimatter from an equation, before anyone had seen it. | In 1928 the British physicist Paul Dirac wrote down an equation that combined quantum mechanics and special relativity to describe the electron. To his surprise, it had solutions that implied a particle with the electron's mass but opposite charge, which he first thought was the proton. In 1932 Carl Anderson found just such a particle, the positron, in cosmic rays. It was the first evidence of antimatter, and Dirac shared the 1933 Nobel Prize.|1902–1984|Dirac, 1928; Anderson, 1932
James Clerk Maxwell | He showed that electricity, magnetism and light are one thing. | In the 1860s the Scottish physicist James Clerk Maxwell wrote down a set of equations that described how electric and magnetic fields behave. He found that they predicted waves that travel at the speed of light, and he concluded that light itself is an electromagnetic wave. Later, Heinrich Hertz generated such waves, which led to radio. Einstein said that Maxwell's work was the most profound change in physics since Newton.|1831–1879|Maxwell, 1865
Michael Faraday | A bookbinder's apprentice who discovered electromagnetic induction. | Michael Faraday left school at 13 to work as a bookbinder's apprentice, and he read the books he bound. He became assistant to the chemist Humphry Davy, and in 1831 he discovered electromagnetic induction: a changing magnetic field creates an electric current. That's how generators work. He also began the Royal Institution's Christmas Lectures for young people in 1825, which are still held every year.|1791–1867|Faraday, 1831
Nikola Tesla | His alternating-current system made power networks possible. | Nikola Tesla was a Serbian-American inventor who worked briefly for Thomas Edison. He designed a practical alternating-current induction motor, which he patented in 1888, and the American industrialist George Westinghouse bought his patents. In the "war of the currents," Westinghouse and Tesla's alternating current beat Edison's direct current, because AC can be sent over long distances at high voltage. It's how electricity is still distributed today.|1856–1943|Tesla, 1888
Richard Feynman | He showed the Challenger disaster's cause with a glass of ice water. | Richard Feynman was a physicist who won the 1965 Nobel Prize for his work on quantum electrodynamics. He was also a gifted teacher. After the Challenger disaster in 1986, he served on the investigating commission. At a televised hearing, he dropped a piece of a rubber O-ring into a glass of ice water and showed that it lost its springiness in the cold, which explained why the seals had failed on the cold launch morning.|1918–1988|Rogers Commission, 1986
Claude Shannon | He founded information theory and gave us the bit. | In 1937, as a master's student, Claude Shannon realized that electrical circuits could carry out logic, laying the foundations for digital computers. In 1948 he published "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," which founded information theory. It showed that any information can be measured in bits and sent through a noisy channel without error, if coded properly. It's behind everything from phone calls to the internet.|1916–2001|Shannon, 1948
John von Neumann | A mathematician who helped shape computers, game theory and quantum mechanics. | John von Neumann was a Hungarian-American mathematician who made major contributions to quantum mechanics, game theory and the design of computers. The stored-program architecture used by most computers today, in which instructions and data live in the same memory, is still called the von Neumann architecture. His colleagues, many of them brilliant themselves, often said that he was the fastest mind they ever met.|1903–1957|von Neumann, 1945
Tim Berners-Lee | The British scientist who invented the World Wide Web. | In 1989 Tim Berners-Lee, a British computer scientist at CERN in Switzerland, proposed a system for sharing documents through links over the internet. His boss wrote "vague but exciting" on the proposal. In 1990 he built the first web browser and server. In 1993 CERN announced that the technology would be free for everyone to use, with no royalties, which is a big reason the web took off.|b. 1955|CERN, 1993
Carl Linnaeus | He gave every species a two-part Latin name. | The Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus created the system that scientists still use to name living things. In his Systema Naturae of 1735 and in Species Plantarum of 1753, he organized plants and animals into groups, and gave each species a two-part Latin name, a genus and a species. Our own species name, Homo sapiens, means "wise human." Before this, names were long, descriptive phrases.|1707–1778|Linnaeus, 1735, 1753
Alexander von Humboldt | He saw nature as a connected web, long before the word ecology. | The German naturalist Alexander von Humboldt traveled through Latin America from 1799 to 1804, measuring and collecting at every opportunity. He saw nature as an interconnected web, in which climate, altitude and plants were all linked, long before the word ecology existed. He influenced Charles Darwin, who carried his books on the Beagle, and many others, including the writer Henry David Thoreau.|1769–1859|Humboldt, 1807
Mary Anning | A fossil hunter who found some of the first complete ichthyosaur and plesiosaur skeletons. | Mary Anning grew up poor in the English seaside town of Lyme Regis, where she and her family collected fossils from the cliffs and sold them. Around 1811, when she was about twelve, she and her brother found the skeleton of a giant marine reptile, an ichthyosaur. Later she found the first complete plesiosaur. She was largely uncredited by the scientific community, and as a woman she couldn't join the Geological Society.|1799–1847|Natural History Museum, London
Jane Goodall | She watched wild chimpanzees use tools and changed our idea of what makes us human. | In 1960 Jane Goodall, then 26, went to Gombe in Tanzania to study wild chimpanzees. She watched one, whom she called David Greybeard, strip leaves from a twig and use it to fish termites out of a mound. It was the first observation of tool-making in animals other than humans. Her mentor Louis Leakey wrote that we must now redefine tool, redefine man, or accept chimpanzees as human.|b. 1934|Goodall, 1964
Rachel Carson | Her 1962 book "Silent Spring" showed how pesticides harm wildlife and people. | The American marine biologist Rachel Carson published Silent Spring in 1962, a book that described how pesticides such as DDT were poisoning birds and the food chain. It was attacked by the chemical industry, but it changed public opinion. It helped lead to the US ban on DDT for agricultural use in 1972 and the creation of the Environmental Protection Agency. She wrote it while being treated for breast cancer, and died in 1964.|1907–1964|Carson, 1962
Norman Borlaug | The plant scientist whose wheat helped feed hundreds of millions. | The American agronomist Norman Borlaug spent years in Mexico breeding wheat that was high-yielding and resistant to rust disease. In the 1960s his varieties were introduced in India and Pakistan, and yields nearly doubled in a few years, helping to prevent widespread famine. This was a key part of what became known as the Green Revolution. He won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970.|1914–2009|Nobel Peace Prize, 1970
Fritz Haber | He made fertilizer from air, and also poison gas. | The German chemist Fritz Haber invented a way to make ammonia from nitrogen in the air, which made artificial fertilizer possible and now feeds billions. He won the 1918 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. But he also led the German chemical weapons program in the First World War, and personally oversaw the first major chlorine gas attack in 1915. His wife Clara, a chemist who opposed his work, took her own life that year.|1868–1934|Haber, 1909
Jonas Salk | He made the first widely used polio vaccine, and did not patent it. | In 1955 the American physician Jonas Salk announced that his polio vaccine was safe and effective, following a huge trial with 1.8 million children. At the time polio paralyzed and killed thousands every year, and people were jubilant. When the journalist Edward R. Murrow asked who owned the patent, Salk reportedly replied, "Well, the people, I would say. There is no patent. Could you patent the sun?"|1914–1995|Francis report, 1955
Hedy Lamarr | The film star who co-invented frequency hopping. | Hedy Lamarr was a famous film actress in the 1930s and 40s who was also an inventor. In 1941 she and the composer George Antheil filed a patent for a system of "frequency hopping," in which a radio signal rapidly changes frequencies in a pattern known to sender and receiver, to prevent enemy jamming of torpedo guidance. It was granted in 1942. The principle is related to technologies later used in Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and GPS.|1914–2000|US Patent 2,292,387
Wangari Maathai | She started a movement that planted tens of millions of trees. | In 1977 the Kenyan environmentalist Wangari Maathai founded the Green Belt Movement, which paid poor rural women a small amount to plant trees, to restore forests, prevent erosion and provide firewood. Over the decades, the movement planted tens of millions of trees. She was jailed and beaten for her activism. In 2004 she won the Nobel Peace Prize, the first African woman to receive it.|1940–2011|Nobel Peace Prize, 2004
Tu Youyou | She found a malaria medicine in an ancient Chinese herbal text. | In the late 1960s the Chinese government set up a secret project to find a malaria cure, and Tu Youyou led a team that searched ancient Chinese medical texts. A recipe for sweet wormwood gave her an idea: the instructions said to soak the plant in cold water, not boil it, and she realized that heat might destroy the active ingredient. The compound she extracted, artemisinin, has saved millions of lives. She won the 2015 Nobel Prize in Medicine.|b. 1930|Tu, 2011
Srinivasa Ramanujan | A self-taught clerk in India who wrote to Cambridge and astonished G. H. Hardy. | Ramanujan was a clerk in Madras, India, who taught himself advanced mathematics from a single textbook. In 1913 he sent a letter with pages of formulas to the Cambridge mathematician G. H. Hardy, who recognized his genius and brought him to England. Once when Hardy visited him in the hospital, he mentioned that his taxi had a boring number, 1729. Ramanujan replied that it's the smallest number that can be written as the sum of two cubes in two different ways.|1887–1920|Hardy, 1940
Archimedes | The Greek who worked out the lever, buoyancy and the area of the circle. | Archimedes of Syracuse was the greatest scientist of the ancient world. He worked out the principle of the lever, found the rule for floating bodies and calculated the area of a circle. In the famous story, he stepped into a bath, realized how to measure the volume of a king's crown by the water it displaced, and ran through the streets shouting "Eureka." He was killed in 212 BCE when the Romans captured his city.|c. 287–212 BCE|Vitruvius; Plutarch
Eratosthenes | A Greek librarian who measured Earth's size around 240 BCE. | Eratosthenes was the head of the great Library of Alexandria in the 3rd century BCE. He'd heard that at noon on midsummer day, the Sun shone straight down a well at Syene in southern Egypt, while in Alexandria, a stick cast a shadow. From the angle of that shadow and the distance between the two cities, he calculated Earth's circumference. His result was within a few percent of the true value, depending on which unit of length he used.|c. 240 BCE|Cleomedes
Hypatia | A mathematician and astronomer who taught in Alexandria. | Hypatia was a mathematician, astronomer and philosopher in Alexandria, Egypt, in the late 4th and early 5th centuries. She taught students from various religions and was widely respected. In 415 she was killed by a mob during a period of political and religious conflict in the city, and her death has been remembered as a symbol of the loss of ancient learning.|c. 360–415|Socrates Scholasticus
Ibn al-Haytham | A scholar in Cairo who tested theories by experiment in the 11th century. | Ibn al-Haytham, known in Europe as Alhazen, worked in Cairo around the year 1000. In his Book of Optics, he proved that we see because light enters the eye, and not because rays leave the eye, as many ancient thinkers believed. He tested ideas with experiments, used the camera obscura to study light and insisted that claims should be tested. Some historians call him a founder of the scientific method.|c. 965–1040|Book of Optics, c. 1011-21
Al-Khwarizmi | A Baghdad scholar whose name gave us "algorithm" and whose book gave us "algebra." | Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi worked in the House of Wisdom in Baghdad in the early 9th century. His book on solving equations, whose title included the word al-jabr, gave us the word algebra. Another of his books introduced the Hindu-Arabic numerals to the Arab world and, through Latin translations, to Europe. His name, in Latin form, gave us the word algorithm.|c. 780–850|al-Khwarizmi, c. 820
Avicenna | A Persian physician whose Canon of Medicine was used in Europe for 600 years. | Ibn Sina, known as Avicenna in Europe, was a Persian physician and philosopher. His Canon of Medicine, completed around 1025, was a huge encyclopedia that described how infectious diseases can spread, recommended quarantine and set out rules for testing drugs. It was translated into Latin and used as a textbook in European universities for about six centuries, into the 1600s.|980–1037|Avicenna, c. 1025
Aryabhata | An Indian astronomer who said the Earth spins. | The Indian mathematician and astronomer Aryabhata wrote a short, dense book in the year 499. In it he stated that the Earth rotates on its axis, and that this causes the apparent daily motion of the stars, a thousand years before Copernicus. He also gave a value for pi, 3.1416, which is very accurate. India's first satellite, launched in 1975, was named after him.|476–550|Aryabhata, 499
Leonardo da Vinci | A painter whose notebooks show he was also an engineer and anatomist. | Leonardo is famous as the painter of the Mona Lisa and The Last Supper, but he filled thousands of pages of notebooks with studies of anatomy, water, flight and machines. About 7,000 pages survive. He drew the valves of the heart and flying machines centuries before others. But he never published his notes, which were scattered after his death, so they had little influence on science at the time.|1452–1519|Kemp, Leonardo (2004)
Andreas Vesalius | He corrected 1,400 years of anatomy by dissecting bodies himself. | For about 1,400 years, doctors relied on the writings of the Roman physician Galen, who'd mostly dissected animals. In 1543 the young Flemish anatomist Andreas Vesalius published De humani corporis fabrica, based on his own dissections of human bodies, and showed that Galen was wrong in many details. The book had magnificent illustrations, and it transformed the teaching of medicine.|1514–1564|Vesalius, 1543
William Harvey | He proved that blood circulates around the body. | In 1628 the English physician William Harvey published a book arguing that blood circulates around the body, pumped by the heart. He had made a simple calculation: the heart pumps out far more blood in an hour than the body could possibly manufacture, so it must be going around in a circle. It contradicted ancient teaching, and many doctors rejected it for years, but he was right.|1578–1657|Harvey, 1628
Robert Hooke | He coined the word "cell" after looking at cork under a microscope. | In 1665 the English scientist Robert Hooke published Micrographia, a book with spectacular drawings of what he'd seen under his microscope, including a flea and a louse. Looking at a slice of cork, he saw little box-like compartments, which reminded him of monks' cells, and he called them "cells." He was also involved in famous disputes with Isaac Newton over credit for discoveries in optics and gravity.|1635–1703|Hooke, 1665
Antonie van Leeuwenhoek | A cloth merchant who first saw microbes. | Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was a cloth merchant in Delft in the Netherlands who ground his own tiny lenses, some magnifying nearly 270 times, better than any microscope of his time. In the 1670s he looked at pond water, and the plaque on his own teeth, and saw swarms of tiny living things, which he called "animalcules." The Royal Society in London doubted him until Robert Hooke confirmed the observations.|1632–1723|van Leeuwenhoek, 1676
Benjamin Franklin | A printer who invented the lightning rod and studied electricity. | Benjamin Franklin was a printer, writer and diplomat, and a serious scientist. He proposed in 1749 that a pointed iron rod could protect buildings from lightning, and the lightning rod was in use by 1752. He also invented bifocal glasses, made one of the first maps of the Gulf Stream and introduced the terms positive and negative for electric charge.|1706–1790|Franklin, 1751
