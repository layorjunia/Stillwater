# Greek and Latin building blocks of English, and word histories that are well documented.
@series words
@src Oxford English Dictionary; Merriam-Webster etymologies
@conf solid

anti- | Greek for "against" or "opposite." | This Greek prefix means "against" or "opposite," and it turns up all over science and daily life. An antibody is a protein your body makes to fight an invader. An antidote works against a poison. Antisocial behavior goes against society, and an antonym is a word whose meaning runs opposite to another's. So when you meet a word that starts with anti-, ask what it's set against.
auto- | Greek for "self." | From the Greek word for "self," auto- shows up whenever something acts by itself or on itself. An automatic machine works on its own. An autobiography is a life written by the person who lived it. Autonomy means governing yourself. An autograph is your own writing of your own name. The word "automobile" is a car, a vehicle that moves by itself, without a horse.
bi- | Latin for "two." | The Latin prefix bi- means "two," and you'll find it in a lot of everyday words. A bicycle has two wheels. A bilingual person speaks two languages. Binary describes something built from two parts, like the zeros and ones in a computer. A biennial event happens every two years. If you spot bi- at the start of a word, there's probably a pair involved.
tri- | Latin and Greek for "three." | Tri- means "three," and it comes from both Latin and Greek. A triangle has three angles, and a tricycle has three wheels. A trilogy is a set of three connected stories, like a film series told in three parts. A triplet is one of three babies born together. It's an easy prefix to spot, and the idea of three is nearly always there somewhere.
uni- | Latin for "one." | The Latin prefix uni- means "one." A unicorn is a creature with one horn. A uniform is made of one form, so everyone looks the same. Unity is the state of being one. Even "universe" comes from words meaning "turned into one," and the root of "unique" is the same, meaning there's only one of something.
mono- | Greek for "one" or "alone." | Mono- is Greek for "one" or "alone." A monologue is a speech by one person. A monotone is a voice that stays on one note. A monopoly is a market with just one seller. A monarchy is rule by one person. When you see mono- at the start of a word, think of something single or solitary, as in a monorail running on a single track.
poly- | Greek for "many." | The Greek prefix poly- means "many." A polygon is a shape with many angles. A polyglot is someone who speaks many languages, from the Greek for "many tongues." Polytheism is belief in many gods. A polymath is a person who has learned many things, like Leonardo da Vinci. It's the opposite of mono-, and it's often a good clue to what a word means.
multi- | Latin for "many." | This Latin prefix also means "many," and it's more common in modern words. To multiply is to make many. A multitude is a great number of people. Multimedia mixes many forms of media, like text, sound and video. Someone who is multilingual speaks several languages. Think of it as the Latin partner of poly-, used in the same way.
semi- | Latin for "half." | Semi- comes from Latin and means "half." A semicircle is half a circle. A semifinal is the round before the final. A semiconductor is a material that conducts electricity partly, somewhere between a conductor and an insulator, and it's the basis of computer chips. A semi, the truck, is short for semitrailer, a trailer that has wheels only at the back and rests its front on the tractor.
omni- | Latin for "all." | Omni- is Latin for "all." An omnivore eats all kinds of food, both plants and animals. Omnipotent means all-powerful, and omnipresent means present everywhere at once. An omnibus, from the same root, means "for all," and it's where we get the word "bus." The root appears whenever a word is about everything or everyone.
pan- | Greek for "all." | The Greek prefix pan- means "all." A panorama is a view of everything, from the Greek for "all" and "sight." A pandemic is a disease that affects all the people, over a wide area. A panacea is a supposed cure for everything. The Pantheon in Rome was a temple to all the gods. So if you see pan- in a word, it probably means "everything" or "everywhere."
macro- | Greek for "large" or "long." | Macro- comes from a Greek word meaning "large" or "long," and it's used when you look at the big picture. Macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole, like national employment and prices. Something macroscopic is big enough to see with the naked eye. A macrocosm is the large world, as opposed to the small one inside it. It pairs naturally with micro-.
micro- | Greek for "small." | The Greek prefix micro- means "small." A microscope lets you see small things. A microbe is a small living thing. A microchip is a tiny piece of electronics, and microeconomics looks at small-scale choices made by individual people and firms. The prefix is also used in measurement, for a millionth. It's the opposite of macro-.
mega- | Greek for "great" or "a million." | Mega- is from the Greek for "great," and it gets used a lot. A megaphone makes your voice great, or loud. A megabyte is a million bytes of computer memory. A megalith is a great stone, like the ones at Stonehenge. Megalomania is a mania, an obsession, for greatness and power. It's an eye-catching prefix that has become popular in modern names for big things.
hyper- | Greek for "over" or "excessive." | The Greek prefix hyper- means "over" or "too much." Someone hyperactive is too active. Hyperbole, meaning exaggeration, comes from a Greek word for "throwing beyond." Hypertension is high blood pressure, and something hypersensitive reacts too strongly. It's a good clue that a word is about going past the usual limit, in speed, size or amount.
hypo- | Greek for "under" or "below." | Hypo- comes from Greek and means "under" or "below." Hypothermia is when the body temperature drops below normal. A hypodermic needle goes under the skin. The word "hypothesis" means something placed under, a foundation on which an argument is built. A hypocrite is one who plays a part, from the Greek for "an actor," a word that has the same prefix. It's the opposite of hyper-.
inter- | Latin for "between" or "among." | The Latin prefix inter- means "between" or "among." To interact is to act between each other. International means between nations. To interrupt is to break in between. An intermission is a break between two parts of a performance. Even the word "internet" is a network between networks. Whenever you see inter-, something is happening in the space between things.
intra- | Latin for "within." | Intra- is Latin for "within" or "inside." An intravenous drip goes into a vein. An intranet is an internal network, available only within an organization, as opposed to the internet. Intramural sports are played within the walls of a school, between its own teams. Intrapersonal means within a single person. It's the opposite of inter-, and the two are easy to mix up.
extra- | Latin for "outside" or "beyond." | The Latin prefix extra- means "outside" or "beyond." Something extraordinary goes beyond the ordinary. An extraterrestrial comes from beyond Earth. An extrovert is someone turned outward, who gets energy from other people. Extravagant means wandering beyond the limits, which is how it came to describe spending too much. The prefix often signals going past a boundary.
ultra- | Latin for "beyond." | Ultra- is Latin for "beyond." Ultraviolet light lies beyond violet in the spectrum, at wavelengths too short for our eyes to see. Ultrasound is sound beyond the range of human hearing, and doctors use it for pictures of babies in the womb. Ultramarine means "beyond the sea," because the blue pigment was imported from far across the Mediterranean. It's now used to mean "extremely."
trans- | Latin for "across" or "through." | Trans- is Latin for "across" or "through." To transport is to carry across. To translate is to carry a meaning across from one language to another. To transform is to change shape. Something transparent lets light shine through. A transatlantic flight goes across the Atlantic. The prefix is all about crossing from one side to the other, or from one state to another.
circum- | Latin for "around." | The Latin prefix circum- means "around." The circumference is the distance around a circle. To circumnavigate is to sail around something, such as the whole world, as Magellan's expedition did. A circumstance is something that stands around you, the conditions of a situation. To be circumspect is to look around carefully before acting. A circus is a ring, from the same root.
peri- | Greek for "around." | Peri- is Greek for "around." A perimeter is a measure around an area. A periscope lets you look around, or over, obstacles, as on a submarine. A period was originally a path around, a circuit, which is why it can mean a stretch of time. Something peripheral is at the outer edge, around the center. It's the Greek partner of circum-.
sub- | Latin for "under." | The Latin prefix sub- means "under." A submarine travels under the sea, and a subway runs under the street. To subtract is to draw something away from underneath. To submit is to send yourself under another's authority. Sub- often appears as suc-, sup- or sur- before certain letters, as in "suppose" and "suffer." It's one of the most common prefixes in English.
super- | Latin for "above" or "over." | Super- is Latin for "above" or "over." To supervise is to look over someone's work. Something superior is higher in rank or quality. Superhuman goes beyond what a human can do, and the supernatural is above nature. It's the Latin source of "superb" and "supreme," and in everyday speech it's become a general word for "very," as in "super tired."
pre- | Latin for "before." | The Latin prefix pre- means "before." To predict is to say something before it happens. To prevent is to come before an event and stop it. A preview is a look beforehand, and prehistoric describes the time before written records. It's one of the most useful prefixes to know, because it can turn up in front of any word that has a time dimension, like "pre-teen" or "pre-order."
post- | Latin for "after." | Post- is Latin for "after." To postpone is to put something off until later. A postscript, or P.S., is something written after the main letter. A postmortem is an examination after death, and it's also used for any review done after an event. Postwar means after the war. If pre- tells you something comes earlier, post- tells you it comes later.
ante- | Latin for "before," often in time or place. | The Latin prefix ante- means "before," in time or place. An antecedent is something that went before, such as the noun a pronoun refers to. An anteroom is a small room before the main room. Antebellum means "before the war," and in the US, it usually refers to the time before the Civil War. Antediluvian means before the Flood, and is used humorously for anything extremely old. Don't confuse it with anti-.
re- | Latin for "again" or "back." | The Latin prefix re- means "again" or "back." To return is to turn back. To review is to see again. To rebuild is to build again, and to recall is to call back to mind. It's one of the most productive prefixes in English: you can stick it onto almost any verb to say that something is done a second time, from "retry" to "rewrite."
retro- | Latin for "backward." | Retro- is Latin for "backward." To think about the past is retrospect, looking back. A retroactive law applies to things that happened before it was passed. Retrograde describes backward motion, like the apparent backward movement of a planet in the sky. To retrofit is to add new equipment to something old. These days "retro" by itself means in the style of the past.
pro- | Latin for "forward," "for" or "in favor of." | Pro- comes from Latin and Greek, and has several meanings: "forward," "in front of," and "in favor of." Progress is a step forward. To promote is to move something forward. To be proactive is to act ahead of events, instead of waiting. A proponent is someone who speaks in favor of something. Its opposite in the "in favor of" sense is con, as in the old phrase "pros and cons."
con-, com- | Latin for "with" or "together." | This Latin prefix means "with" or "together," and it changes its spelling depending on the next letter. To connect is to join together. A community is a group of people together. To compose is to put things together, and a conference is a gathering that brings people together. When you see con- or com- at the start of a word, it's usually about joining, sharing or doing something collectively.
contra- | Latin for "against." | The Latin prefix contra- means "against." To contradict is to speak against what someone says. Something contrary is opposite. To contrast is to stand things against each other and show their differences. Contraband is goods brought in against the ban, in other words smuggled. It's a cousin of anti-, but it often suggests opposition from the other side rather than a flat rejection.
dis- | Latin for "apart" or "not." | Dis- is a Latin prefix meaning "apart," "away" or "not." To disagree is to not agree. To disconnect is to unjoin. To disperse is to scatter apart, and to dismiss is to send someone away. It can reverse the meaning of a word, as in "dislike" or "disappear," which makes it easy to coin new words. Its meaning is nearly always some form of separation or negation.
ex- | Latin for "out" or "from." | The Latin prefix ex- means "out" or "from." To exit is to go out. To extract is to draw out, like a tooth or a lesson from a story. To exclude is to shut out. To expel is to drive out. The prefix also means "former" when used with a hyphen, as in "ex-president." The core meaning is movement away from the inside.
de- | Latin for "down," "away" or "reversal." | The Latin prefix de- means "down," "away" or "reversal." To descend is to climb down. To decline is to lean away, and to deform is to undo the form. To depart is to go away. It can also reverse an action, as in "defrost" and "decode." So de- often means that something is being taken off, taken down or undone.
ab- | Latin for "away from." | Ab- is Latin for "away from." Something abnormal is away from the norm. To be absent is to be away from a place. To abduct is to lead someone away by force. To abstract is to draw an idea away from the particulars. It becomes "abs-" or "a-" before some letters, as in "abstain" and "avert." The idea is separation, getting away from something.
ad- | Latin for "to" or "toward." | The Latin prefix ad- means "to" or "toward." To adhere is to stick to something. To advance is to move forward, toward. Adjacent means lying next to. To admire, from the Latin for "to wonder at," is to look at something with wonder. Ad- changes to match the next letter, as in "attract," "assist" and "arrive." It's the opposite of ab-.
ambi- | Latin for "both." | Ambi- is Latin for "both." An ambidextrous person is equally skilled with both hands, though the word literally means "right-handed on both sides." To be ambivalent is to have both feelings at once. Something ambiguous leads in both directions, so it has more than one meaning. Ambient means going around, which is how we describe the surrounding light or sound.
a-, an- | Greek for "without" or "not." | The Greek prefix a-, or an- before a vowel, means "without" or "not." An atheist is without belief in a god. Something amoral is without moral sense. Anonymous means without a name. Asymmetry means not symmetrical. It's a handy way of making an opposite, and in medicine, "anemia" literally means "without blood."
mal- | Latin for "bad" or "badly." | Mal- is Latin for "bad" or "badly." A malfunction is a bad function, a failure to work. Malicious means full of ill will. Malnutrition is bad nutrition. Malaria comes from Italian words meaning "bad air," from the old belief that the disease came from foul air in swamps, before it was found to be spread by mosquitoes. It's the opposite of bene-.
dys- | Greek for "bad" or "difficult." | Dys- comes from Greek and means "bad," "difficult" or "disordered." Dyslexia is difficulty with reading words. Dysfunction is bad function. A dystopia is a bad place, the opposite of a utopia, a word invented by Thomas More. Dysentery is a painful disease of the intestines. In medicine, many disorders begin with dys-, and it generally signals that something isn't working properly.
syn-, sym- | Greek for "together." | This Greek prefix means "together" or "with." Synonyms are words that have the same meaning, literally "same name." To synchronize is to make things happen at the same time. A symphony is a sounding together, an orchestra's piece. Sympathy is feeling together with another person. Before certain letters, syn- turns into sym-, as in "symmetry" and "symbol."
para- | Greek for "beside" or "beyond." | The Greek prefix para- means "beside" or "beyond." Parallel lines run alongside each other. A paragraph was originally a mark written beside the text to show a break. A paramedic works beside, or alongside, the doctor. A parable is a story placed beside a lesson, to illustrate it. It also appears in "paranormal," meaning beyond the normal.
neo- | Greek for "new." | Neo- comes from the Greek for "new." Neonatal means relating to newborn babies. A neologism is a new word, like many that were invented by the internet. Neoclassical describes a revival of the style of ancient Greece and Rome. Even the gas neon takes its name from the same root, since its discoverers called it "the new one" in 1898.
bene- | Latin for "well" or "good." | The Latin prefix bene- means "well" or "good," and it appears in a family of positive words. A benefit is a good result, from "well done." Someone benevolent wishes others well. Benign means kind or harmless, and a benediction is a good word, a blessing. It's the opposite of mal-. If you ever feel unsure about the meaning of a word starting with bene-, assume that it's good.
aqua | Latin for "water." | The Latin word aqua means "water." An aquarium is a place for water animals. Something aquatic lives in or near water. An aqueduct is a channel for carrying water, such as the great stone ones the Romans built. An aquifer is a layer of rock that holds underground water. The same root gives us "aquamarine," the color of seawater, which is also the name of a blue gem.
aud | Latin for "hear." | Aud comes from the Latin verb audire, "to hear." An audience is a group who listen. Audio is sound, the thing heard. An auditorium is a place for hearing, a hall. Audible means able to be heard, and an audition is a hearing of a performer. Even "audit" comes from it, since early auditors listened to people read out accounts.
bio | Greek for "life." | Bio comes from the Greek word for "life." Biology is the study of life. A biography is the writing of a life. An antibiotic is a medicine against bacteria, literally "against life," although it's against only the harmful kind. A biome is a large community of living things, like a desert or rainforest. It's one of the most productive roots in science.
cap, capt, cept | Latin for "take" or "seize." | This root comes from the Latin verb capere, "to take" or "seize." To capture is to take hold of. To accept is to take something to yourself. A concept is a thing taken together in the mind, an idea. To intercept is to take something between its sender and receiver. The spellings vary with the word, but the idea of grasping is always in there.
ced, ceed, cess | Latin for "go" or "yield." | This Latin root means "to go" or "to yield." To proceed is to go forward. To recede is to go back, like a hairline or a tide. Access is a way of going to a place. To concede is to yield, or give way, in an argument. And "ceed" is the spelling in only three common English words: exceed, proceed and succeed.
chron | Greek for "time." | Chron comes from the Greek word for "time." Chronology is the order of events in time. To synchronize is to make things happen at the same time. A chronic illness is one that lasts a long time. An anachronism is something that doesn't belong in its time, like a clock in a play set in ancient Rome. Chronos was also the Greek personification of time.
cide, cis | Latin for "kill" or "cut." | This Latin root means "to kill" or "to cut." Homicide is killing a person, and suicide is killing oneself. A pesticide kills pests. An incision is a cut into something, like the one a surgeon makes. Scissors, precise, and decide, which means to cut off other options, are relatives. So is "concise," cut down to the essentials.
clud, clus | Latin for "close" or "shut." | Clud comes from the Latin word for "to close" or "to shut." To include is to shut something in. To exclude is to shut it out. A conclusion is a closing, the end of an argument. Claustrophobia, the fear of closed spaces, comes from the same root. A cloister is a closed courtyard, and a clause was originally a closing part of a sentence.
cogn | Latin for "know." | Cogn comes from the Latin cognoscere, "to know." To recognize is to know again. Cognitive means relating to knowing and thinking, as in cognitive science. Incognito means unknown, hiding who you are. To be cognizant is to be aware of something. It's related to the English word "know" itself, through a common ancestor older than Latin.
corp | Latin for "body." | From the Latin corpus, "body," this root shows up in more words than you might expect. A corporation is a legal body made of many people. A corpse is a dead body. A corps, pronounced "core," is a body of people, like the Peace Corps. To incorporate is to put something into a body. Even "corpulent" comes from it, meaning having a large body.
cred | Latin for "believe" or "trust." | Cred comes from the Latin credere, "to believe" or "to trust." Credit is trust that you'll pay later. Something credible is believable, and incredible is not believable, though we now use it to mean amazing. A creed is a statement of belief, from the Latin word for "I believe." A credential is something that makes people trust you.
cycl | Greek for "circle" or "wheel." | This Greek root means "circle" or "wheel." A cycle is a circle of events that repeats. A bicycle has two wheels. A cyclone is a circling storm. An encyclopedia was originally a "circle of learning," a complete course of education. Even "cycle" in a washing machine follows the idea: a series of steps that returns to the start.
dem | Greek for "people." | Dem comes from the Greek demos, meaning "the people." Democracy is rule by the people. Demographics are statistics about a population. An epidemic is a disease that comes upon the people, spreading through a community. A demagogue is a leader who appeals to the people's emotions. The ancient Greek city of Athens gave us both the idea and the word.
dict | Latin for "say" or "speak." | From the Latin dicere, "to say" or "speak," this root fills our vocabulary of speech. To dictate is to say aloud for someone else to write. To predict is to say before. A verdict is a true saying, the decision of a jury. To contradict is to speak against. A dictionary is a book of sayings, or words. Diction is the way you speak.
duc, duct | Latin for "lead." | This Latin root, from ducere, means "to lead." To produce is to lead forth, to bring something out. To reduce is to lead back, to make smaller. To conduct is to lead with, as in guiding an orchestra or electricity. Education comes from "leading out," drawing out what's in a person. A duke was originally a leader, and a duct is a channel that leads air or water.
equi | Latin for "equal." | Equi comes from the Latin aequus, "equal" or "even." Something equal is the same in measure. An equation says that two things are equal. Equilibrium means equal balance, when forces cancel out. An equinox, in spring and fall, is the time when day and night are equal in length, from "equal night." Equity, meaning fairness, is also related.
fac, fact, fect | Latin for "make" or "do." | From the Latin facere, "to make" or "to do," this root gives us many words about making. A factory is a place of making. To manufacture was originally to make by hand. An effect is something made by a cause. Something perfect is completely made, or finished. A fact was originally a thing done, and facile means easy to do.
fer | Latin for "carry" or "bring." | This Latin root from ferre means "to carry" or "to bring." To transfer is to carry across. To refer is to carry back, as in referring to a book. A ferry carries people across water. A conifer is a tree that bears cones. Fertile means able to bear fruit, and a "conference" is a bringing together. It's one of those roots that appear in many short words.
fid | Latin for "faith" or "trust." | Fid comes from the Latin fides, "faith" or "trust." Fidelity is faithfulness, which is why the motto of the US Marine Corps is "Semper Fidelis." To confide is to trust someone with a secret. Being confident is having trust, especially in yourself. A fiancé is a person who has pledged to marry. The common dog name "Fido" is the Latin for "I trust."
flect, flex | Latin for "bend." | This Latin root means "to bend." To reflect is to bend back, as a mirror does with light, or as your mind does with a memory. Something flexible can bend without breaking. An inflection is a bend in the voice, or in a word's ending. To deflect is to bend something away from its course. A flexible schedule is one that can bend as needed.
form | Latin for "shape." | From the Latin forma, "shape," this root gives us many words. To transform is to change shape. A uniform has one shape, so everyone looks alike. To reform is to give something a new shape, or improve it. A formula was originally a little form, a small pattern or rule. Information is what gives a shape to your thoughts.
fort | Latin for "strong." | Fort comes from the Latin fortis, "strong." To fortify is to make strong, as with a castle wall. Effort is strength put out, the force you apply. Fortitude is inner strength, the courage to endure. To comfort someone is to strengthen them with your presence. A fort is a strong place. Even "force" is closely related.
frag, fract | Latin for "break." | This Latin root, from frangere, means "to break." Something fragile is easily broken. A fracture is a break, especially in a bone. A fraction is a broken piece of a whole number. To refract is to break back, which is what happens to light when it bends as it moves from air into water. Fragments are broken pieces.
gen | Latin and Greek for "birth," "kind" or "origin." | Gen is an old root from Latin and Greek that means "birth," "kind" or "origin." To generate is to give birth to something, to produce it. Genesis is origin, the first book of the Bible, a word that means "beginning." A genre is a kind of art or writing. A gene is a unit of heredity, passed from parent to child. It's a very productive root.
geo | Greek for "earth." | Geo comes from the Greek word for "earth." Geography is earth writing, the description of the planet. Geology is the study of the earth, its rocks and history. Geometry was originally earth measure, because it began with measuring land in ancient Egypt. Geothermal energy comes from the earth's heat. It's a good clue that a word concerns the planet.
graph, gram | Greek for "write" or "draw." | This Greek root means "to write" or "to draw." An autograph is your own writing of your name. A telegram is writing that goes far, a message sent by wire. A graph is a drawn diagram. A photograph is light writing, and grammar is the art of letters and writing. It's why we use "graphic" for images that are drawn or written.
grat | Latin for "pleasing" or "thankful." | Grat comes from the Latin gratus, "pleasing" or "thankful." To be grateful is to be full of thanks. Gratitude is thankfulness. Gratis means free of charge, given as a favor. To congratulate is to rejoice together with someone. A gratuity is a tip, a pleasing extra. The Spanish "gracias" and French "grâce" come from the same root.
gress, grad | Latin for "step" or "go." | This Latin root means "step" or "go," from gradus, a step. To progress is to step forward. To graduate is to step up a grade, a level. Something gradual happens step by step, bit by bit. To regress is to step back. A "grade" in school is a step in the ladder, and a "gradient" is a slope you can step up or down.
hydr | Greek for "water." | Hydr comes from the Greek word for "water." To hydrate is to add water to the body. A hydrant is an outlet where water comes out. Hydroelectric power makes electricity from moving water. To dehydrate is to remove water. The element hydrogen is named for it too, because when it burns, it produces water.
jud, jus | Latin for "law" or "right." | This Latin root means "law" or "right." A judge is a person who speaks the law. Justice is rightness, fairness. Injustice is its opposite. Prejudice is a judgment made before knowing the facts. A jury is a group sworn to give a lawful verdict. Words related to law and courts often share this root.
junct | Latin for "join." | Junct is Latin for "join," from jungere. A junction is a place where things join, like a road meeting another. A conjunction is a word that joins others, such as "and" or "but." An adjunct is something joined to a main thing as an extra. An injunction is, originally, a command that joins someone to an obligation, a court order.
lab | Latin for "work." | Lab comes from the Latin labor, "work." A laboratory is a place of work, where experiments are done. To elaborate is to work something out in detail. To collaborate is to work together with others. Labor is work itself, especially hard work. A related word, "laborious," means taking much effort, and "belabor" means to work at something too long.
liber | Latin for "free." | From the Latin liber, "free," this root has to do with freedom. Liberty is the state of being free. To liberate is to set free. Someone liberal was originally generous, free-giving, before the word took on its political meanings. To deliver is to set free, as in delivering someone from danger. The Statue of Liberty is named from it.
log, logy | Greek for "word," "reason" or "study." | This Greek root, from logos, means "word," "reason" or "study." A dialogue is words exchanged between two people. Biology is the study of life. Logic is the art of reason. A prologue is words that come before the main part of a story. "Logos" had deep meanings for the Greeks, including the idea of an order behind things, which is why it appears at the start of the Gospel of John.
luc, lum | Latin for "light." | This Latin root means "light." Lucid describes something clear and bright, such as a lucid explanation. To illuminate is to throw light on something. Something luminous gives off light. Translucent means letting light through, but not clearly. The name Lucy and the word "Lucifer," meaning light-bringer, come from the same root, as does "lunar," relating to the moon.
magn | Latin for "great." | Magn comes from the Latin magnus, "great." To magnify is to make something appear greater. Something magnificent is great in deed or appearance. Magnitude is size or greatness, as in the magnitude of an earthquake. Someone magnanimous has a great soul, and is generous. The word "magnum" for a large bottle of wine comes from it, too.
man | Latin for "hand." | This root comes from the Latin manus, "hand." A manual is done by hand, or a book that you hold in your hand. A manuscript is handwritten. To manufacture was to make by hand. To manipulate is to handle something skillfully, or to handle people. A manicure is hand care, and to "emancipate" is to release from the hand, to set free.
mar | Latin for "sea." | Mar comes from the Latin mare, "sea." Something marine belongs to the sea. A submarine travels under the sea. Maritime means relating to the sea, as in maritime law. A mariner is a sailor. The Spanish word for sea is "mar," and the name of the planet Mars comes from a different root, as does the month March.
mem | Latin for "remember." | From Latin memor, meaning "mindful," this root is about remembering. Memory is the power to remember. A memorial is something that helps people remember, like a statue. A memento is a reminder, a keepsake. To remember is, literally, to bring back to mind. A memorandum is "something to be remembered," a note, and a memoir is a written memory.
meter, metr | Greek for "measure." | This Greek root, from metron, means "measure." A thermometer measures heat. Geometry measures the earth. Symmetry means measured together, with matching parts. A diameter measures across a circle. A kilometer is a thousand measures, a meter. The word "meter" for a unit of length was set up in France in the 1790s.
migr | Latin for "move." | From the Latin migrare, "to move," this root is about changing places. To migrate is to move from one place or region to another, as birds do. An immigrant moves into a country, and an emigrant moves out of it. Migration is the movement of a group. Both words describe the same journey seen from opposite ends.
mit, miss | Latin for "send." | This Latin root means "to send." To transmit is to send across, like a radio signal. A mission is a sending, a task someone is sent to do. To dismiss is to send away. To permit is to send through, to allow. A missile is something sent, and an emissary is someone sent out. It's one of the most common roots in English.
mort | Latin for "death." | Mort comes from the Latin mors, "death." Something mortal is subject to death, and immortal is not. A mortgage is a "dead pledge": the pledge ends, or dies, when the loan is paid off or when the debt is lost. A mortuary is a place for the dead. Even "mortified" means feeling as if you could die of shame.
mov, mot | Latin for "move." | This Latin root means "to move." To remove is to move away. Motion is moving. Emotion is a moving out, since feelings stir us from calm. To promote is to move forward. A motor is something that moves, and a "motive" is what moves you to act. A movie is a moving picture.
nat | Latin for "born." | From the Latin nasci, "to be born," this root connects birth and nature. Something native is born in a place. Nature is the way things are born and grow. A nation is people born together in the same land. Prenatal means before birth. Natal means relating to birth, and "innate" means born in you, as in an innate talent.
nov | Latin for "new." | Nov is the Latin root for "new." A novel was once simply something new, which is why the book form got that name when it was a new kind of story. To innovate is to make something new. To renovate is to make it new again. A novice is a new learner, a beginner. The month November has a different origin, as "ninth month."
pater, patr | Latin for "father." | This Latin root means "father." Paternal means fatherly. A patriot loves the fatherland, from the Latin for "country of one's fathers." A patron was a protector, like a father, who looked after his dependents. Paternity is fatherhood. Even "pattern" may come from the same root, since a father was a model to copy, though that origin is debated.
mater, matr | Latin for "mother." | Mater is the Latin word for "mother." Maternal means motherly. Matrimony is the state of marriage, from a Latin word that meant the condition of being a mother. A matriarch is a woman who rules a family or tribe. The word "matter" is closely related to it, and so is "material," the stuff things are made from.
path | Greek for "feeling" or "suffering." | This Greek root, from pathos, means "feeling" or "suffering." Sympathy is feeling with someone. Empathy is feeling into someone else's situation. Apathy is a lack of feeling. Pathology is the study of disease. A psychopath is someone with a diseased mind. "Pathetic" originally meant arousing pity, and "pathos" is the quality in a story that makes us feel for the people in it.
ped | Latin for "foot." | Ped comes from the Latin pes, "foot." A pedal is a lever that you push with your foot. A pedestrian is someone who goes on foot. A centipede has, or seems to have, a hundred feet. To expedite is to free the feet, from a Latin word for getting something out of a snare, so that it can be quick. A pedicure is foot care.
pod | Greek for "foot." | Pod comes from the Greek word for "foot," a cousin of the Latin ped. A podium is a place to stand. A tripod is a stand with three feet. An octopus has eight feet, or arms. A podiatrist is a foot doctor. Even "antipodes," the opposite side of the world, means those whose feet are opposite ours.
pend, pens | Latin for "hang" or "weigh." | This Latin root means "to hang" or "to weigh." To suspend is to hang something under. A pendulum is a hanging weight that swings. Expensive means weighed out, because money used to be weighed, and so is "expend." A pension was a payment weighed out regularly. To "depend" is to hang from something else.
phil | Greek for "love." | Phil comes from the Greek philein, "to love." Philosophy is the love of wisdom. Philanthropy is love of humankind, giving to help others. The city of Philadelphia was named for the Greek for "brotherly love." A bibliophile is a lover of books. A "philharmonic" orchestra is one that loves harmony.
phon | Greek for "sound" or "voice." | From the Greek phone, "sound" or "voice," this root is about sound. A telephone carries sound far. A symphony is sounds together. Phonics teaches reading by the sounds of letters. A microphone makes small sounds bigger. The word "phonetic" describes spelling that follows pronunciation, and "saxophone" is named for its inventor and its sound.
photo | Greek for "light." | Photo comes from the Greek phos, "light." A photograph is a drawing or writing with light. Photosynthesis is how plants put together sugar using light. A photon is a particle of light, the smallest unit. A photocopy is a copy made with light. In the 1830s the astronomer John Herschel is credited with first using the word "photography."
plic, ply | Latin for "fold." | This Latin root, from plicare, means "to fold." To apply is to fold something onto something else, to bring it to bear. Something complicated is folded together, tangled. To reply is to fold back an answer. Implicit means folded in, not stated openly. The word "simple" comes from the same root, meaning "one fold," and "duplicate," from "two folds."
port | Latin for "carry." | Port comes from the Latin portare, "to carry." To transport is to carry across. To import is to carry in, and to export is to carry out. Something portable can be carried easily. A porter carries your bags. The word "support" means to carry from below, and "report" means to carry back news.
psych | Greek for "mind," "soul" or "breath." | From the Greek psyche, "mind," "soul" or "breath," this root is about the mind. Psychology is the study of the mind. A psychiatrist is a healer of the mind, a medical doctor. The psyche is the soul or inner self. Something psychosomatic involves both mind and body. In Greek myth, Psyche was a mortal woman who became the goddess of the soul.
quer, quis | Latin for "ask" or "seek." | This Latin root means "to ask" or "to seek." A query is a question. A question is a thing asked. To inquire is to ask into something. To request is to ask again and again, or just to ask. A quest is a search, and an inquisition is an investigation. Even "exquisite" comes from it, meaning sought out, and so, choice.
rupt | Latin for "break." | Rupt is Latin for "break," from rumpere. To erupt is to break out, like a volcano. To interrupt is to break between. To be bankrupt is to be broken financially, from Italian words meaning "broken bench." To corrupt is to break together, or ruin. A "rupture" is a break, and "abrupt" means broken off, sudden.
scrib, script | Latin for "write." | This Latin root means "to write." To describe is to write down what something is like. Scripture is a writing, especially a sacred one. To prescribe is to write before, as a doctor does with medicine. To subscribe is to write under, to sign your name, as to a magazine. A script is writing for actors, and a manuscript is handwritten.
sect | Latin for "cut." | Sect comes from the Latin secare, "to cut." A section is a cut part of something. To dissect is to cut apart, as a biology student does with a frog. An insect is a cut-up creature, named for its body, which looks as if it's cut into segments. To intersect is to cut between, as two roads cross. A "sector" is a cut piece of a circle, too.
sens, sent | Latin for "feel" or "sense." | This Latin root means "to feel" or "to sense." A sense is a way of feeling the world. A sentiment is a feeling. To consent is to feel together, to agree. To resent is to feel again, a lasting bitterness. Something sensible is full of sense. "Sensation" and "sensitive" belong to the same family.
spect | Latin for "look." | Spect comes from the Latin specere, "to look." To inspect is to look into. A spectator is someone who looks on. Perspective is looking through, a way of seeing things. To respect is to look back at someone, to regard them with honor. A spectacle is something worth looking at, and "suspect" means to look at from below, with doubt.
spir | Latin for "breathe." | From the Latin spirare, "to breathe," this root links breath with spirit. To inspire is to breathe into, to fill with an idea or feeling. Respiration is breathing again and again. To expire is to breathe out, or to breathe one's last. To conspire is to breathe together, to whisper a plot. The word "spirit" itself is "the breath of life."
struct | Latin for "build." | This Latin root from struere means "to build." A structure is a built thing. To construct is to build together. To instruct is to build up knowledge in someone's mind. To destroy is to unbuild, to take down. An "instrument" is a tool for building, and "industry" is related, as organized work.
tact, tang | Latin for "touch." | Tact comes from the Latin tangere, "to touch." Contact is touching together. Something tactile is related to touch. Something tangible can be touched, so it's real and solid. Something intact has not been touched, and so is whole. And "tact," the skill of handling delicate situations, means a delicate touch.
techn | Greek for "art" or "skill." | Techn is from the Greek techne, "art" or "skill." Technique is the way of doing a skill. Technology is the study of skills and craft, and now of machines. A technician is a skilled worker. A polytechnic teaches many skills. For the Greeks, techne covered both craft and art, and that's why the root can sound artistic as well as technical.
tele | Greek for "far." | Tele is the Greek word for "far" or "at a distance." The telephone carries sound far away. The telescope looks far, and television sees far. Telepathy would be feeling far, a communication of minds. The prefix is useful in new words as technology advances, such as "teleconference" and "telecommute."
tend, tens | Latin for "stretch." | This Latin root means "to stretch." To extend is to stretch out. Tension is a stretching, a pulling tight. Something intense is stretched tight, to a high degree. To pretend is to stretch something forward, like a cover. A tent is stretched cloth, and a "tendon" is a cord that stretches from a muscle.
terr | Latin for "earth" or "land." | Terr comes from the Latin terra, "earth" or "land." Terrain is the ground, its shape and features. A territory is a piece of land. A terrace is a raised, flat platform of earth. Something terrestrial is of the earth. A terrier is a dog that goes into the earth after burrowing animals, and a "terrarium" is a little land habitat.
the, theo | Greek for "god." | This Greek root, from theos, means "god." Theology is the study of god or the divine. An atheist is someone without belief in god. Enthusiasm originally meant to have the god within you, as with a prophet in a trance. Monotheism is belief in one god. A theocracy is a state ruled in the name of god.
therm | Greek for "heat." | Therm is from the Greek therme, "heat." A thermometer measures heat. A thermostat is a device that keeps heat steady. Thermal means relating to heat. Hypothermia is low body heat, a dangerous cold. A thermos bottle keeps drinks hot or cold. The root appears whenever something concerns temperature.
tract | Latin for "pull" or "drag." | This Latin root, from trahere, means "to pull" or "to drag." To attract is to pull toward. A tractor is a machine that pulls. To subtract is to pull away from underneath. To distract is to pull apart your attention. A "contract" pulls people together, and "traction" is the pulling grip of a tire.
urb | Latin for "city." | Urb comes from the Latin urbs, "city." Something urban is of the city. A suburb is under, or near, the city. Urbane means polished and refined, as city people were once thought to be. A conurbation is a group of cities that have grown together. "Urban" and "rural" are opposites.
vac | Latin for "empty." | This Latin root, from vacare, means "empty." Something vacant is empty. A vacuum is an empty space, with no air. To evacuate is to empty out a place, such as a building in an emergency. A vacation is a time emptied of work, or school. "Vacate" means to leave a place empty.
ven, vent | Latin for "come." | Ven comes from the Latin venire, "to come." An event is a coming out, something that happens. To prevent is to come before. An adventure is a thing that is about to come, a chance event. A convention is a coming together of people. "Revenue" is what comes back, and "avenue" is a way of coming to a place.
ver | Latin for "true." | Ver is Latin for "true," from verus. To verify is to make true, by checking. A verdict is a true saying, the jury's decision. Veracity is truthfulness. To aver is to declare something to be true. Even the word "very," as in "the very truth," comes from it, from an Old French word for true.
vert, vers | Latin for "turn." | This Latin root means "to turn," from vertere. To convert is to turn around, to change. To reverse is to turn back. An introvert is someone turned inward. Something versatile turns easily, from task to task. A "version" is a turning, a different telling, and "adverse" means turned against.
vid, vis | Latin for "see." | This Latin root means "to see," from videre. Video means "I see." Vision is a seeing, the power of sight. Evident means clearly seen, obvious. To supervise is to see over, to oversee. "Visit" is to go to see someone. The word "television" is a hybrid, Greek tele for far and Latin visio for sight.
vit, viv | Latin for "life." | Vit and viv come from the Latin vita, "life." Something vital is of life, essential to it. Something vivid is full of life, bright. To survive is to live beyond, to outlive danger. A vitamin was named "vitamine" in 1912, a life amine, because these nutrients are necessary to life. The word "vivacious" describes a lively person.
voc, vok | Latin for "call" or "voice." | Voc comes from the Latin vocare, "to call," and vox, "voice." Vocal means of the voice. To invoke is to call upon, as in a prayer or a law. An advocate is one called to help, to speak for someone. A vocation is a calling, the job you feel meant to do. A "vocabulary" is a collection of words you can call on.
volv, volut | Latin for "roll" or "turn." | This Latin root, from volvere, means "to roll" or "to turn." To revolve is to roll around and back. To evolve is to roll out, to unfold gradually. A revolution is a turning around, a complete change. To involve is to roll into, to wrap up in something. A "volume" was originally a scroll, rolled up.
zoo | Greek for "animal." | Zoo comes from the Greek zoon, "animal." Zoology is the study of animals. A zoo is short for "zoological garden," where animals are kept. The zodiac is a circle of animals, the constellations in the sky that have animal names. Protozoa are the first animals, tiny single-celled creatures. It's a handy root for anything about living creatures.
anthrop | Greek for "human." | From the Greek anthropos, "human," this root is about people. Anthropology is the study of humans, their cultures and history. Philanthropy, from the Greek for "love of humankind," is giving to help others. A misanthrope is a hater of humankind. Anthropoid means humanlike, as in apes. It's a root that scientists use for the study of our species.
arch | Greek for "rule" or "first." | Arch comes from the Greek archos, "ruler," and arche, "first." A monarchy is rule by one. Anarchy is without rule, a lack of government. Archaic means from the first, very old. An architect is a chief builder. An "archbishop" is a chief bishop, and an "archenemy" is the chief enemy.
astro, aster | Greek for "star." | This Greek root means "star." Astronomy is the law, or study, of the stars. An asterisk is a little star, the symbol *. An astronaut is a star sailor, one who travels in space. A disaster was originally an "ill star," a bad omen, since people thought that a calamity came from an unfavorable position of the stars.
carn | Latin for "flesh." | Carn comes from the Latin caro, "flesh." A carnivore is a flesh eater, an animal that eats meat. Carnival is usually traced to words meaning "farewell to meat," the feast before Lent. To incarnate is to make flesh, to give a body to a spirit. Carnage is the slaughter of many people. "Carnation," the flower, may come from a related word for the flesh color.
cardi | Greek for "heart." | Cardi is from the Greek kardia, "heart." Cardiac means of the heart. Cardiology is the study of the heart. A cardiogram is a record of the heart's beats, an electrocardiogram. The pericardium is the sac around the heart. In English, "cordial" and "courage" are related words from the Latin cor, which also means heart.
dent | Latin for "tooth." | Dent is from the Latin dens, "tooth." A dentist is a doctor for the teeth. A trident is a three-toothed spear, like the one that Poseidon carries. To indent is to cut a notch, like a tooth, into the edge of something, as with a paragraph. Dental means relating to teeth. A "dandelion" is named from French for "lion's tooth," from its jagged leaves.
derm | Greek for "skin." | Derm comes from the Greek derma, "skin." Dermatology is the study of the skin. The epidermis is the upper skin, the outer layer. Hypodermic means under the skin, as with a needle. A pachyderm is a thick-skinned animal, like an elephant or rhinoceros. "Taxidermy" is arranging the skin, preserving animals.
gastr | Greek for "stomach." | This Greek root, from gaster, means "stomach." Gastric means of the stomach, as in gastric juice. Gastronomy is the art of eating well, literally, "stomach law." Gastritis is stomach inflammation. A gastropod is a stomach-foot, such as a snail, which seems to crawl on its stomach. It's a root for food and digestion.
hem, hema | Greek for "blood." | Hem comes from the Greek haima, "blood." Hemoglobin is the protein in blood that carries oxygen. A hemorrhage is a bursting of blood, heavy bleeding. Hematology is the study of blood. Anemia means without blood, a shortage of red blood cells. It also appears in "hemophilia," the disease in which blood clots poorly.
neuro | Greek for "nerve." | Neuro is from the Greek neuron, "nerve." A neuron is a nerve cell. Neurology is the study of the nervous system. A neurosis was once thought to be a nerve disorder. A neurotransmitter is a chemical that carries messages between nerve cells. The Greeks used the word for "sinew" and "cord," before it came to mean nerve.
opt, ops | Greek for "eye" or "sight." | This Greek root, from ops, means "eye" or "sight." Optic means of the eye or sight. An optician makes eyeglasses. Myopia is short sight, or nearsightedness. An optometrist is an eye measurer, who tests your sight. The word "optics" is the science of light and vision, and "synopsis" is a seeing together, a summary.
oste | Greek for "bone." | Oste is from the Greek osteon, "bone." Osteoporosis is a condition of porous, weakened bones. An osteopath is a bone healer, a kind of doctor. Osteology is the study of bones. The periosteum is the membrane that surrounds a bone. "Ostracize" is a different root, from the Greek for "shell," though it sounds alike.
somn | Latin for "sleep." | Somn comes from the Latin somnus, "sleep." Insomnia is no sleep, difficulty in sleeping. A somnambulist is a sleepwalker. Somnolent means sleepy, drowsy. Somniloquy is talking in your sleep. The Roman god of sleep was Somnus, and his Greek counterpart was Hypnos, who gave us "hypnosis."
vor | Latin for "eat." | Vor is Latin for "to eat" or "to devour," from vorare. A carnivore eats flesh. A herbivore eats plants. Voracious means eating greedily, with great appetite. To devour is to eat up, to consume quickly. A "locavore" is a recent coinage for someone who eats local food.
-phobia | Greek for "fear." | This Greek ending means "fear." Claustrophobia is the fear of closed spaces. Xenophobia is the fear of strangers or foreigners. Acrophobia is the fear of heights. Phobos was the Greek god of fear, and a phobia is an intense, irrational fear. There are hundreds of named phobias, and new ones are often invented for fun.
-cracy | Greek for "rule" or "power." | This Greek ending, from kratos, means "rule" or "power." Democracy is rule by the people. Aristocracy is rule by the best, the nobles. Bureaucracy is rule by desks, or offices, from the French word for a desk. A theocracy is rule by god, or by religious leaders. It's a handy ending that you can recognize in the names of governments.
-ectomy | Greek for "cutting out." | This Greek ending, from ektome, means "cutting out." An appendectomy is the surgical removal of the appendix. A tonsillectomy removes the tonsils. A mastectomy removes the breast, often because of cancer. A vasectomy cuts part of the tube that carries sperm. It always signals an operation to take something out, and so it's a useful clue in medical terms.

Bronchitis is inflammation of the airways, the bronchial tubes. Dermatitis is inflammation of the skin. It's become a joke ending as well, as in "senioritis," a student's lack of motivation near graduation.
-scope | Greek for "look at" or "see." | This Greek ending, from skopein, means "to look at" or "to see." A microscope is for seeing small things. A telescope is for seeing far. Many instruments for viewing end in -scope, from the kaleidoscope to the horoscope, which is literally "a look at the hour."
-ology | Greek for "the study of." | This Greek ending means "the study of," from logos, "word" or "reason." Biology is the study of life. Geology is the study of the earth. Psychology is the study of the mind, and anthropology of humankind. It's the most common ending for names of sciences, and it often appears joined to a Greek root that tells you the subject.
-less | English for "without." | The English ending -less means "without." To be careless is to be without care. A homeless person is without a home. Something endless goes on without end. To be fearless is to be without fear. Unlike many suffixes, this one is not from Latin or Greek. It comes from Old English, and is related to the word "loose," meaning free of something.
-ous | Latin for "full of" or "having." | This ending, from Latin, means "full of" or "having." Something famous is full of fame. Dangerous means full of danger. Joyous is full of joy. Glorious is full of glory. It turns nouns into adjectives, and it's a very common ending in English, often with a slight spelling change, as in "adventurous" or "ambitious."
-ify, -fy | Latin for "to make." | This Latin ending, from facere, means "to make." To purify is to make pure. To simplify is to make simple. To magnify is to make great, or larger. To clarify is to make clear. It's a helpful ending for turning an adjective or a noun into a verb, as in "beautify," "solidify" and "justify."
-ize | Greek for "to make" or "to become." | This Greek ending, from -izein, means "to make" or "to become." To modernize is to make modern. To realize is to make real, to understand. To criticize is to act as a critic. To apologize is to give an apology. In the US it's the standard ending, while some other countries spell it -ise.

It's from astrology: people used to believe that a calamity was caused by an unfavorable position of the stars and planets. Charles Boycott was a land agent in County Mayo, Ireland, in 1880, who refused tenants' demands for lower rents. In response, tenants, shopkeepers and even the postman refused to deal with him. The newspapers described it as "boycotting," and within a year the word was used across Europe and America.|1880|Oxford English Dictionary

In ancient Athens, citizens could vote to send away a person who was seen as a threat to the city, for ten years, by scratching his name on an ostrakon, a broken piece of pottery, used like a ballot. The Greeks believed that sudden, unexplained terror, especially when alone in the hills, was caused by Pan, who could cry out and frighten flocks. So the fear was called panikon, "of Pan," and it became our word for sudden fright.||Oxford English Dictionary

Daisy | It means "day's eye." | The word means "day's eye." It comes from the Old English dægesege, which is "day" plus "eye." In Renaissance Italy, money changers did business at benches in the marketplace, and, according to the traditional story, if a banker failed and couldn't pay what he owed, his bench was broken, publicly, so that everyone would know. Following a clew gradually became following a clue, a trail that leads you to the answer.||Oxford English Dictionary

The word later came to mean any rule about when you have to be home.||Oxford English Dictionary
