# Mental models, rules of thumb and decision tools.

First principles | Break a problem down to what is surely true, then build up from there instead of copying how it is usually done. | Aristotle described a first principle as "the first basis from which a thing is known": something you can't break down any further. The habit is to ask what you actually know to be true about a problem, set aside what everyone assumes, and build your answer up from there. Most of us reason by analogy, copying what others do with small tweaks. That's usually fine and efficient. When you're stuck, though, going back to the basics is how you find options that no one else was looking at.||Aristotle, Metaphysics
Inversion | Solve a problem backward: ask what would guarantee failure, then avoid that. | The mathematician Carl Jacobi used to say "invert, always invert," meaning that a hard problem can often be solved by turning it around. Instead of asking how to succeed, ask what would guarantee failure and then avoid it. The investor Charlie Munger loved this approach and liked to quote the line "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there." It's simple, and it works well for decisions where the ways to fail are easier to list than the ways to win.||Munger, Poor Charlie's Almanack (2005)
Second-order thinking | Ask "and then what?" about the consequences of the consequences. | The first effect of a choice is usually easy to see. The second effect, which is what happens after that, is the one people miss. In 1902, the French colonial government in Hanoi tried to fight rats by paying a bounty for each rat tail. Some people started breeding rats for the money, and others cut off tails and let the rats go. The rat problem grew. Before acting, ask yourself "and then what?" at least twice.||Vann, 2003
Occam's razor | When two explanations fit the facts equally well, test the simpler one first. | When two explanations account for the facts equally well, try the simpler one first. William of Ockham was a 14th-century English friar, though the popular wording about not multiplying "entities" came later. It's worth remembering what the razor is not. It isn't a law of nature, and the world isn't always simple. It's guidance about where to begin looking. The simple explanation is usually quicker to test, and if it fails, you can move on to a more complicated one.||Standard philosophy reference
Hanlon's razor | Do not blame malice for what carelessness or confusion explains. | The advice is not to blame malice for what can be explained by carelessness, distraction or confusion. A friend doesn't answer your message, and a colleague sends a curt email. It's very tempting to assume the worst, but far more often they're busy, tired or didn't realize how it would read. The saying appeared in print in 1980 in a book of Murphy's Law variations, credited to a Robert Hanlon. It tends to save relationships a lot of unnecessary grief.||Bloch, Murphy's Law Book Two (1980)
Chesterton's fence | Before you tear down a fence, find out why it was put up. | In 1929 the writer G. K. Chesterton gave a little parable. Imagine you come across a fence across a road, and you can't see any reason for it. A reformer says, "Let's clear it away." A wiser reformer says, "If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think. Then, when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it, I may allow you to destroy it." The point isn't that nothing should change, but that you should understand why something exists before you remove it.||Chesterton, The Thing (1929)
Opportunity cost | The true cost of something is the best alternative you gave up for it. | The true cost of something isn't only the price you pay, but the best alternative you gave up to get it. The French economist Frédéric Bastiat made the point in 1850 with an essay about "what is seen and what is not seen." We notice what a purchase gets us. We rarely notice what the same money or the same hour would have done somewhere else. Asking "instead of what?" is a small habit that changes a lot of decisions.||Bastiat, 1850
Margin of safety | Leave a buffer for what you cannot foresee. | When engineers design a bridge, they don't build it to carry exactly the load they expect. They build it to carry several times that, because the future always holds surprises. The investor Benjamin Graham borrowed the idea for his book The Intelligent Investor in 1949. He advised buying only when a price is well below what you think a thing is worth. It applies to ordinary life too: leave extra time for the trip, extra money in the account, and extra room in the schedule.||Graham, The Intelligent Investor (1949)
Circle of competence | Know the edge of what you truly understand and stay careful beyond it. | Everyone understands some things well and others hardly at all. Warren Buffett has said that what matters isn't how big your circle of competence is, but how well you know where it ends. Most expensive mistakes happen just outside the edge, where we know enough to feel confident and not enough to be careful. A useful habit is to say plainly, "That's outside what I know," and to find someone who knows it when the stakes are high.||Buffett, 1996 shareholder letter
The map is not the territory | A model of reality is a simplification, and it can mislead if mistaken for the real thing. | In 1931 the scholar Alfred Korzybski pointed out that a map isn't the land it describes. A subway map is very useful precisely because it leaves out almost everything: the real distances, the streets above, the hills. But try to find a particular building with it and you'll see how little it tells you. Every model, chart, theory and statistic is a map. It helps you think. It goes wrong when you forget that there's a real, messier world behind it.||Korzybski, 1931
The Pareto principle | A small share of causes often produces most of the results. | Around 1896 the economist Vilfredo Pareto noticed that roughly 80% of the land in Italy was owned by about 20% of the people. The 80/20 split isn't a law, and the real numbers vary. The lasting lesson is that outcomes are often unevenly spread. A handful of customers, bugs or tasks can account for most of the results, so it helps to find which ones.||Pareto, 1896; Juran
The Lindy effect | For ideas and books, the longer something has lasted, the longer it may keep lasting. | The idea, popularized by Nassim Taleb, is that for things that don't age like living creatures, such as books, ideas and technologies, the longer they've survived, the longer they're likely to last. A book that has stayed in print for a hundred years is a better bet to still be read in another hundred than this week's bestseller. Time acts as a filter. It's a rough rule, and it doesn't apply to people or to things that wear out.||Taleb, Antifragile (2012)|mixed
Fermi estimation | Get a rough answer by breaking a question into pieces you can guess. | The physicist Enrico Fermi was known for getting reasonable answers to questions that seem impossible. A classic example is: how many piano tuners work in Chicago? You guess the city's population, the share of households with a piano, how often a piano gets tuned, and how many tunings one person can do in a day. Each guess may be off, but the errors tend to partly cancel. The answer usually lands within a factor of two or three, which is often good enough to decide something.||Standard physics-teaching reference
Black swan | A rare event with huge impact that we explain as obvious only after it happens. | For centuries Europeans took it for granted that all swans were white, because every swan they'd ever seen was. Then in 1697 the Dutch explorer Willem de Vlamingh saw black swans in Western Australia. Nassim Taleb uses the phrase for rare, high-impact events that nobody expected and that we then explain as if they'd been obvious all along. It's a warning about how much weight to put on a record of calm. One surprise can overturn thousands of ordinary days.|1697|Taleb, The Black Swan (2007)
Antifragility | Some things gain from shocks: muscles, bones, immune systems. | Some things are fragile and break under stress. Others are robust and just hold up. Nassim Taleb's word "antifragile" describes things that get stronger when they're stressed, like muscles under a workout, bones under load, or immune systems exposed to germs. It's a helpful way of looking at systems, including our own habits. As a full scientific theory it's more a framework than something tested in detail, but the main idea, that some stress is useful, rings true in many places.||Taleb, Antifragile (2012)|mixed
Hormesis | A small dose of a stressor can help, while a large dose harms. | Many things are harmful in large amounts and beneficial in small ones, a pattern called hormesis. Exercise is an example: a moderate workout makes you stronger, while too much breaks you down. Some researchers suggest fasting and sun exposure follow the same curve. In toxicology, scientists still debate whether low doses of things like poisons or radiation can actually help. So the idea is well supported in some areas, and still argued over in others.||Calabrese & Baldwin, 2003|mixed
Unintended consequences | Actions have effects nobody planned or wanted. | In 1936 the sociologist Robert Merton published a short paper listing why actions so often have effects nobody planned. He pointed to ignorance of how things interact, mistakes in analysis, and what he called the "imperious immediacy of interest," meaning that people focus on the short term. Policies, products and personal decisions all show it. The takeaway isn't to avoid acting, but to spend a little time on the second and third effects, and to try things small before scaling them.||Merton, 1936
Parkinson's law | Work expands to fill the time available. | "Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion." C. Northcote Parkinson wrote that line in a humorous 1955 essay in The Economist. He had noticed that the British Admiralty kept adding administrators even as the navy shrank. It rings true on a smaller scale. A task you're given a week for tends to take a week, though it might have taken an afternoon. A shorter deadline sometimes gets the same job done with less fuss.||Parkinson, The Economist (1955)
Hofstadter's law | It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take Hofstadter's law into account. | Douglas Hofstadter wrote in his 1979 book Gödel, Escher, Bach: "It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law." He was describing how long it would take computers to play chess at a high level. The joke is a recursion, but it reflects a serious habit known as the planning fallacy. Our estimates are usually too hopeful. A good rule is to pad your first guess, then look at how long similar jobs have actually taken.||Hofstadter, Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979)
Brooks's law | Adding people to a late software project makes it later. | Fred Brooks managed the huge IBM System/360 software project, and in his 1975 book The Mythical Man-Month he wrote that adding people to a late software project makes it later. New people have to be taught, which takes time from the people who already know the work. And every extra person adds more lines of communication. As he put it, nine women can't make a baby in one month. Some work simply can't be divided up.||Brooks, The Mythical Man-Month (1975)
The Peter principle | In a hierarchy, people tend to be promoted until they reach a job they can't do well. | In 1969 Laurence Peter, an educator, wrote a funny book arguing that people in an organization get promoted for doing well until they reach a job they can't do well, and there they stay. In 2019 economists studied sales data on 53,000 workers at 214 firms and found a version of it. Companies tended to promote the best salespeople into management, where selling skills matter little. Good performance in one role doesn't guarantee it in the next.||Peter, 1969; Benson, Li & Shue, 2019
Diminishing returns | Each extra unit of effort or money adds a little less than the last. | The first hour of study usually teaches you a lot. The tenth hour of the same day teaches far less. Each extra unit of effort, money or time tends to add a bit less than the one before. The French economist Anne-Robert-Jacques Turgot described it for farming in 1767: extra labor on a given plot yields smaller and smaller extra crops. It's a good reason to stop polishing at some point and to spread your effort across more than one thing.||Turgot, 1767
The veil of ignorance | Choose the rules of a society as if you did not know who you would be in it. | The philosopher John Rawls proposed a thought experiment in his 1971 book A Theory of Justice. Imagine you're designing the rules of a society, but you don't know who you'll be in it: rich or poor, healthy or sick, talented or not, any race or gender. Rawls argued you'd then choose rules that are fair to everyone, since you could end up anywhere. It's a handy test for any rule. Would you still endorse it if you didn't know which side of it you'd be on?||Rawls, A Theory of Justice (1971)
The trolley problem | Would you pull a lever to divert a runaway trolley, killing one person to save five? | A runaway trolley is heading toward five people on a track. You can pull a lever to divert it to a side track where it will kill one person instead. Philippa Foot posed the puzzle in 1967, and in 1976 Judith Jarvis Thomson added a version where you'd have to push a large man off a footbridge to stop it. Most people say they'd pull the lever and won't push the man, though the numbers are the same. Philosophers still argue about why.||Foot, 1967; Thomson, 1976
The Ship of Theseus | If every plank of a ship is replaced, is it still the same ship? | The Greek writer Plutarch told of the ship in which the hero Theseus sailed home to Athens. The Athenians kept it for centuries, and as planks rotted they replaced them with new ones. Eventually every plank had been replaced. Was it still the same ship? It's a puzzle about what makes something the same thing over time. Your own body is a version of it, since most of your cells are replaced over the years, and you still feel like you.||Plutarch, Life of Theseus
Sagan's baloney detection kit | Rules for testing claims: get independent confirmation, consider alternatives, and welcome tests that could prove you wrong. | In his 1995 book The Demon-Haunted World, Carl Sagan gave readers a toolkit for spotting bad arguments. It includes seeking independent confirmation of the facts, considering more than one hypothesis, and asking whether a claim could in principle be proven false. One of his tips is to avoid getting too attached to a hypothesis just because it's yours. He thought the tools should be used on yourself as well as on others, since the first person you're most likely to fool is you.||Sagan, The Demon-Haunted World (1995)
The devil's advocate | Arguing the other side on purpose to test an idea. | The phrase comes from a real job. When the Catholic Church was considering someone for sainthood, it appointed an official called the advocatus diaboli, who had to argue against the case and look for flaws. The role was ended by Pope John Paul II in 1983. The idea is still useful. Before a big decision, ask someone to argue the opposite on purpose. If the plan survives a serious attack, you can trust it more, and if it doesn't, you've saved yourself a lot of trouble.|1983|Standard history of canonization
Red teaming | Assigning a team to attack your plan to find where it breaks. | A red team is a group whose job is to attack your plan, system or idea to see where it breaks. Militaries use them in war games, and companies use them to test their security by trying to break in. The reason it works is that the people who built something are blind to its flaws, because they know how it's supposed to work. It helps to hire a friendly skeptic. You can use a small version of it at home: ask someone to find the three biggest holes in your plan.||Standard security and military practice
The checklist | A short list of steps can prevent errors that experts still make. | Experts make mistakes, not because they don't know enough but because complicated jobs have too many steps to hold in your head. In 2006 a team at Johns Hopkins introduced a simple five-step checklist for inserting central lines in Michigan intensive care units, covering things like washing hands and cleaning the skin. Within three months, the median rate of line infections fell from 2.7 per 1,000 catheter-days to zero. A short list can beat expertise alone.|2.7 to 0|Pronovost et al., NEJM, 2006
The Eisenhower matrix | Sort tasks by urgent and important; do, schedule, delegate or drop. | President Eisenhower, in a 1954 speech, quoted a college president who had said, "The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent." From that, people built a simple grid for sorting tasks into urgent or not, and important or not. The most valuable box is the one for things that matter but have no deadline, like your health, your relationships and your long-term work. They're the easiest to put off, because nothing pushes you to do them.||Eisenhower, 1954 address, quoting J. Roscoe Miller
The decision journal | Write down what you decided and why, before you know how it turned out. | Before you know how something turned out, write down what you decided, what you expected, and why. It takes a few minutes. Later, when the result is in, you can separate your reasoning from your luck. You also can't tell yourself that you knew it all along, because the page says otherwise. It works directly against hindsight bias. Over months it shows you the kinds of decisions you tend to get right and the ones where you're too sure.||Duke, Thinking in Bets (2018)
Resulting | Judging a decision by its outcome rather than by how well it was made. | The poker player Annie Duke uses the word "resulting" for judging a decision purely by how it turned out. A sensible bet can lose, and a reckless one can win. If you only look at results, you'll learn the wrong lessons: you'll repeat a lucky blunder and abandon a good plan after one bad break. A better question is "given what I knew then, was it a good decision?" That's harder to answer, but it's the one that actually helps you improve.||Duke, Thinking in Bets (2018)
Satisficing | Take the first option that is good enough instead of searching for the best. | In 1956 the economist and psychologist Herbert Simon pointed out that people rarely find the best option. They find one that is good enough and stop. He called that satisficing, a blend of "satisfy" and "suffice." Later, Barry Schwartz and colleagues found that people who try to find the best, whom they called maximizers, often feel less satisfied with their choices and more regret. Not every study agrees, but "good enough" is often a wise place to stop.||Simon, 1956; Schwartz et al., 2002|mixed
The 10-10-10 rule | Ask how you will feel about a choice in 10 minutes, in 10 months and in 10 years. | When you're stuck on a decision, imagine how you'll feel about it in ten minutes, ten months and ten years. The writer Suzy Welch popularized the method in 2009. It pulls your attention out of the heat of the moment. The urge to send an angry message may feel overwhelming in ten minutes, and in ten months it may seem trivial. The choice between two job offers might look different over ten years. It's a quick way to check your priorities.||Welch, 10-10-10 (2009)
Reversible versus irreversible choices | Move fast on decisions you can undo, slowly on the ones you cannot. | In a letter to shareholders, Jeff Bezos described two kinds of decisions. Some are one-way doors, which are hard or impossible to undo. Others are two-way doors: if you walk through and don't like it, you can go back. Most decisions are the second kind, and they can be made quickly, with less than complete information. Save your slow, careful thinking for the one-way doors, and try not to treat every choice as though it were permanent.||Bezos, 2015 shareholder letter
Noise in judgment | Different experts, or the same one on different days, give different answers to the same case. | We usually think about bias, which is when judgments are off in the same direction. Noise is something different: scatter, when different people, or the same person on different days, make different calls on identical cases. In a study at an insurance company, underwriters were asked to price the same case. Their quotes differed by 55% on average, and the executives had expected around 10%. The cost of that scatter is large, and it's hard to see, since each person only sees their own judgment.|55%|Kahneman, Sibony & Sunstein, Noise (2021)
The wisdom of crowds | The average of many independent guesses is often better than most individual guesses. | In 1907 the statistician Francis Galton visited a fair where people guessed the weight of an ox. He collected the 787 entries and found that the middle guess, 1,207 pounds, was within a percent of the true weight of 1,198. The crowd was better than almost any individual. It works under certain conditions: people have to guess independently and bring different information. If they copy each other, or a loud voice sways the room, the effect disappears.|1,207 vs 1,198|Galton, Nature, 1907; Surowiecki, 2004
The outside view | Estimate by looking at how similar cases turned out, not just at the details of yours. | When we plan a project, we naturally focus on its details and how things will go this time. Daniel Kahneman and Dan Lovallo called that the inside view. The outside view asks a different question: how did similar projects turn out? Bent Flyvbjerg's research on large infrastructure projects found that most go over budget and take longer than planned. So before you trust your own timeline, look up how long comparable jobs have really taken.||Kahneman & Lovallo, 1993; Flyvbjerg, 2003
Superforecasting | Some people predict events well because of how they think, not because they know more. | The political scientist Philip Tetlock ran forecasting tournaments in which thousands of ordinary people predicted world events. A small group did consistently better than the rest, and better than many experts. They weren't geniuses. They broke big questions into smaller ones, thought in probabilities rather than yes or no, and updated their views as evidence came in. The skill seems to come from habits of thinking, and it can be learned.||Tetlock & Gardner, Superforecasting (2015)
The Brier score | A way to measure how good probability forecasts are. | How do you grade a weather forecaster who says "70% chance of rain"? If it rains, were they right? If it doesn't, were they wrong? The meteorologist Glenn Brier devised a score in 1950 that fixes this. Over many forecasts, a person who says 70% should be right about seven times in ten. The score rewards being well calibrated, and it punishes being very sure and wrong. It's a standard tool for judging forecasts of all kinds.||Brier, 1950
Sturgeon's law | Ninety percent of everything is rubbish. | The science fiction writer Theodore Sturgeon is said to have been told, in the 1950s, that ninety percent of science fiction is crud. His reply was that ninety percent of everything is crud. It's a joke, and also a useful reminder. When you judge a whole field by its average, you'll be disappointed. Judge it by its best, and you'll find what draws people to it. The same goes for books, movies, music and even your own ideas.||Sturgeon, 1950s
The law of triviality | People give the most attention to the issues they understand best, which are often the least important. | C. Northcote Parkinson imagined a committee with a short agenda. It approves a huge nuclear reactor in a couple of minutes, since few members understand it well enough to object. Then it spends an hour arguing about the bicycle shed, because everyone has an opinion on that. People devote most attention to the matters they feel competent about, which are often the least important. In a meeting, it's worth asking whether the time spent matches the stakes.||Parkinson, 1957
The principle of charity | Interpret others in the most sensible way their words allow before you reply. | When you're about to disagree with someone, first read their words in the most sensible way they allow. Philosophers use the principle so that they argue against the strongest version of a view, not a weak one. Neil Wilson named it in 1959, and Donald Davidson developed it. It also saves a lot of needless fights in ordinary life. Assume the person is reasonable until the evidence shows otherwise, and you'll often find they meant something different from what you first heard.||Wilson, 1959; Davidson
The Overton window | The range of ideas the public will accept at a given time. | Joseph Overton was a policy analyst at a think tank in Michigan. He observed that at any time there is a range of ideas the public will accept as reasonable, and that politicians generally stay inside it. Ideas outside the window seem too extreme. The window shifts over time, and activists often work by pushing at its edges so what was unthinkable becomes debated. It's a helpful way of seeing why some policies are discussed and others aren't.||Mackinac Center, 1990s
The law of the instrument | If all you have is a hammer, everything looks like a nail. | If the only tool you have is a hammer, it's tempting to treat everything as if it were a nail. Abraham Kaplan wrote in 1964 that "give a small boy a hammer, and he will find that everything he encounters needs pounding." Experts do the same: the person trained in one method tends to see problems that method can solve. It's worth asking, when you're stuck, what other tools exist, and whether you picked this one because it fits or only because it's the one in your hand.||Kaplan, 1964; Maslow, 1966
The streetlight effect | Looking for something where it is easy to look, not where it probably is. | A man is on his hands and knees under a streetlight looking for his keys. A passerby asks where he lost them. "Over there in the park," he says, "but the light is better here." We do something like it all the time. Researchers measure what's easy to measure, managers track what's easy to count, and we look for answers where it's convenient. If you notice that you're searching where it's easy, ask whether the thing you want is probably somewhere else.||Standard reference
Cargo cult science | Copying the forms of science without its honesty. | In a 1974 commencement speech at Caltech, Richard Feynman described islanders in the South Pacific who, after the war, built runways and wooden control towers and waited for cargo planes, as the military had done. They copied the form of what they'd seen and none of the substance, so no planes came. He said some research is like that. It looks like science but lacks a kind of utter honesty, including reporting anything that might show you're wrong.||Feynman, Caltech address, 1974
You are the easiest person to fool | The first rule of science is not to fool yourself. | In the same 1974 Caltech speech, Richard Feynman said: "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool." It's the heart of how he thought science should work. You have to be harder on your own ideas than you'd be on anyone else's, because you want them to be true. In practice, that means looking for how you might be wrong first, and being glad when you find out.||Feynman, Caltech address, 1974
