The speed of light | Exactly 299,792,458 meters per second, in empty space. | Light in empty space travels at exactly 299,792,458 meters per second. It's exact because we define it that way. Since 1983 the meter has been defined as the distance light covers in 1/299,792,458 of a second, so the speed fixes the length. It's also the same for every observer, however fast they're moving, which is the strange fact that Einstein built relativity on. Nothing that has mass can ever reach it.|299,792,458 m/s|BIPM definition of the meter
Sunlight is old news | Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach us. | Light takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to get from the Sun to Earth. So when you look at the Sun, which you shouldn't do directly, you're seeing it as it was a little over eight minutes ago. If the Sun disappeared, we wouldn't know for those eight minutes. The next-nearest star, Proxima Centauri, is just over four light-years away, so its light shows you the star as it was more than four years ago.|8 min 20 s|NASA
Newton's three laws | Things keep their motion, force makes them accelerate, and forces come in pairs. | In 1687 Isaac Newton published three rules of motion in his Principia. An object keeps doing what it's doing unless a force acts on it. A force makes an object accelerate, more for a lighter object. And forces come in pairs: when you push on something, it pushes back equally. A rocket works through the third law. It throws gas backward, and the gas pushes the rocket forward. It doesn't need air to push against, which is why rockets work in space.||Newton, Principia (1687)
Feather and hammer | In a vacuum, a feather and a hammer fall at the same rate. | Galileo is said to have shown that heavy and light objects fall together. In 1971, astronaut David Scott tested it on the Moon, where there's no air. He held a geologist's hammer and a falcon feather at shoulder height, let go, and both hit the lunar dust at the same moment, on live television. On Earth, air resistance holds the feather back. Without it, gravity treats everything the same.|1971|Apollo 15, NASA
Conservation of energy | Energy is never created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another. | Energy can't be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another. A pendulum swaps motion for height and back. A battery turns chemical energy into electricity and then into light or motion. Several scientists reached the idea in the 1840s, and Hermann von Helmholtz set out the law in its general form in 1847. It's one of the most reliable rules in physics, and no machine that gets energy from nothing has ever worked.||Helmholtz, 1847
Absolute zero | The lowest possible temperature is about -273.15 degrees Celsius, or 0 kelvin. | The lowest temperature there could be is about minus 273.15 degrees Celsius, or zero on the Kelvin scale. At that point, the particles in a substance would have as little motion as physics allows. Scientists have cooled atoms to within a billionth of a degree above it, but the laws of thermodynamics say you can't reach it exactly. The coldest natural place we know of, the Boomerang Nebula, is about one degree above.|-273.15 °C|Kelvin scale; NIST
Ohm's law | Current equals voltage divided by resistance. | In 1827 the German schoolteacher Georg Ohm published what became the first rule of electrical circuits: the current through a conductor equals the voltage across it divided by its resistance. Double the voltage across a fixed resistor, and the current doubles. Double the resistance, and the current halves. It was dismissed at first, and Ohm struggled for years. Now every electrician uses it, and the unit of resistance carries his name.||Ohm, 1827
Lightning is hotter than the Sun's surface | A lightning bolt heats the air to about 30,000 kelvin. | A bolt of lightning heats the air around it to about 30,000 kelvin, roughly five times hotter than the visible surface of the Sun, which is about 5,800 kelvin. The heating is so sudden that the air expands faster than the speed of sound, producing a shock wave. That's the thunder you hear. You can estimate how far away a strike was by counting the seconds between the flash and the sound, about five seconds per mile.|30,000 K|NOAA
Earth's magnetic field | The field shields us from charged particles and has flipped many times. | Earth's core acts like a giant magnet, and its field deflects charged particles from the Sun, which helps protect life. The field isn't fixed, though. Rocks record that its direction has reversed many times. The last full flip was about 780,000 years ago, and after one, a compass needle would point south. A reversal takes thousands of years, and nothing suggests one would be a catastrophe, but nobody knows when the next will be.||Standard geophysics reference
The speed of sound | In air at 20 degrees Celsius, sound travels about 343 meters per second. | In air at 20 degrees Celsius, sound travels at about 343 meters per second, roughly a kilometer every three seconds. It moves faster through liquids and solids: about 1,480 meters per second in water, and over 5,000 in steel. The reason is that denser, stiffer materials pass vibrations along more quickly. It's why you can hear an approaching train by putting an ear to the rail before you hear it in the air.|343 m/s|Standard physics reference
The Doppler effect | A moving source's pitch rises as it approaches and falls as it leaves. | When a source of sound moves toward you, the waves are squeezed together and the pitch sounds higher. When it moves away, the waves are stretched and the pitch drops. You hear it when an ambulance passes. Christian Doppler proposed the idea in 1842, and in 1845 the Dutch scientist Buys Ballot tested it with trumpeters playing a steady note on a moving train. Astronomers use the same effect with light to measure how fast distant galaxies are moving.||Doppler, 1842; Buys Ballot, 1845
Why the Tacoma Narrows Bridge fell | The 1940 collapse was caused by wind making the deck twist, not simple resonance. | In November 1940 a new suspension bridge in Washington state twisted wildly in a moderate wind and collapsed. Many physics textbooks used to call it a simple case of resonance. Later analysis showed it was something more subtle, called aerodynamic flutter, in which the twisting motion of the deck changes how the wind pushes on it, which feeds more energy into the motion. Engineers have designed bridges to avoid it since.|1940|Billah & Scanlan, 1991
Newton's prism | White light is a mix of colors, and a prism splits it. | Before Newton, many people thought a prism added colors to white light. In 1666 he showed otherwise. He sent a beam of sunlight through one prism and it spread into a rainbow. Then he sent the colors through a second prism turned the other way, and they came back together as white. So white light is a mixture of all the colors, and the prism only separates them. A rainbow does the same thing using raindrops.|1666|Newton, Opticks (1704)
The double-slit experiment | Light and matter behave like waves and particles. | In 1801 Thomas Young shone light through two narrow slits and saw a pattern of bright and dark stripes on a screen behind them, which is what waves do when they overlap. A century later, scientists found that light also comes in particles. Then in 1927 Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer found that electrons behave like waves too. Later experiments sent single electrons, one at a time, through the slits, and the stripes still built up.||Young, 1801; Davisson & Germer, 1927
The uncertainty principle | You cannot know both a particle's exact position and momentum at once. | In 1927 Werner Heisenberg showed that you can't know both the exact position and the exact momentum of a particle at the same time. The more precisely you pin down one, the fuzzier the other becomes. It's often described as the effect of measuring disturbing the thing measured. But the deeper point is that it's built into nature: a particle simply doesn't have a perfectly sharp position and a perfectly sharp velocity together.||Heisenberg, 1927
Superposition | Until measured, a quantum system is described by a combination of possibilities. | In quantum mechanics, a particle can be in a combination of states at once, until it's measured. Erwin Schrödinger found this so strange that in 1935 he made up a thought experiment in which a cat in a box is linked to a quantum event, so that it's both alive and dead until someone opens the box. He meant it as a criticism, to show how odd the idea gets when scaled up. Physicists still debate what a measurement actually does.||Schrödinger, 1935
Quantum entanglement | Two particles can share a state so that measuring one tells you about the other. | Two particles can be linked so that measuring one instantly tells you something about the other, however far apart they are. Einstein didn't like it and called it "spooky action at a distance." But experiments, from Alain Aspect's in the early 1980s to tests with no loopholes in 2015, have confirmed that it's real. Aspect, John Clauser and Anton Zeilinger shared the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work. It doesn't let you send messages faster than light.|2022|Nobel Prize in Physics, 2022
Quantum tunneling | Particles can pass through barriers they classically could not. | In everyday life, a ball can't roll through a hill that's higher than its energy. In the quantum world, particles sometimes can. This is called tunneling, and it's how the Sun shines: protons in its core don't have quite enough energy to overcome their mutual repulsion and fuse, but a few tunnel through, enough to keep it burning. The effect is also used in scanning tunneling microscopes, and in the flash memory in phones.||Gamow, 1928
The photoelectric effect | Light knocks electrons from metal only if its frequency is high enough, no matter how bright. | When light hits a metal surface, it can knock electrons loose, but only if its frequency is high enough. A dim blue light does it, while a very bright red light doesn't. Physicists couldn't explain that with waves. In 1905 Einstein proposed that light comes in packets of energy, later called photons, and each must carry enough energy to free an electron. This, and not relativity, won him the 1921 Nobel Prize.|1905|Einstein, 1905
Atoms are mostly empty | The nucleus is about 1/100,000 the size of the atom. | In 1911 Ernest Rutherford's team fired particles at thin gold foil and found that most went straight through, but a few bounced right back. He concluded that an atom has a tiny, dense nucleus with mostly empty space around it. The nucleus is about 100,000 times smaller than the atom. If the atom were a stadium, the nucleus would be a pea at the center. Things feel solid because of the electric forces between atoms.|1/100,000|Rutherford, 1911
General relativity | Gravity is the bending of space and time by mass and energy. | In 1915 Einstein published a new theory of gravity. Instead of a force, he described it as the bending of space and time by mass and energy. Matter tells space how to curve, and curved space tells matter how to move. In 1919, during a solar eclipse, Arthur Eddington's expeditions measured starlight bending as it passed the Sun, by an amount consistent with Einstein's prediction. It made him world-famous overnight.||Einstein, 1915; Dyson, Eddington & Davidson, 1920
Gravitational waves | Ripples in spacetime, first detected in 2015. | Einstein predicted in 1916 that moving masses would send out ripples in spacetime. They're incredibly faint. On September 14, 2015, the LIGO detectors in Louisiana and Washington picked up a signal from two black holes that had merged 1.3 billion light-years away. The stretching of space they measured was thousands of times smaller than the width of a proton. Rainer Weiss, Kip Thorne and Barry Barish won the 2017 Nobel Prize for it.|2015|LIGO, Abbott et al., 2016
Black holes | Regions where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, escapes. | A black hole is a region where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape from inside its boundary, called the event horizon. They form when very massive stars collapse. The first image of one, in the galaxy M87, was released in 2019 by the Event Horizon Telescope. In 2022 the same team showed the black hole at the center of our own galaxy, which is about four million times the mass of the Sun.||Event Horizon Telescope, 2019, 2022
Hawking radiation | Black holes slowly lose mass by emitting faint radiation. | In 1974 Stephen Hawking showed that, thanks to quantum effects near the event horizon, black holes should slowly lose mass by emitting a faint glow of radiation. It means they aren't completely black, and that they can eventually evaporate. For a black hole of the size we know, the process would take far, far longer than the current age of the universe. No one has ever detected the radiation, since it's far too weak.||Hawking, 1974
Time runs slower near mass | Clocks tick slower where gravity is stronger. | General relativity predicts that time passes more slowly in stronger gravity. A clock at sea level ticks slightly slower than one on a mountain top. In 2010 researchers at NIST measured it with extremely precise atomic clocks that were only about 33 centimeters, a foot, apart in height. The difference was tiny but real. It also matters in practice: GPS satellites need to correct for it, or they'd drift by kilometers each day.||Chou et al., 2010
The Big Bang | The universe has been expanding from an extremely hot, dense state for about 13.8 billion years. | The evidence says the universe began as an incredibly hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago, and has been expanding and cooling since. The Belgian priest and physicist Georges Lemaître first proposed it in 1927. Three main things support it: galaxies are moving apart, the whole sky glows with leftover radiation, and the proportions of hydrogen and helium match the predictions. It describes what happened after the beginning, not what caused it.|13.8 billion yrs|Planck Collaboration, 2018
The cosmic microwave background | Faint radiation from the early universe fills all of space. | In 1964 two radio astronomers at Bell Labs, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, were bothered by a faint hiss in their big horn antenna. They even cleaned out pigeon droppings, thinking they might be the cause. The hiss turned out to be the afterglow of the Big Bang, light released when the universe was about 380,000 years old and had cooled enough to become transparent. They won the 1978 Nobel Prize.|1964|Penzias & Wilson, 1965
The expanding universe | Distant galaxies move away from us, and the farther they are, the faster. | In 1929 Edwin Hubble showed that galaxies are moving away from us, and that the farther away they are, the faster they're receding. This built on the 1927 work of Georges Lemaître. It doesn't mean we're at the center. Space itself is stretching, so every galaxy sees the others moving away, like dots on the surface of an inflating balloon. It was the first key observation behind the Big Bang model.||Hubble, 1929
Dark matter | Unseen matter holds galaxies together with its gravity. | In the 1970s the astronomer Vera Rubin measured how fast stars orbit at the edges of galaxies. They moved as fast as the stars near the center, which didn't match the visible matter. The best explanation is that there's an unseen halo of matter whose gravity holds everything together. It makes up about 27 percent of the universe. We don't know what it's made of, and experiments around the world are trying to find out.|About 27%|Rubin & Ford, 1970; Planck, 2018
Dark energy | Something is making the universe's expansion speed up. | In 1998 two teams studying distant exploding stars expected to find that the expansion of the universe was slowing down because of gravity. Instead, they found it was speeding up. Something is pushing space apart, and it's been named dark energy. It makes up about 68 percent of the universe, and nobody knows what it is. The leaders of the two teams shared the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics.|About 68%|Riess et al., 1998; Perlmutter et al., 1999
Ordinary matter is rare | Everything you can see, atoms and all, is about 5% of the universe. | Everything you can see, from the Earth to the stars to you, is made of ordinary atoms, and all of that is only about 5 percent of the universe. The other 95 percent is dark matter, about 27 percent, and dark energy, about 68 percent. The measurements come mainly from satellites studying the cosmic microwave background. We've learned that most of the universe is something we don't yet understand.|About 5%|Planck Collaboration, 2018
The observable universe | The part we can see is about 93 billion light-years across. | The part of the universe we can see is about 93 billion light-years across. That may sound odd, since the universe is only 13.8 billion years old. But space has been stretching all the while light was traveling to us, so the distance to the places that sent us the oldest light is much larger now. There may well be more beyond the edge, but its light hasn't had time to reach us.|93 billion ly|Standard cosmology reference
The Milky Way | Our galaxy has 100 to 400 billion stars and spans about 100,000 light-years. | Our galaxy is a spiral of somewhere between 100 and 400 billion stars, about 100,000 light-years across. We live in one of its arms, about halfway out from the center. The Sun takes about 230 million years to go once around. In roughly four to five billion years the Milky Way will merge with its nearest large neighbor, the Andromeda galaxy, which is heading our way.||NASA; van der Marel et al., 2019
The Sun | A middle-aged star, about 4.6 billion years old. | The Sun is a middle-aged star, about 4.6 billion years old. Every second, in its core, it turns about 600 million tons of hydrogen into helium, and the small amount of mass that vanishes in the process becomes the energy that warms us. It has enough fuel for roughly another five billion years. After that it will swell into a red giant, and then shrink into a white dwarf.|4.6 billion yrs|NASA
Stars have life cycles | A star's mass decides how it lives and dies. | A star's life is decided mainly by its mass. Small ones, called red dwarfs, burn their fuel slowly and can last trillions of years. Massive stars burn fast and bright, lasting only millions of years, and end in a supernova explosion that leaves behind a neutron star or a black hole. Our Sun, in the middle, will end more quietly as a white dwarf, a small, dense, cooling ember.||Standard astronomy text
We are stardust | The carbon, oxygen and iron in your body were made in stars. | Just after the Big Bang, the universe was almost entirely hydrogen and helium. Every heavier element, including the carbon in your cells, the oxygen you breathe and the iron in your blood, was made later inside stars, and was scattered into space when stars died. Some heavy elements also come from the collisions of neutron stars. In a real sense, you're made of material that was cooked in stars.||Burbidge, Burbidge, Fowler & Hoyle, 1957
Neutron stars | The collapsed cores of massive stars, packing the Sun's mass into a city-size ball. | When a massive star explodes as a supernova, its core can collapse into a neutron star, a ball only about 20 kilometers across but with more mass than the Sun. A teaspoon of its material would weigh about a billion tons. Some neutron stars spin hundreds of times a second. They're among the densest things in the universe, short of black holes.|~1 billion tons|NASA
Pulsars | Jocelyn Bell Burnell found a signal in 1967 that pulsed every 1.34 seconds. | In 1967 a graduate student at Cambridge, Jocelyn Bell, noticed a strange signal in her radio telescope data: a pulse repeating every 1.337 seconds, as regular as a clock. The team half-jokingly named it LGM-1, for "little green men." It turned out to be a rapidly spinning neutron star, sweeping a beam of radio waves past Earth like a lighthouse. Her supervisor, Antony Hewish, shared the 1974 Nobel Prize for the discovery. She was not included.|1967|Hewish et al., 1968
Supernova 1987A | The closest supernova in modern times, seen in 1987. | On February 23, 1987, astronomers saw a star explode in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy next to ours, about 168,000 light-years away. It was the closest supernova in about four centuries. A few hours before the light arrived, detectors deep underground in Japan and the US caught a burst of neutrinos, ghostly particles produced in the explosion, confirming how such stars die.|1987|NASA
Gold from neutron star mergers | In 2017 astronomers saw two neutron stars collide and make heavy elements. | In August 2017, detectors picked up gravitational waves from two neutron stars spiraling into each other 130 million light-years away. Telescopes around the world watched the glow that followed. It showed signs of heavy elements being created, including gold and platinum. It helped answer a long-standing question about where the heaviest elements come from.||Abbott et al., 2017
Mercury | The smallest planet and the closest to the Sun, with a year of 88 days. | The closest planet to the Sun is also the smallest, a little bigger than our Moon. A year there lasts just 88 days. With almost no atmosphere to hold in heat, its temperature swings wildly, from about 430 degrees Celsius in the day to about minus 180 at night. Even so, scientists have found water ice in permanently shadowed craters near its poles.|88 days|NASA
Venus | A planet with a day longer than its year. | Venus is a strange world: it spins backward and so slowly that a day, 243 Earth days, is longer than its year of 225 days. Its thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide traps heat in a runaway greenhouse effect, making it the hottest planet at about 465 degrees Celsius, hotter than Mercury, even though it's farther from the Sun. The pressure at the surface is about 90 times Earth's.|465 °C|NASA
Earth in motion | You are moving about 30 kilometers per second around the Sun. | You're sitting still, but you're traveling. Earth orbits the Sun at about 30 kilometers per second, nearly 70,000 miles per hour. At the equator, the planet's spin carries you eastward at about 1,670 kilometers per hour. You don't feel any of it, because the speed is smooth and steady and everything around you is moving along with you. We notice only changes in motion.|30 km/s|NASA
Mars | The red planet owes its color to iron oxide, or rust. | Mars looks red because its surface is rich in iron oxide, which is rust. It has the biggest volcano known in the solar system, Olympus Mons, about 22 kilometers high, roughly two and a half times the height of Mount Everest. Billions of years ago, it had rivers and lakes of liquid water, and rovers have found signs of old streambeds. Today it's cold and dry, with a thin atmosphere.|22 km|NASA
Jupiter | A giant that could hold more than 1,300 Earths. | Jupiter is by far the largest planet, with enough volume to hold more than 1,300 Earths. It's made mostly of hydrogen and helium, with no solid surface to land on. Its Great Red Spot is a giant storm, wider than Earth, that has been observed for at least 150 years. Its strong gravity has probably shaped the solar system, and has pulled in comets and asteroids.|1,300 Earths|NASA
Saturn | A planet less dense than water. | Saturn is the least dense planet in the solar system, with an average density lower than water. In theory, it would float if you could find a bathtub big enough. Its famous rings are made mostly of water ice, in pieces from dust to house-size boulders, and they spread out over hundreds of thousands of kilometers, yet in places they're only about ten meters thick.||NASA
Uranus | A planet that spins on its side, tilted about 98 degrees. | Uranus is tilted about 98 degrees, so it spins almost on its side, like a rolling ball. As a result, each pole gets 42 years of constant sunlight, followed by 42 years of darkness. William Herschel found it in 1781, the first planet discovered with a telescope. A giant collision early in the planet's history may have knocked it over, though no one is sure.|98°|NASA
Neptune | Found in 1846 by mathematics before anyone saw it. | Neptune was found with mathematics before anyone saw it. After Uranus was discovered, astronomers noticed its orbit wasn't quite as predicted, and in 1846 Urbain Le Verrier in France, and John Couch Adams in England, each calculated where an unseen planet must be. Johann Galle in Berlin pointed his telescope at the spot Le Verrier gave and found it that very night. Neptune has the fastest winds in the solar system, over 2,000 kilometers per hour.|1846|Le Verrier, 1846
Pluto | Reclassified in 2006 as a dwarf planet. | Pluto was called the ninth planet from its discovery in 1930 until 2006, when the International Astronomical Union voted to define a planet as something that has cleared the neighborhood around its orbit. Pluto hasn't, so it became a dwarf planet. In July 2015 NASA's New Horizons spacecraft flew by it and showed a surprisingly active world, including a large heart-shaped plain of frozen nitrogen.|2006|IAU, 2006; NASA New Horizons
The Moon's origin | The Moon was probably made when a Mars-size body hit the young Earth. | The leading explanation, developed in the 1970s, is that about 4.5 billion years ago a planet-sized body, which has been called Theia, struck the young Earth. The debris from the collision orbited the planet and gradually came together as the Moon. Moon rocks brought back by Apollo astronauts have a chemistry very close to Earth's outer layers, which fits the idea. It's called the giant-impact hypothesis.||Hartmann & Davis, 1975
Tides | Two bulges of ocean follow the Moon around Earth. | The Moon's gravity pulls more strongly on the side of Earth nearest it and less on the far side. That stretches the oceans into two bulges, one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side, and as Earth turns, coastlines pass through both, giving most places two high tides a day. The Sun adds its own pull. When Sun and Moon line up, at new and full moon, the tides are higher, called spring tides.||NOAA
The Moon is drifting away | It moves about 3.8 centimeters farther from Earth each year. | The Moon is moving away from Earth at about 3.8 centimeters a year, roughly the speed at which your fingernails grow. Scientists know this because Apollo astronauts left mirrors on the surface, and lasers fired from Earth bounce off them, letting us measure the distance to within a few millimeters. As it recedes, Earth's rotation slows slightly, so our days get gradually longer.|3.8 cm a year|Apollo Lunar Laser Ranging
Why we only see one side of the Moon | The Moon turns exactly once for every orbit. | The Moon spins exactly once for every trip it makes around Earth, so the same face always points toward us. It's called tidal locking, and it happened because Earth's gravity slowly slowed the Moon's spin until the two matched. It's not a "dark side," though. The far side gets as much sunlight as the near side. It was first photographed by a Soviet spacecraft in 1959.||NASA
Total solar eclipses | The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times farther away. | By a remarkable coincidence, the Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times farther away, so they look nearly the same size in our sky. When the Moon passes exactly in front of the Sun, it can cover the disk completely and reveal the Sun's faint outer atmosphere, the corona. Because the Moon is slowly drifting away, in about 600 million years it will be too small to cover the Sun fully.|400×|NASA
Io | The most volcanically active world in the solar system. | Jupiter's moon Io is the most volcanically active place in the solar system, with hundreds of volcanoes. The reason is tidal heating: Jupiter and its other big moons tug on Io, squeezing and flexing it constantly, which heats the interior. Its volcanoes spray sulfurous material hundreds of kilometers into space. Voyager 1 spotted the first plumes in 1979, and an image analyst, Linda Morabito, noticed them.||Morabito et al., 1979
Europa | A moon of Jupiter with an ocean of salty water under its icy shell. | Europa, a moon of Jupiter, has a surface of ice, and under it is an ocean of salty liquid water, kept warm by tidal flexing. It may contain twice as much water as all of Earth's oceans. Because liquid water is one ingredient of life as we know it, scientists are very interested. NASA launched the Europa Clipper spacecraft in 2024 to fly past it dozens of times and study whether it could be habitable.||NASA
Titan | Saturn's moon has a thick atmosphere and lakes of liquid methane. | Titan, Saturn's largest moon, is the only moon with a thick atmosphere, and the only other place in the solar system where we've seen stable liquid on the surface. But it's not water: it's lakes and rivers of liquid methane and ethane, at about minus 180 degrees Celsius. In 2005 the Huygens probe landed there, and sent back pictures of a landscape that looks eerily like a shoreline.|2005|ESA/NASA, Cassini-Huygens
Enceladus | A small moon of Saturn that sprays water into space. | Enceladus is a small, icy moon of Saturn, only about 500 kilometers across. In 2005 the Cassini spacecraft discovered jets of water vapor and ice particles shooting from cracks near its south pole. Later flybys found salt, organic molecules and hydrogen in the spray, which suggests there's a liquid ocean beneath the ice and active chemistry. It's among the most promising places to look for life beyond Earth.|2005|NASA Cassini
The asteroid belt | Between Mars and Jupiter orbit millions of rocky bodies, but they are far apart. | In movies, the belt between Mars and Jupiter is crowded with rocks, and ships weave between them. In reality it's mostly empty space. There are millions of asteroids, but they're spread so far apart that spacecraft have passed through without any close calls. The total mass of the belt is less than that of our Moon, and about a third of it is in the largest body, the dwarf planet Ceres.||NASA
The dinosaur-killing impact | An asteroid about 10 kilometers wide struck Mexico 66 million years ago. | About 66 million years ago, an asteroid roughly 10 kilometers wide struck what is now the Yucatán Peninsula in Mexico. In 1980 the father-and-son team of Luis and Walter Alvarez noticed a thin layer of clay around the world rich in iridium, a metal common in asteroids, and suggested that it came from an impact. In the 1990s, the buried Chicxulub crater was identified as the site. About three-quarters of species died.|66M years ago|Alvarez et al., 1980
Halley's comet | It returns about every 76 years; last seen in 1986, next due in 2061. | Halley's comet comes back about every 76 years. It was last seen in 1986, and it's due again in 2061. In 1705 Edmond Halley noticed that comets seen in 1531, 1607 and 1682 had similar orbits, and he used Newton's new laws to predict it would return in 1758. It did, after his death, and it was the first comet shown to travel in a regular orbit, a major triumph for Newtonian physics.|76 years|Halley, 1705
Voyager 1 | The most distant human-made object, launched in 1977. | Launched in 1977 to study the outer planets, Voyager 1 is now the most distant human-made object, and it entered interstellar space in 2012. It's still sending data from over 24 billion kilometers away, on only a couple of hundred watts of power. Attached to its side is a gold-plated record containing greetings in 55 languages, music and sounds from Earth, in case anyone ever finds it.|1977|NASA
The Pale Blue Dot | A 1990 photo from Voyager 1 shows Earth as a point of light. | In 1990, as Voyager 1 was leaving the solar system, Carl Sagan asked NASA to turn its camera back for one last look. In the resulting picture, from about 6 billion kilometers away, Earth is less than a single pixel, a tiny speck in a ray of sunlight. Sagan wrote of it that everyone you love, everyone you know, lived their lives on that dot. It's become a symbol of our place in the universe.|1990|NASA; Sagan, Pale Blue Dot (1994)
The first humans on the Moon | Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed on July 20, 1969. | On July 20, 1969, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed the lunar module Eagle on the Moon, while Michael Collins stayed in orbit above in the command module. They spent about two and a half hours walking on the surface, collecting samples and setting up experiments, and brought back 21.5 kilograms of lunar rock and soil. About 600 million people watched on television.|July 20, 1969|NASA Apollo 11
Sputnik and Gagarin | The first satellite flew in 1957 and the first person in space in 1961. | On October 4, 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, a polished metal ball that beeped as it orbited Earth. It was the first artificial satellite and it started the Space Race. Less than four years later, on April 12, 1961, Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space, and completed one orbit in 108 minutes. Many people in the US were shocked, and it spurred major investment in science education.|1957, 1961|NASA History
The Hubble Space Telescope | Launched in 1990, it has changed our picture of the universe. | Launched in 1990, Hubble orbits above the atmosphere, which blurs the view from the ground. Its first mirror was ground to the wrong shape, giving blurry images, until astronauts installed corrective optics in 1993. After that it transformed astronomy. The James Webb Space Telescope | A giant infrared telescope that began its work in 2022. | The James Webb telescope launched on Christmas Day 2021 and began science work in 2022. The Drake equation | A way to organize the question of how many civilizations might be out there. | In 1961 the astronomer Frank Drake wrote a formula to organize the question of how many civilizations in our galaxy might be able to communicate with us. It multiplies factors like the rate of star formation, the fraction of stars with planets, the fraction of those where life arises, and how long civilizations last. We know a few of the numbers and have to guess the rest, so answers range from "we're alone" to "millions."|1961|Drake, 1961|mixed
The Fermi paradox | If life is common, where is everybody? | Around 1950 the physicist Enrico Fermi was having lunch with colleagues, discussing extraterrestrial life, when he asked, "Where is everybody?" If the galaxy is so old and so large, and life is common, why haven't we seen any sign of it?
