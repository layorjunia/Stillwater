Mean, median and mode | Three different "averages", and the right one depends on the data. | These are three ways of saying "typical," and they can give very different answers. The mean is the usual average, found by adding everything up and dividing. The median is the middle value when you line them all up, and the mode is the most common one. If Bill Gates walks into a bar, the average wealth of the people in it shoots up, but the median hardly moves. For things like income or house prices, where a few huge values pull the mean, the median is usually the more honest summary.||Standard statistics text
Standard deviation | How spread out the numbers are around the average. | The average on its own doesn't tell you how spread out the numbers are. The standard deviation does. Karl Pearson gave it its name in 1893. Imagine two classes that both average 70 on a test. In one, the standard deviation is 5, so almost everyone scored between 60 and 80. In the other it's 20, so you have students at 30 and others at 100. The averages match, but the classes are very different places.|1893|Pearson, 1893
What a confidence interval means | A 95% interval comes from a method that captures the true value 95% of the time. | Studies often report a "95% confidence interval." It sounds like there's a 95 percent chance the true value is inside the range, but that's not quite what it means. It means that the method used would capture the true value in 95 percent of repeated studies. The difference is subtle, and surveys have found that many researchers get it wrong. A practical reading is that the interval shows the range of values the data can't rule out.||Hoekstra et al., 2014
The margin of error | A poll of about 1,000 people has a margin of about 3 percentage points. | News stories about polls often mention a margin of error, usually about plus or minus 3 percentage points for a poll of around 1,000 people. It reflects only random chance in picking the sample. It doesn't cover badly worded questions, or people who didn't respond, which can matter more. If a poll shows 52 percent to 48 percent with that margin, the two sides are statistically tied, even if the headline says someone's ahead.|±3%|Standard polling reference
The Literary Digest fiasco | In 1936 a poll of 2.4 million people got the election wrong. | In 1936 the magazine Literary Digest mailed out ten million ballots and got back more than 2.4 million, and predicted that Alf Landon would beat Franklin Roosevelt in a landslide. Roosevelt won in one of the biggest landslides in history. The magazine had drawn its sample from its own readers and from telephone and car-owner lists, which in the Depression skewed richer. George Gallup, polling a much smaller but representative sample, got it right.|2.4 million|Squire, 1988
Effect size | How big a difference is, not just whether it exists. | A study can find that something "works" and still tell you nothing about how much. Effect size measures how large the difference is. Jacob Cohen suggested rough benchmarks for a standardized difference: 0.2 is small, 0.5 is medium and 0.8 is large. A "statistically significant" result with a tiny effect may be of no practical use, particularly in large studies where even trivial differences can be detected.||Cohen, 1988
Statistical power | Small studies often miss real effects. | A study with too few participants may fail to detect a real effect. That's low statistical power. A 2013 review of neuroscience studies estimated that the typical study had a power of only about 21 percent, which means it would miss most real effects. Worse, low power also means the results that do cross the threshold of significance tend to exaggerate the true effect.|21%|Button et al., 2013
How to make music make you younger | A 2011 paper "showed" listening to "When I'm Sixty-Four" made people 18 months younger. | In 2011 Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson and Uri Simonsohn published a paper claiming that listening to the Beatles song "When I'm Sixty-Four" made people nearly a year and a half younger. It was a deliberate demonstration of how researchers, by making flexible choices about which data to include and which tests to run, can find "significant" results in nonsense. They then proposed rules to stop that kind of p-hacking.||Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn, 2011
The dead salmon | A dead fish in a brain scanner appeared to react to photos of people. | In 2009 the neuroscientist Craig Bennett put a dead Atlantic salmon in an fMRI brain scanner, showed it photographs of people in social situations, and asked it to determine their emotions. Without correcting for the thousands of statistical tests involved, the analysis found brain activity in the dead fish. It was a joke with a serious point: you have to correct for multiple comparisons. It won an Ig Nobel Prize in 2012.||Bennett et al., 2009
Pre-registration | Writing down your plan before you collect data. | In pre-registration, researchers publicly record their hypotheses and methods before collecting data. That stops them from quietly changing the question after they see which results turn out well. A 2021 comparison of studies found that 44 percent of those published in a "registered report" format found a positive result, compared with 96 percent of standard published papers, which suggests the usual literature is skewed toward positive findings.|44% vs 96%|Scheel, Schijen & Lakens, 2021
The meta-analysis | A study of studies, combining results to see the overall picture. | A meta-analysis pools the results of many studies on a question to give a clearer overall picture. The psychologist Gene Glass coined the term in 1976, when he combined hundreds of studies of psychotherapy. A larger body of evidence gives more precise answers than any single study. But it can only be as good as the studies it includes, and if negative results were never published, even the pooled answer can be biased.||Glass, 1976
Relative versus absolute risk | "Doubles your risk" can mean almost nothing if the risk was tiny. | A headline says a drug "doubles your risk," which sounds alarming. But if the risk was tiny to begin with, doubling it may mean very little. In 1995 the UK government warned that a new type of contraceptive pill doubled the risk of blood clots. The actual risk went from about 1 in 7,000 women to about 2 in 7,000. Many women stopped taking the pill, and there was a rise in unwanted pregnancies and abortions.|1 in 7,000 to 2|Gigerenzer, Calculated Risks (2002)
Number needed to treat | How many people must take a treatment for one to benefit. | One helpful way to express how well a treatment works is the number of people who need to take it for one person to benefit. If a drug cuts a risk from 2 percent to 1 percent, you could call that a 50 percent reduction. But 100 people have to take it for one to be helped, and the others get the costs and side effects. The number makes the trade-offs clearer.||Laupacis, Sackett & Roberts, 1988
Sensitivity and specificity | A test's power to find the sick, and to clear the healthy. | Any medical test can make two kinds of error. Sensitivity is how good it is at finding people who do have the condition, and specificity is how good it is at correctly clearing people who don't. A test can do very well on one and badly on the other. A very sensitive test catches nearly everyone who's sick but may also flag many healthy people, which is why a positive result often needs a follow-up.||Standard medical statistics text
Lead-time bias | Earlier detection can make survival look longer without extending life. | Screening tests find diseases earlier, and the apparent survival time after diagnosis gets longer. But that can be an illusion. Suppose a cancer is found at age 63 without screening and the patient dies at 70, which is 7 years of survival. With screening, it's found at 60, and the patient still dies at 70, so survival after diagnosis is 10 years, but the person didn't live any longer. The fairer test of screening is whether fewer people die of the disease.||Standard epidemiology text
Anscombe's quartet | Four data sets can have the same averages and correlation but look completely different. | In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe made up four small sets of numbers that have almost identical averages, spreads and correlations. Yet when you plot them, they look completely different: one is a neat line, one is a curve, one has a single outlier pulling the line, and one is a vertical stack of points. His point was that you should always graph your data, because summary numbers can hide what's really going on.|1973|Anscombe, 1973
The Datasaurus | A scatter of dots shaped like a dinosaur has the same statistics as a cloud of random dots. | In 2016 the data visualizer Alberto Cairo made up a data set that, when plotted, looks like a dinosaur, yet has the same averages, spread and correlation as a random scatter of dots. In 2017 researchers Justin Matejka and George Fitzmaurice built on it, showing a dozen other shapes, including a star and a bullseye, that all share the same summary statistics. It's a modern version of Anscombe's lesson: always look at the picture.||Matejka & Fitzmaurice, 2017
Truncated graphs | Cutting the bottom off an axis makes small differences look huge. | A bar chart is supposed to show quantities in proportion to the height of the bars. If the vertical axis starts at 95 percent instead of zero, a difference between 97 and 99 can look like the second bar is several times as tall. It's a common way to make small differences look dramatic, sometimes by accident. Always check where the axis starts. Edward Tufte called misleading graphs a violation of graphical integrity.||Tufte, The Visual Display of Quantitative Information (1983)
The law of truly large numbers | With enough chances, even very unlikely things will happen. | When there are enough chances, even extremely unlikely things are bound to happen. The statisticians Persi Diaconis and Frederick Mosteller pointed out that if an event has a one-in-a-million chance on a given day, then with hundreds of millions of people in America, it should happen to hundreds of people every day. So coincidences aren't strange. They're expected, when you consider how many opportunities there are.||Diaconis & Mosteller, 1989
The Prussian cavalry | Deaths from horse kicks in Prussian army corps fit a simple chance pattern. | In 1898 the Russian-Polish statistician Ladislaus Bortkiewicz looked at records of how many Prussian cavalry soldiers were killed by horse kicks each year, in 14 army corps over 20 years. He showed that the numbers fit a mathematical pattern known as the Poisson distribution, which describes rare events that occur independently at a steady average rate. It's a classic example of how even odd accidents follow statistical laws.|1898|Bortkiewicz, 1898
The lady tasting tea | Ronald Fisher's famous experiment on whether a woman could tell the milk was added first. | In the 1920s, at an English country research station, a woman claimed that she could tell whether the tea or the milk had been poured into a cup first. The statistician Ronald Fisher turned this into a famous experiment: eight cups, four of each kind, presented in random order. If she's just guessing, the chance of getting all eight right is 1 in 70. The example became a model for randomization and significance testing.|1 in 70|Fisher, 1935
A/B testing | Show two versions to different random users and see which does better. | When a website wants to know which version of a page works better, it can show version A to some visitors and version B to others, chosen at random, and compare the results. It's the same logic as a medical trial. In 2009 Google reportedly tested 41 shades of blue for a link bar, and a designer left the company partly because he felt that such testing was replacing design judgment.||Bowman, 2009
Lottery odds | The Powerball jackpot has odds of about 1 in 292 million. | The chance of winning the Powerball jackpot is about 1 in 292 million. If you bought a ticket for every weekly drawing, you'd have to keep going for about 5.6 million years to expect to win once. On average, a ticket is worth less than what you pay for it. People still play, and most of the pleasure is in the daydream.|1 in 292M|Multi-State Lottery Association
The Kelly criterion | A formula for how much to bet when you have an edge. | If you're offered a bet that's in your favor, how much of your money should you put on it? John Kelly, a physicist at Bell Labs, published a formula in 1956 for the answer. Bet too much, and a bad run can wipe you out, even though you have an edge. Bet too little, and you waste the edge. Many professional gamblers and investors use half the Kelly amount, to be safer.||Kelly, 1956
Supply and demand | Prices rise when something is scarce and fall when it is plentiful. | When something is scarce, its price tends to rise. When it's plentiful, it falls. The English economist Alfred Marshall drew the now-famous crossing curves of supply and demand in his 1890 textbook. The point where they cross is the price at which the amount that sellers want to sell equals the amount that buyers want to buy. It's a simple picture, but it explains a lot about how markets work.|1890|Marshall, 1890
Marginal thinking | Decisions depend on the next unit, not the average. | Water is essential for life but cheap, and diamonds are useless for survival but expensive. Economists puzzled over this paradox until the 1870s, when Carl Menger, William Stanley Jevons and Léon Walras each realized that value depends on the next unit, not the total. Because you already have plenty of water, one more glass is worth little, while one more diamond is scarce. Decisions are made at the margin.||Menger; Jevons; Walras, 1871-74
Comparative advantage | Countries and people gain from trading even if one is better at everything. | In 1817 the economist David Ricardo showed something surprising: two parties can both gain from trade even if one is better at everything. Imagine a lawyer who also types faster than her assistant. She still comes out ahead by letting the assistant type, because her time is worth more spent on legal work. The same logic applies to countries, and it's the main reason that economists generally favor trade.|1817|Ricardo, 1817
Adam Smith's pin factory | Dividing work makes everyone far more productive. | In The Wealth of Nations, published in 1776, Adam Smith described a pin factory where the work was divided into about eighteen distinct steps. He said ten workers, each specializing in a few tasks, could produce about 48,000 pins a day, while a single worker doing everything might make one pin a day, or at most twenty. The division of labor makes everyone far more productive.|48,000 pins|Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776)
The invisible hand | Individuals pursuing their own interests may unintentionally help society. | The phrase "invisible hand" is one of the best-known in economics, and Adam Smith used it only once in The Wealth of Nations, in 1776. He argued that people pursuing their own interests may, without intending it, end up helping society. Yet he was no simple cheerleader for business: in the same book, he warned that merchants rarely meet together without it ending in a conspiracy against the public.||Smith, 1776
Inflation | A general rise in prices, which means your money buys less. | Inflation means that prices in general are rising, so each dollar buys less. Many central banks aim for about 2 percent a year, which they consider a healthy level. Even that adds up. At 2 percent, prices double in about 36 years. At 7 percent, they double in about 10. That's why money sitting in a drawer slowly loses what it can buy.|About 2%|Standard macroeconomics text
Hyperinflation | When prices spiral out of control, money becomes worthless. | When a government prints too much money, prices can spiral out of control. In Germany in 1923, prices at one point doubled every few days, and people were paid twice a day and rushed to spend the money before it lost value. In Zimbabwe, in November 2008, monthly inflation reached an estimated 80 billion percent. When money stops working, people turn to barter or foreign currencies.||Hanke & Kwok, 2009
Central banks | Institutions that set interest rates to steer the economy. | A central bank sets short-term interest rates to influence the economy: lowering them to encourage borrowing and spending, and raising them to cool down inflation. The US Federal Reserve was created in 1913, after a series of bank panics, especially one in 1907. It has what's called a dual mandate from Congress: to keep prices stable and to promote maximum employment.|1913|Federal Reserve Act, 1913
Fiat money | Money that has value because governments say so and people accept it. | The dollar bill in your wallet has no gold behind it, and it isn't worth much as paper. It's money because the government says it is and because everyone accepts it. That's called fiat money, from the Latin for "let it be done." Until 1971, the US dollar could be exchanged for gold at a fixed rate, and when President Nixon ended that, the world moved fully to fiat currencies. Most money today is just numbers in bank accounts.|1971|Standard economics text
Diversification | Spreading your money lowers risk without lowering the average return. | "Don't put all your eggs in one basket" has a mathematical backing. In 1952 Harry Markowitz showed that if you combine investments that don't all move in the same direction at the same time, you can reduce your overall risk without lowering your expected return. He won a Nobel Prize for it in 1990. It's often called the only free lunch in finance.|1952|Markowitz, 1952
Index funds | A fund that owns the whole market instead of trying to pick winners. | An index fund simply buys all the stocks in a market, rather than trying to pick winners. John Bogle founded the first one available to ordinary investors in 1976, at Vanguard, and he was ridiculed for it at the time. Reports that compare funds, such as the S&P SPIVA scorecards, regularly find that over a 15-year period, around nine in ten professional stock funds do worse than the index they're trying to beat.|About 9 in 10|Bogle, 1976; S&P SPIVA reports
The efficient market hypothesis | Prices already reflect what is publicly known, so beating the market is very hard. | Eugene Fama proposed in 1970 that stock prices already reflect all publicly available information, so it's very hard to consistently beat the market. Other economists, like Robert Shiller, argued that markets can be driven by emotion and bubbles. In 2013 Fama and Shiller, with another economist, shared the Nobel Prize in economics, an indication of how both views have supporters. It's still debated.||Fama, 1970|mixed
Tulip mania | The 1637 Dutch craze for tulip bulbs is a famous bubble. | In the 1630s tulip bulbs in the Netherlands sold at extraordinary prices, and the craze is retold as the classic example of a speculative bubble that ruined thousands. Modern historians, like Anne Goldgar, who looked through the records, say it was much smaller than the legend: few people seem to have been ruined, and the evidence is thin. The story has been told so many times that the myth became the lesson.|1637|Goldgar, 2007; Garber, 1990|mixed
Lump sum or averaging in? | Investing all your money at once beat spreading it out about two-thirds of the time. | If you have a pile of money to invest, is it better to invest it all at once, or to spread it out over several months? A 2012 Vanguard study found that investing all at once did better about two-thirds of the time, simply because markets tend to rise more often than they fall. Spreading it out makes you less likely to regret putting everything in just before a drop, so it's a trade-off.|About 2 in 3|Vanguard, 2012
The time value of money | A dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year. | Would you rather have $100 today or $100 in a year? Most people say today, and they're right. You could invest the money and have more later, and prices will likely have risen. Economists "discount" future money to reflect this. It's the principle behind interest on loans and savings, and it's how the value of any investment or business is worked out.||Standard finance text
Franklin's bequest | Benjamin Franklin left money to Boston and Philadelphia, to be used after 200 years. | When Benjamin Franklin died in 1790, he left 1,000 pounds each to the cities of Boston and Philadelphia, to be loaned out at interest and not fully used for 200 years. By 1990 the funds had grown to millions of dollars: about $5 million in Boston's fund and $2 million in Philadelphia's. It's a vivid example of the power of compound interest over a long time.|1790|Franklin, 1790
The tragedy of the commons | Shared resources get overused when each user gains and the cost is spread. | In 1968 the ecologist Garrett Hardin described a pasture open to all herders. Each herder gains by adding another cow, while the cost of overgrazing is shared by everyone, so the pasture gets destroyed. It's an image for many shared resources, such as fisheries and the atmosphere. Elinor Ostrom, who won the 2009 Nobel Prize in economics, showed that many communities have managed shared resources well by creating their own rules.||Hardin, 1968; Ostrom, 1990
The prisoner's dilemma | Two people each do better by betraying, yet both do worse than if they cooperated. | Two suspects are held separately. Each is offered a deal to betray the other. Whatever the other does, betraying seems best for you. But if both betray, they do worse than if both had stayed quiet. Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher devised the game in 1950, and Albert Tucker told the prison story. In Robert Axelrod's computer tournaments around 1980, the winning strategy was "tit for tat": cooperate first, then copy the other player's last move.|1950|Flood & Dresher, 1950; Axelrod, 1984
The Nash equilibrium | A situation where nobody gains by changing their choice alone. | A Nash equilibrium is a situation in which no player can do better by changing only their own choice, given what the others are doing. John Nash proved in 1950 that every game with a finite number of players and choices has at least one. It describes stable outcomes, not necessarily good ones, as the prisoner's dilemma shows, where both players betraying is a stable result that's bad for both. Nash won the Nobel Prize in 1994.|1950|Nash, 1950
Network effects | Some things become more valuable as more people use them. | Some things become more valuable as more people use them. A telephone is useless if you're the only one who has one, and valuable if everyone you know does. That's a network effect. Metcalfe's law says that the value of a network grows with the square of the number of users, but studies of real networks suggest it grows more slowly. It helps explain why one social network or payment system can come to dominate.||Katz & Shapiro, 1985|mixed
The market for lemons | When buyers cannot tell good from bad, bad goods can drive out good ones. | In 1970 the economist George Akerlof wrote a paper about the used-car market. Sellers know whether their car is good or a "lemon," but buyers can't tell, so buyers are only willing to pay an average price. At that price, owners of good cars won't sell, and the market fills up with lemons. The paper showed how a lack of information can make markets fail. He shared the 2001 Nobel Prize for it.|1970|Akerlof, 1970
Moral hazard | People take more risks when someone else bears the cost. | When someone else bears the cost of a risk, people tend to take more of it. A driver with full insurance may be a little less careful. A bank that expects the government to bail it out if it fails may place bigger bets. Insurers try to limit it with deductibles and co-payments, so that the person still has something to lose.||Standard economics text
Externalities | A cost or benefit that falls on people who did not choose it. | An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on someone who didn't choose to take part. A factory's pollution harms people downwind, a negative externality. A neighbor's beautiful garden pleases passers-by, a positive one. In 1920 the economist Arthur Pigou argued that a tax on the polluter can make the price of a product reflect its true cost to society. Many modern carbon taxes follow the idea.|1920|Pigou, 1920
Public goods and free riders | Things everyone can use, like street lights or defense, are hard to fund voluntarily. | Some things, like street lighting, clean air or national defense, benefit everyone, whether or not they pay. That creates a free-rider problem: if you can use it either way, why pay? Voluntary contributions tend to fall short. That's the reason economists give for why governments usually pay for such goods with taxes, to make sure that everyone chips in.||Samuelson, 1954
The Coase theorem | If people can bargain cheaply, they will settle on an efficient outcome whoever holds the rights. | Ronald Coase argued in a 1960 paper that if people can bargain easily with each other, they'll reach an efficient arrangement no matter who starts out holding the legal rights. For example, a factory and its neighbors could settle the problem of smoke by negotiation, without a government rule. In real life, the costs of getting everyone together and agreeing are often too high, so the theorem works best for small groups.|1960|Coase, 1960
Price elasticity | How much demand changes when the price changes. | Price elasticity is how much demand changes when the price changes. For things people need and can't easily replace, like gasoline in the short run, demand barely drops when the price goes up. That's low elasticity. For things with lots of substitutes, like one brand of soda, a small price rise sends buyers elsewhere. That's high elasticity. Businesses and governments use it to predict how changes in price will play out.||Marshall, 1890
GDP and what it misses | Gross domestic product counts output, not well-being. | Gross domestic product measures the total value of goods and services a country produces. Simon Kuznets, who developed the measure in the 1930s, warned in a 1934 report to Congress that the welfare of a nation can scarcely be inferred from a measurement of national income. GDP counts cleaning up after a disaster as growth, but it doesn't count unpaid housework, leisure or the health of the environment.||Kuznets, 1934
The Gini coefficient | A single number for inequality, from 0 (everyone equal) to 1 (one person has everything). | The Gini coefficient is a single number that measures how unequal an income distribution is, from 0, where everyone has the same, to 1, where one person has everything. The Italian statistician Corrado Gini introduced it in 1912. It's handy for comparing countries: Scandinavian countries are around 0.25 to 0.28, and some other countries are above 0.6. It doesn't show where in the distribution the inequality lies.|1912|Gini, 1912
The minimum wage debate | Does raising it cost jobs? | Does raising the minimum wage cost jobs? In 1994 the economists David Card and Alan Krueger compared fast-food restaurants in New Jersey, which had raised its minimum wage, with neighboring Pennsylvania, which hadn't. They found no loss of jobs in New Jersey. Other studies have found different results, so the debate continues. David Card shared the 2021 Nobel Prize in economics for his work on such "natural experiments."||Card & Krueger, 1994|mixed
Natural experiments | Using chance events to study cause and effect. | Economists can't usually run controlled experiments on whole economies. But sometimes events create something close: a lottery for the military draft, a state border where laws differ or a sudden change in a rule. These are called natural experiments. In 2021 David Card, Joshua Angrist and Guido Imbens shared the Nobel Prize in economics for developing ways to learn about cause and effect from them.||Nobel Prize in Economics, 2021
Nudges | Small changes in how choices are presented can steer behavior. | A nudge is a small change in how choices are presented that steers people toward a better decision, without forbidding anything, such as making organ donation or saving for retirement the default. Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein popularized the idea in their 2008 book. A 2022 review found that, once publication bias is accounted for, the average effect shrinks to nearly zero, though some well-designed nudges clearly work.||Thaler & Sunstein, 2008; Maier et al., 2022|mixed
Veblen goods | Things people want more as the price goes up. | Normally, when a price goes up, people buy less. But for some luxury goods, demand can rise as the price rises, because the high price is part of the appeal. In 1899 the economist Thorstein Veblen wrote about "conspicuous consumption," buying things to show status. Designer handbags, luxury watches and certain sports cars fit. Ordinary goods don't work that way.|1899|Veblen, 1899
Cash flow versus profit | A company can be profitable and still run out of cash. | A company can show a profit on paper and still run out of cash. Profit counts sales when they're made, even if customers haven't paid yet. Cash flow is the money that's actually in the bank. Many businesses that look healthy fail because customers pay slowly while suppliers and employees need to be paid right away. It's why experienced owners say "cash is king."||Standard accounting text
Margin versus markup | Margin is profit as a share of price, markup is profit as a share of cost. | Margin and markup are easy to mix up. Markup is profit as a percentage of what the item cost you. Margin is profit as a percentage of the price you charge. If you buy something for $60 and sell it for $100, the profit is $40, so the markup is about 67 percent and the margin is 40 percent. Confusing the two is a common cause of pricing too low.||Standard accounting text
Unit economics | Does each sale make money after what it costs to make and win it? | Unit economics asks whether you make money on each individual sale, after counting everything it costs to make the product and to win the customer. Suppose it costs $50 in advertising to get a customer who brings in $30 of profit. Every sale loses $20, and growing faster just loses money faster. It's worth working out before you try to scale a business.||Standard business text
The break-even point | The sales you need to cover all your costs. | The break-even point is how much you need to sell to cover all your costs. You work it out by dividing your fixed costs, such as rent and salaries, by the profit you make on each unit. Below that number, you lose money, and above it, every additional sale adds to your profit. It's one of the first numbers to know before starting a business or launching a product.||Standard accounting text
Disruptive innovation | Cheaper, simpler products can start at the bottom and overtake leaders. | In 1997 the Harvard professor Clayton Christensen argued that successful companies can be overtaken by cheaper, simpler products that start at the bottom of the market, and improve until they threaten the leaders. It became enormously influential. Critics, including the historian Jill Lepore in a 2014 article, said that his examples were chosen after the fact, so it's better as a way of describing the past than predicting the future.||Christensen, 1997; Lepore, 2014|mixed
The minimum viable product | The simplest version of something that tests whether people want it. | A minimum viable product is the simplest version of a product that you can put in front of real customers to learn whether they want it. Eric Ries made the term popular in his 2011 book The Lean Startup. The idea is to learn early, with little cost, before spending months building something nobody wants. It's about testing assumptions, not about shipping something poor.||Ries, The Lean Startup (2011)
Opportunity cost of time | An hour spent on one thing is an hour not spent on another. | Money can be saved for later, but time can't. Every hour is spent on something, and spending it on one thing means not spending it on another. That's why the question "Is this worth doing?" is a weak test, because nearly anything has some value. A better question is "What else could I do with this hour?" That brings the real cost into view.||Standard economics text
