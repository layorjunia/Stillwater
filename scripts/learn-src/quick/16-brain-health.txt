# Health, sleep, food and exercise: what the evidence supports, and where it is mixed. General information, not medical advice.

How much exercise? | Adults are advised 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, plus muscle work on two days. | The World Health Organization's 2020 guidelines say that adults should aim for 150 to 300 minutes a week of moderate activity, such as brisk walking, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening work on two days. But the guidelines also stress that any activity is better than none. If you can't hit the target, doing even a little less still brings most of the benefit, so don't treat it as all or nothing.|150-300 min|WHO, 2020
The 10,000 steps story | The round number came from a 1960s Japanese pedometer ad, not from research. | The round number didn't come from research. It seems to have started with a Japanese pedometer sold in the mid-1960s, called Manpo-kei, which means "10,000 steps meter." It's often said that the number was chosen partly because the character for 10,000 looks like a person walking. When researchers looked at actual health data in 2022, they found that the risk of early death fell until about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day for adults over 60, and 8,000 to 10,000 for younger adults. More is fine, but the gains level off.||Paluch et al., Lancet Public Health, 2022
Exercise and lifespan | Meeting the exercise guidelines goes with living about three years longer. | In 2012 researchers pooled data from six large studies covering about 650,000 people. They found that people who got about 150 minutes a week of brisk walking, the recommended minimum, had a life expectancy 3.4 years longer than those who got none. That's an association, not proof of cause, since healthier people may exercise more. But the pattern is strong and consistent across many studies.|3.4 years|Moore et al., PLoS Medicine, 2012
Muscle and aging | We lose muscle gradually from about age 30, unless we use it. | After about age 30, we gradually lose muscle mass, at a rate of roughly 3 to 8 percent per decade, unless we do something about it. The loss is called sarcopenia, and it speeds up in later life, which affects balance, strength and independence. Strength training, such as lifting weights or using resistance bands twice a week, can slow or even reverse it, at any age, and it's linked with a lower risk of early death.||Volpi et al., 2004
Muscle soreness | Soreness peaks one to three days after a workout, and lactic acid is not the cause. | The ache that peaks a day or two after an unfamiliar workout is called delayed-onset muscle soreness. It used to be blamed on lactic acid building up, but lactate clears from muscles within an hour or so of exercise. The soreness is thought to come from tiny tears in muscle fibers and the inflammation that follows. It fades as your body adapts, which is why the same workout hurts less the next time.||Cheung, Hume & Maxwell, 2003
Sitting and moving | Long unbroken sitting is linked with health risks, and short walks break it up. | Long stretches of unbroken sitting have been linked to health risks. In one 2012 experiment, people who got up for just a couple of minutes of light walking every 20 minutes had lower blood sugar and insulin levels after meals than those who sat the whole time. How much of sitting's harm is separate from simply not exercising is still debated. Either way, getting up often does no harm.||Dunstan et al., 2012|mixed
Insomnia treatment | The best-supported first treatment for chronic insomnia is not a pill but a talking therapy. | For chronic insomnia, the best-supported first treatment isn't a sleeping pill. It's a form of talking therapy called CBT for insomnia, which the American College of Physicians recommended as first-line treatment in 2016. It includes keeping a fixed wake-up time, limiting the time you spend awake in bed, and changing anxious thoughts about sleep. It helps most people, and its benefits last longer than those of sleeping pills.||Qaseem et al., ACP, 2016
A fixed wake-up time | The most useful sleep habit may be waking at the same time every day. | If you could change just one thing about your sleep habits, a good choice might be to wake at the same time every day, including weekends. It anchors your body clock, which makes it easier to feel sleepy at the right time in the evening. Sleeping in for hours on days off creates a kind of "social jetlag," as if you'd flown across time zones, and it's one reason Monday mornings feel so hard.||Roenneberg et al., 2006
Alcohol and sleep | A drink can help you fall asleep and still ruin the night. | A drink or two may make it easier to fall asleep, which is why many people use it. But as your body breaks down the alcohol during the night, it disrupts sleep. It reduces REM sleep, the stage when most dreaming occurs, and it fragments the second half of the night. You may not remember waking, but the sleep is lighter and less restful, and you often feel tired the next day.||Ebrahim et al., 2013
A cool bedroom | Sleep is easier at a lower room temperature, around 60 to 67 degrees Fahrenheit. | Most sleep experts recommend a bedroom temperature of about 60 to 67 degrees Fahrenheit, or roughly 15 to 19 degrees Celsius. Your core body temperature naturally falls as you go to sleep, and a cool room helps that happen. The range comes from sleep medicine guidance rather than a single study, and comfort varies from person to person. Heavy blankets and a cool room often work better than a warm room and light covers.|60-67 °F|Sleep Foundation; Okamoto-Mizuno & Mizuno, 2012
Blue light and sleep | Is the glow of screens ruining sleep? | It's a common worry that the glow from phones and tablets is ruining our sleep. Bright light late in the evening does delay the body clock, and blue-rich light does so a bit more. A 2014 study found that reading on a light-emitting e-reader before bed delayed the release of melatonin. But the effect of typical screens in real life seems small, and what you're doing on the screen, whether it's exciting or stressful, may matter as much.||Chang et al., 2015|mixed
Melatonin | A hormone that signals night to the body, not a sedative. | Melatonin is a hormone your brain releases at night to signal that it's dark, and the pills are often taken as sleep aids. But it isn't really a sedative. It works best for shifting the body clock, as with jet lag or for people who go to sleep very late. Small doses, around 0.5 to 3 milligrams, work as well as large ones, and tests have found that the amounts in supplements vary widely from what's on the label.||Herxheimer & Petrie, 2002; Cohen et al., 2023
The eight glasses myth | There is no scientific basis for "drink eight glasses of water a day". | You've probably heard that you should drink eight glasses of water a day. There's no scientific basis for it. It may trace to a 1945 recommendation of about 2.5 liters a day, which also noted that most of that is already in prepared foods. Needs vary with your size, the weather and how active you are. For most healthy people, thirst is a good guide, and tea, coffee and fruit all count.||Valtin, 2002
Sugar and hyperactivity | Sugar does not make children hyperactive. | Many parents are sure that sugar makes children hyper. But when scientists tested it in controlled trials, giving some children sugar and others a sugar-free placebo without anyone knowing which, there was no effect on behavior. A 1995 review of 23 such studies found nothing, even in children who were said to be sensitive. In another experiment, parents who were told that their child had had sugar rated the child as more hyperactive, though they hadn't.||Wolraich et al., 1995
Protein | Adults need at least 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight a day. | The official recommended minimum for adults is 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, which works out to about 56 grams for a person of 70 kilograms, or 154 pounds. Some experts argue for more, especially for older adults, who absorb protein less efficiently, and for people who lift weights. It's easy to meet from foods like beans, eggs, fish, dairy and meat.|0.8 g/kg|US Dietary Reference Intakes
Fiber | Most Americans eat only about half the fiber recommended. | Fiber is the part of plants that your body can't digest, and it's good for your gut and your heart. Experts recommend about 25 grams a day for women and 38 for men, but the average American eats only about 16. Beans, oats, vegetables, fruit and whole grains are good sources. Diets high in fiber are linked with a lower risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and some cancers.|About 16 g|USDA Dietary Guidelines
Ultra-processed food | In a tightly controlled trial, people ate more on an ultra-processed diet. | In a 2019 study at the US National Institutes of Health, 20 volunteers lived in a research center for a month. For two weeks they ate meals made mostly of ultra-processed foods, and for two weeks, mostly unprocessed foods, with the menus matched for calories, sugar, fat and fiber on offer. On the ultra-processed diet, they ate about 500 more calories a day, and gained about a kilogram in two weeks.|+500 kcal|Hall et al., Cell Metabolism, 2019
The Mediterranean diet | A pattern rich in vegetables, olive oil, fish, nuts and legumes lowers heart risk. | A pattern of eating rich in vegetables, olive oil, fish, nuts and legumes, with little red meat, has among the best evidence for protecting the heart. The 2013 PREDIMED trial in Spain found that it cut major heart events by about 30 percent. The paper was retracted in 2018 because of problems with how some participants had been randomized, then reanalyzed and republished with similar results.|About -30%|Estruch et al., NEJM, 2013, 2018
Is breakfast essential? | The claim that breakfast is the most important meal is not well supported. | "Breakfast is the most important meal of the day" was popularized, in part, by cereal marketing. When researchers reviewed the trials in 2019, they found that eating breakfast didn't help people lose weight, and that those who ate it tended to consume slightly more calories overall. Whether you eat breakfast matters less than what you eat during the day. If you're hungry in the morning, eat; if not, there's no need to force it.||Sievert et al., BMJ, 2019|mixed
Intermittent fasting | Eating only in certain hours leads to weight loss, but not better than ordinary dieting. | Eating only during a limited window, say eight hours a day, has become very popular. In a 2022 trial of 139 people with obesity, one group ate within an eight-hour window and another simply counted calories. After a year, there was no difference in weight loss. Time-restricted eating works for people who find it easier, but the best plan is the one that you can stick with.||Liu et al., NEJM, 2022|mixed
Vitamin D supplements | Taking vitamin D does not prevent cancer or heart disease in people who are not deficient. | Vitamin D is essential, and many people take supplements hoping to prevent disease. But in the large VITAL trial, 25,871 healthy adults took 2,000 units a day or a placebo for about five years. There was no reduction in cancer or heart disease. People who are actually deficient do need vitamin D, which a blood test can show, but for everyone else, extra pills don't seem to add protection.||Manson et al., NEJM, 2019
Vitamin C and colds | Vitamin C does not prevent colds for most people. | Vitamin C has a long reputation as a cold preventer, popularized by the chemist Linus Pauling. A Cochrane review of dozens of trials found that taking it regularly doesn't prevent colds in most people. It does shorten them slightly, by about 8 percent in adults. The one exception is people under heavy physical stress, such as marathon runners and skiers, among whom it halved the risk.|About 8%|Hemilä & Chalker, Cochrane, 2013
Multivitamins | For healthy adults, a daily multivitamin has not been shown to prevent disease. | A multivitamin is a popular daily habit. In 2022 the US Preventive Services Task Force reviewed the evidence and concluded that there isn't enough to say whether most vitamin and mineral supplements prevent cancer or heart disease in healthy adults. They haven't been shown to do harm at normal doses, but a balanced diet comes first. Some people, such as pregnant women, do need particular supplements.||USPSTF, 2022|mixed
Fish oil supplements | Supplements of omega-3 fats have not shown heart benefits in large trials. | Eating fish twice a week is linked with better health, so fish oil capsules seem like a natural idea. But in large trials of healthy people, including VITAL and ASCEND, omega-3 supplements didn't reduce heart attacks or strokes. It may be that the benefit comes from eating fish in place of other foods, or from other nutrients in it. For people with very high triglycerides, a prescription form can help.||ASCEND, 2018; Manson et al., 2019|mixed
Handwashing | Twenty seconds with soap removes most germs. | The CDC recommends washing your hands with soap and water for at least 20 seconds, which is about as long as humming "Happy Birthday" twice. Scrub the backs of your hands, between your fingers and under your nails. Soap and friction lift germs from the skin, so they're rinsed away. If you can't wash, an alcohol-based sanitizer with at least 60 percent alcohol is a good second choice.|20 seconds|CDC
Do cold weather and wet hair cause colds? | Colds come from viruses, not from chill. | Your grandmother may have told you to dry your hair or you'd catch a cold. Colds are caused by viruses, not by chill. But there may be a grain of truth. Cooling of the nose can weaken its defenses against viruses such as rhinovirus, and in winter, people gather indoors, where germs spread. Going out with wet hair doesn't cause an infection by itself, but it may make you more vulnerable if a virus is present.||Eccles, 2002; Johnson & Eccles, 2005|mixed
Herd immunity for measles | About 95% of people need to be immune to stop measles spreading. | When enough people in a community are immune, a disease can't spread easily, and even those who can't be vaccinated are protected. For measles, which is extremely contagious, about 95 percent of people need to be immune. One infected person can pass it on to 12 to 18 unprotected people. That's why even a small fall in vaccination rates can lead to outbreaks.|About 95%|WHO
Sunscreen | SPF 30 blocks about 97% of the sun's burning UVB rays. | A sunscreen of SPF 30 blocks about 97 percent of the burning UVB rays, and SPF 50 blocks about 98 percent, so the gain beyond 30 is small. In an Australian trial in the 1990s, people told to use sunscreen daily had about half as many invasive melanomas, the most dangerous skin cancer, as the others, after about ten years of follow-up. Reapply every two hours and after swimming, as no sunscreen blocks everything.||Green et al., J Clin Oncol, 2011
Quitting smoking | Stopping before age 40 gains back nearly all the years lost. | In a large study published in 2013, researchers followed more than 200,000 American adults. Smokers who quit between the ages of 25 and 34 gained about ten years of life compared with those who kept smoking, essentially reaching the life expectancy of those who'd never smoked. Quitting between 35 and 44 gained about nine years, and between 45 and 54, about six. Even those who quit at 65 gained some years.|~10 years|Jha et al., NEJM, 2013
Alcohol and health | The World Health Organization says no level of alcohol is entirely safe. | In 2023 the World Health Organization stated that no level of alcohol consumption is entirely safe for health. For years, studies suggested that moderate drinking might protect the heart. But a 2016 analysis found that the apparent benefit mostly disappeared when it was taken into account that the "non-drinkers" in many studies included former drinkers who'd quit because of illness, which made abstainers look unhealthy.||WHO, 2023; Stockwell et al., 2016|mixed
Normal body temperature | The average is a bit below 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit. | The "normal" body temperature of 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit came from a huge study of about 25,000 people by the German doctor Carl Wunderlich in the 1850s. Modern measurements find the average is lower, about 97.5 to 97.9, and a 2020 study found that the average in the US has been drifting downward, by about 0.03 degrees Celsius for each decade of birth. It also varies by time of day and between people.|~97.9 °F|Wunderlich, 1868; Protsiv et al., eLife, 2020
Blood pressure | Normal blood pressure is below 120 over 80. | Blood pressure is written as two numbers, such as 120 over 80. The top number is the pressure in your arteries as the heart beats, and the bottom is the pressure between beats. Normal is below 120 over 80. High blood pressure usually has no symptoms, so the only way to know is to have it measured. It's a major risk factor for heart disease and stroke, and it's treatable.|120/80|American Heart Association
Cracking knuckles | It does not cause arthritis. | The idea that cracking your knuckles causes arthritis is a persistent myth. The popping sound comes from gas bubbles in the joint fluid. A doctor named Donald Unger cracked the knuckles of his left hand, but not his right, at least twice a day for more than 60 years, to test it. He found no arthritis in either hand, and he received an Ig Nobel Prize in 2009.||Unger, 1998
Swallowed gum | Gum does not stay in the stomach for seven years. | You may have heard that swallowed gum stays in your stomach for seven years. That's false. Gum base isn't digested like other food, but the gut moves it along like other indigestible things, such as seeds or fiber, and it passes out within a few days. Swallowing gum occasionally isn't dangerous, though an excessive amount in a small child can cause a blockage.||Standard pediatric guidance
Hair and nails after death | They do not keep growing. | It's a classic scary story that hair and nails keep growing after death. They don't. Growth requires the energy supply of living cells. What happens is that skin dries out and pulls back, which exposes more of the nails and the stubble on a face, and makes them look longer. This is the origin of the myth.||Standard medical reference
CPR rhythm | Press hard and fast on the center of the chest, about 100 to 120 times a minute. | In cardiopulmonary resuscitation, you push hard and fast on the center of the chest, about 100 to 120 times a minute, to keep blood flowing to the brain. The Bee Gees song "Stayin' Alive" has about 103 beats per minute, and in one study, people who practiced to it kept good pace. If you see someone collapse, call emergency services first, and use an automated external defibrillator if there's one nearby.|100-120/min|American Heart Association; Matlock et al., 2012
Stroke: FAST | Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call emergency services. | A stroke happens when blood flow to part of the brain is blocked or a vessel bursts. The acronym FAST helps you recognize it: Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, and Time to call emergency services. Treatment works best within a few hours. On average, the brain loses about 1.9 million neurons for each minute that a stroke goes untreated, which is why doctors say "time is brain."||American Stroke Association
Heart attacks differ in women | Women more often have symptoms other than chest pain. | The classic picture of a heart attack, crushing chest pain and pain down the left arm, is more typical of men. Women, while they often do have chest discomfort too, are more likely to have other symptoms: unusual tiredness, nausea, shortness of breath, or pain in the jaw, neck or back. Because the signs can be less obvious, women sometimes delay getting help. Any of these symptoms deserves a call for help.||American Heart Association
Drowning is quiet | Drowning rarely looks like thrashing and shouting. | In films, drowning looks like a person thrashing and shouting. In reality, it's usually silent. A person in trouble can't call out because they need all their breath just to breathe. Their arms tend to press down on the water, they stay upright, and it can take as little as 20 to 60 seconds. It's why parents are advised to watch children near water constantly, and not to rely on hearing a splash or a cry.||Pia, 1974
Pain is not the same as damage | How much it hurts depends on more than how much is injured. | How much something hurts depends on much more than how badly the body is hurt. The brain decides how to interpret signals from the body, and mood, attention, past experience and the situation all change the result. Soldiers with serious wounds sometimes report little pain, and people with no visible damage can have severe pain. Both are real. Understanding this can be a helpful part of managing chronic pain.||Melzack & Wall, 1965; Moseley & Butler, 2015
Time in nature | Spending about two hours a week in nature is linked with better health and well-being. | In a 2019 survey of nearly 20,000 people in England, those who spent at least 120 minutes a week in green spaces, such as parks and woods, were significantly more likely to report good health and high well-being than people who spent none. There was no benefit with less than that. It didn't matter if the time was in one long visit or several short ones. It's a measure of association, but it fits other research.|120 min a week|White et al., Scientific Reports, 2019
Bright light for winter blues | Sitting in front of a very bright light in the morning helps seasonal depression. | Some people become depressed each winter, when the days are short. In 1984 the psychiatrist Norman Rosenthal and colleagues described the condition, which they called seasonal affective disorder. A common treatment is to sit in front of a special lamp that gives off 10,000 lux of bright light for about 30 minutes in the morning. It has helped many people, and is considered a first-line treatment for the condition.|10,000 lux|Rosenthal et al., 1984
Probiotics | Live bacteria in food or pills help some conditions, but claims run ahead of evidence. | Probiotics are live bacteria in foods like yogurt or in supplements, and they're marketed for all kinds of health benefits. The evidence is good for a few things, such as preventing diarrhea that's caused by antibiotics. For general gut health in healthy people, though, results are mixed and depend on the specific strain. Claims on labels often run ahead of what research can support.||Hill et al., 2014|mixed
Posture | Slouching has not been shown to cause back pain. | We're often told to sit up straight to avoid back pain. But reviews of the research haven't found a strong link between slouching and pain. A more useful rule may be that the best posture is the next one: changing position regularly, instead of staying in any one posture for a long time. And for back pain that you already have, staying active generally helps more than resting in bed.||Slater et al., 2019|mixed
The 20-20-20 rule | Every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds. | To reduce eye strain from long stretches at a screen, eye doctors suggest this rule: every 20 minutes, look at something at least 20 feet away for 20 seconds. Remembering to blink fully also helps, since we blink much less when staring at screens. The research for the exact numbers is limited, but taking regular breaks from close-up work is sensible and does reduce discomfort.||Sheppard & Wolffsohn, 2018|mixed
Static stretching before a workout | Holding a stretch for a long time right before lifting or sprinting may briefly reduce power. | Holding a stretch for a long time before lifting weights or sprinting has been found in some studies to briefly reduce muscle power and performance. A better way to prepare is a dynamic warm-up: light movements that resemble the activity, like leg swings, lunges or easy jogging, which raise your temperature and heart rate. Stretching is still useful for building flexibility over weeks, just not as a warm-up.||Behm et al., 2016|mixed
