Optimism bias | Most people expect their own future to go better than the average person's. | In 1980 the psychologist Neil Weinstein asked college students to rate their chances of a list of life events against those of their classmates. They put good things, like a long life or a satisfying job, above average for themselves, and bad things, like a divorce or an illness, below. That can't be right for everyone, since half of any group is at or below the middle. Mild optimism can help you keep going. The risk comes when it makes us skip the insurance, the savings or the doctor's visit.||Weinstein, 1980
Overconfidence | We are surer of our answers than our accuracy deserves. | Researchers have run a simple test for decades. They ask people questions like "how long is the Nile?" and ask them to give a range they're 90% sure contains the true answer. If people were well calibrated, about nine in ten ranges would hit. In the classic studies, only about half to two-thirds did. People weren't ignorant, they just drew the lines too narrow. The cheap fix is to widen your ranges until they feel uncomfortably wide, then check how often you're right.|50-65%|Alpert & Raiffa, 1982; Russo & Schoemaker, 1992
Endowment effect | Owning something makes you value it more than you did a minute before. | In 1990 Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch and Richard Thaler tried a neat experiment with coffee mugs. Half the students in a classroom were given a mug and the rest got nothing. Then they traded. The owners wouldn't sell for less than about $7, but the others wouldn't pay more than a little under $3. If it were only about the mug, there'd be plenty of deals. The mug hadn't changed, only who held it, and giving something up hurts more than not having it ever did.|$7 vs $3|Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, 1990
Status quo bias | Given a choice, we tend to stay with what we have, even when a change would be better. | When William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser looked at how people chose among options in the late 1980s, they saw a strong pull toward whatever they already had or whatever was marked as the default. At Harvard, long-time employees mostly stayed in their old health plans, while newly hired ones chose the newer plans far more often. Doing nothing feels safe because the current state is the baseline against which everything else looks like a risk. The cost is the better option you never looked at because you didn't have to.||Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988
The default effect | The option that is pre-selected wins far more often than it should. | In some countries you become an organ donor only if you tick a box to opt in. In others you're a donor unless you tick a box to opt out. Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein compared the two systems in 2003. In Germany, where you opt in, about 12% were registered, and in Austria, where you opt out, it was almost everyone, 99.98%. People didn't suddenly differ in generosity. Most simply went with whatever the form did if they did nothing.|12% vs 99.98%|Johnson & Goldstein, 2003
The halo effect | One good trait makes us assume other good traits. | Psychologist Edward Thorndike noticed in 1920 that army officers who rated soldiers on separate traits gave suspiciously similar scores across all of them. A good impression in one area seemed to glow onto everything else. In a later experiment, Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson had students watch a lecturer who was either warm or cold to them. Those who saw the cold version found his accent and mannerisms more irritating. Both groups saw the same man; he just acted differently. They also insisted that liking him hadn't affected their ratings.||Thorndike, 1920; Nisbett & Wilson, 1977
Self-serving bias | We credit ourselves for successes and blame circumstances for failures. | When something goes well, we tend to say it's because of our ability and effort. When it goes badly, we point to bad luck, a hard test or other people. It reverses for others, where we're quicker to blame their character for their failures. Researchers have found this pattern across many kinds of tasks. Some of it may be honest guesswork, since you know your own effort better than anyone's. But part of it clearly protects how we feel about ourselves.||Miller & Ross, 1975; Mullen & Riordan, 1988
The just-world belief | We want to believe people get what they deserve, so we blame victims. | Melvin Lerner got curious about why people blame victims. In a 1966 study with Carolyn Simmons, volunteers watched what looked like a woman being given painful shocks as part of a memory experiment. When they couldn't help, and she seemed to keep suffering, they began to rate her as less likable and less worthy. We like to think the world is fair, so if something bad happened to someone, it can feel safer to believe they somehow earned it.||Lerner & Simmons, 1966
Mere exposure | The more often we see something, the more we tend to like it. | In the 1960s Robert Zajonc showed people a series of made-up words and Chinese-looking characters, some once and some many times. Later he asked which seemed to mean something good. The ones people had seen more often got better ratings, even though no one knew what any of them meant. The effect works without our noticing. It helps explain why familiar songs, brands and faces start to feel likable, and why advertisers repeat themselves so much.||Zajonc, 1968
Primacy and recency | In a list, we remember the first items and the last ones best. | If you read someone a list of twenty words and ask them to write down all they can remember, the result tends to look like a U. People recall the first few words well, probably because they had time to rehearse them into long-term memory. They also recall the last few, which are still sitting in short-term memory. The words in the middle fade. It's why the beginning and end of a talk stick, and why it's wise to put what matters most in one of those spots.||Murdock, 1962
The Barnum effect | Vague descriptions feel personal when we are told they are about us. | In 1948 Bertram Forer gave his students a personality sketch and said it had been written just for them from a test. Every student actually got the same text, assembled from lines of a newsstand horoscope. Lines like "you have a need for others to like you" and "at times you doubt yourself." They rated how well it described them from 0 to 5, and the average was 4.26. Vague statements that apply to nearly everyone feel personal when we think they're about us.|4.26 / 5|Forer, 1949
Cognitive dissonance | Holding two clashing beliefs, or acting against a belief, is uncomfortable, so we change one. | Leon Festinger's idea was that holding two conflicting thoughts is uncomfortable, and we work to ease it. In his classic 1959 experiment, people did a very dull task, then were paid either $1 or $20 to tell the next person it was fun. Those paid $1 later rated the task as more enjoyable than those paid $20. The $20 group had a good reason to say something untrue. The $1 group had only the thought "I said it was fun," so some of them quietly decided it must have been.|$1 vs $20|Festinger & Carlsmith, 1959
The overjustification effect | Rewarding something people already enjoy can make them enjoy it less. | Mark Lepper, David Greene and Richard Nisbett studied preschoolers who liked to draw. Some were promised a "good player" certificate for drawing, some got one as an unexpected surprise, and some got nothing. A week or two later, the children who'd expected a reward spent less time drawing on their own than the others. A reward given for something already enjoyed can turn it into work. We may start to think we're doing it for the prize.||Lepper, Greene & Nisbett, 1973
Illusory correlation | We see links between things that are not really linked. | Loren and Jean Chapman wanted to understand why clinicians kept trusting the Draw-a-Person test. They showed students drawings paired with symptoms, but the pairings were random. The students still reported the same links that the clinicians believed in, such as big eyes going with suspiciousness. It showed that those links were in people's heads, not in the data. When we expect two things to go together, we notice the cases that fit and forget the many that don't.||Chapman & Chapman, 1967
The ostrich effect | We avoid information that might be bad news. | Researchers looked at how often investors in Sweden logged in to check their accounts, and found a pattern. After the stock market had gone down, people looked less often than after it had gone up. Avoiding the news felt like avoiding the loss. It's a very human response, but it can be costly. If a bill, a test result or a bank statement is waiting, not looking won't change what it says, and it takes away your chance to respond early.||Karlsson, Loewenstein & Seppi, 2009
Zero-risk bias | We prefer to wipe out one risk completely rather than cut a larger total risk by more. | Suppose you can make one hazard vanish entirely or cut a bigger hazard by more than that. Many people prefer the first, because getting to zero feels like a clean win. In surveys on cleaning up hazardous waste sites, Jonathan Baron and colleagues found people did lean toward plans that removed a small risk completely, even if another plan would reduce the total risk by more. The comfort of "none" can steer resources away from where they'd do the most good.||Baron, Gowda & Kunreuther, 1993
The hot hand | Does a basketball player who has hit several shots in a row become more likely to hit the next? | Basketball fans believe a player who has made several shots in a row is more likely to make the next. In 1985 Thomas Gilovich, Robert Vallone and Amos Tversky looked at the data and said no, it was an illusion, and the paper became a famous example of a bias. In 2018 Joshua Miller and Adam Sanjurjo found a subtle statistical error in how the streaks had been counted. When they corrected it, a small real hot-hand effect appeared. A good reminder that even famous findings get revised.||Gilovich, Vallone & Tversky, 1985; Miller & Sanjurjo, 2018|mixed
The curse of knowledge | Once you know something, it is hard to imagine not knowing it. | In 1990 Elizabeth Newton, then a Stanford student, asked people to tap out the rhythm of a well-known song like "Happy Birthday" on a table, while a listener tried to guess it. The tappers predicted listeners would get half of them. Listeners guessed correctly only about 2.5% of the time. The tappers could hear the tune in their heads and couldn't imagine not hearing it. This is why experts can be poor teachers, and why a first draft often makes sense only to its author.|50% vs 2.5%|Newton, 1990; Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber, 1989
Mental accounting | We treat money differently depending on its label, though a dollar is a dollar. | Richard Thaler suggested that people don't treat money as one big pot, but keep mental accounts with different rules. Kahneman and Tversky made it vivid with two stories. Someone loses a $10 ticket to a play and has to decide whether to buy another, and 46% said they would. Someone else loses $10 in cash on the way in, and 88% still bought a ticket. In both cases they were out $10, but the ticket seemed to belong to the "entertainment" account.|88% vs 46%|Kahneman & Tversky, 1984
Hyperbolic discounting | A reward now looks much bigger than the same reward a little later. | Most of us value now more than later, but the odd part is how the discount changes with distance. Someone might take $50 today rather than $100 in a year. Yet that same person, asked to choose between $50 in five years and $100 in six, will usually wait for the larger sum. The gap is the same, but when the first reward is close, it's irresistible. That's why we say we'll start saving or exercising "next month" and rarely do when next month comes.||Ainslie, 1975; Laibson, 1997
Illusory superiority | Most of us think we are better than average at most things. | In 1981 the Swedish psychologist Ola Svenson surveyed American drivers and asked how their skill compared with the others in the study. 93% said they were better than the median, which is mathematically impossible, since only half can be above it. The finding has been repeated for intelligence, humor and many other traits. Most of us rate ourselves above average, at least on things we care about, which may help us feel good but doesn't help us improve.|93%|Svenson, 1981
The bias blind spot | We see bias in other people but think we are fair. | Emily Pronin and her colleagues asked people how susceptible they were to a list of biases, then how susceptible the average American was. People rated themselves as less biased than others. In another part of the study, they were told about a bias, such as the tendency to take credit for good outcomes, and many still felt they were exempt. It's an uncomfortable finding, because knowing about the biases doesn't seem to protect us from them.||Pronin, Lin & Ross, 2002
Naive realism | We think we see the world as it is, so people who disagree must be uninformed or biased. | Lee Ross and Andrew Ward described a common feeling that we see the world as it is, that reasonable people see it the same way, and that people who disagree must be misinformed, lazy or biased. Everyone feels this way, which means two people can each think the other is the one with the filter. It's a key idea in conflict research. It suggests that when we disagree, a good first question is what the other person has seen or experienced that we haven't.||Ross & Ward, 1996
The illusory truth effect | Hearing a claim again and again makes it feel more true. | In 1977 Lynn Hasher, David Goldstein and Thomas Toppino had people rate how true a series of statements were, over several weeks. The statements they'd seen before were rated as more likely true than new ones, whether they were true or not. A 2015 study by Lisa Fazio and colleagues found it holds even when people actually know better. Hearing something again and again makes it easy to process, and ease can feel a lot like truth.||Hasher, Goldstein & Toppino, 1977; Fazio et al., 2015
The false consensus effect | We assume more people share our views and habits than really do. | In a 1977 study, Lee Ross, David Greene and Pamela House asked students whether they'd walk around campus for thirty minutes wearing a sandwich board that said "Eat at Joe's." Those who agreed thought most other students would agree too. Those who refused thought most would refuse. Each side saw its own choice as the common-sense one. We use our own views as a sample, and since we tend to know people who think like us, the sample is biased.||Ross, Greene & House, 1977
Pluralistic ignorance | Everyone privately doubts a norm but thinks everyone else accepts it. | In the 1990s Deborah Prentice and Dale Miller asked Princeton students how comfortable they felt about the heavy drinking on campus, and how comfortable they thought the average student was. Individually, they were less comfortable than they imagined everyone else to be. Each person assumed they were the odd one out, so nobody said anything, and the norm everyone privately doubted stayed in place. It can happen in classrooms, meetings and families.||Prentice & Miller, 1993
Social loafing | People often put in less effort when working as a group. | A French engineer, Max Ringelmann, noticed in the 1880s that people pulling on a rope pulled less hard per person as the group got bigger. In 1979 Bibb Latané and his colleagues tested it with shouting and clapping. They had people make as much noise as they could, either alone or in a group while blindfolded and wearing headphones. Each person was quieter in a group. When effort isn't visible, it's easy to let others carry it.||Latané, Williams & Harkins, 1979
Self-fulfilling prophecy | A false belief can cause the behavior that makes it come true. | The sociologist Robert Merton described in 1948 how a false belief can come true because of the way people respond to it. His example was a bank that was perfectly healthy until a rumor started that it was going to fail. Customers rushed to take out their money, and the bank did fail. The prediction caused the outcome. It works on a smaller scale too, when a teacher who expects less from a student gives them less chance to show what they can do.||Merton, 1948
Misattributing arousal | Racing hearts get explained away by whatever is nearby. | Donald Dutton and Arthur Aron's 1974 experiment is famous. Men crossing either a high, swaying suspension bridge or a low solid one were met by a female interviewer, who gave them her phone number. The men on the scary bridge were more likely to call, which the researchers suggested was because they mistook the excitement from the heights for attraction. Later studies have had mixed results. Still, the idea that our bodies' signals can be misread is well supported.||Dutton & Aron, 1974|mixed
The decoy effect | A third, worse option can change which of the first two you choose. | Imagine choosing between two options, each better in some way. Add a third that is clearly worse than one of them but not the other. In studies starting with Joel Huber, John Payne and Christopher Puto in 1982, people tended to pick the one that made the third look bad. The decoy didn't have to be chosen to do its job. The effect shows up in many tests but not all, and some later studies found it vanishes when the numbers are easy to compare directly.||Huber, Payne & Puto, 1982|mixed
Choice overload | Too many options can freeze us or leave us less satisfied. | In 2000 Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper set up a tasting table of jam in a grocery store. One day there were 24 kinds, another day 6. More people stopped at the large display, but 30% of the people at the small display bought a jar, compared with 3% at the large one. It became a classic. When researchers pooled many such studies in 2010, the average effect was close to zero, so it seems to happen in some situations and not others.|30% vs 3%|Iyengar & Lepper, 2000; Scheibehenne et al., 2010|mixed
The Pygmalion effect | Expect more from someone and they may deliver more. | In 1968 Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson told elementary school teachers that certain pupils had been identified by a test as likely to bloom intellectually that year. In fact, the pupils had been picked at random. At the end of the year, some of those children, mainly the youngest ones, had gained more on an IQ test. The study was criticized for its methods, but later research has found that teachers' expectations do have a small, real effect.||Rosenthal & Jacobson, 1968|mixed
Stereotype threat | Fear of confirming a negative stereotype can hurt performance. | In 1995 Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson gave Black and white students difficult verbal questions. When the test was described as measuring intellectual ability, Black students did worse than when it was described as a problem-solving exercise. Steele suggested that worry about confirming a negative stereotype can use up mental effort. Later attempts to repeat it have found weaker and less consistent results, so the size and conditions of the effect are still debated.||Steele & Aronson, 1995|mixed
Groupthink | Tight-knit groups can drift into agreeing too soon. | Irving Janis studied how smart, capable groups made terrible decisions, including the Kennedy administration's Bay of Pigs invasion of 1961. He argued that when a close group values agreement above all, it silences doubts, assumes it can't fail and ignores outside views. He named the pattern in 1972. Later research has found the model fits some famous failures but not all, and that careful, well-run groups can avoid it. Still, the habit of inviting dissent is wise.||Janis, 1972|mixed
Power posing | Standing in a confident pose will change your hormones and your behavior. | In 2010 Dana Carney, Amy Cuddy and Andy Yap reported that standing in an expansive "power pose" for two minutes raised testosterone, lowered cortisol and made people more willing to take risks. It spread widely, including in a popular TED talk. A larger replication in 2015 by Eva Ranehill and colleagues found no effect on hormones or on behavior. People did say they felt a bit more powerful. Carney later said she no longer believed the original effects were real.||Carney, Cuddy & Yap, 2010; Ranehill et al., 2015|contested
The facial feedback hypothesis | Does smiling make you happier? | The idea is that the muscles of your face can influence how you feel. In 1988 Fritz Strack, Leonard Martin and Sabine Stepper had people hold a pen in their teeth, which forces a smile, or with their lips, which blocks one, while rating cartoons. The smilers found them funnier. In 2016, seventeen labs tried to repeat the experiment and found no effect. Researchers still debate whether small effects exist under the right conditions.||Strack, Martin & Stepper, 1988; Wagenmakers et al., 2016|contested
The Mozart effect | Listening to Mozart will make you smarter. | In 1993 Frances Rauscher, Gordon Shaw and Katherine Ky reported that college students who listened to ten minutes of a Mozart sonata did slightly better on a spatial reasoning task. The press turned it into "Mozart makes you smarter," and one US state gave classical CDs to every newborn. The original effect was small and brief. A later review in 2010 concluded that any boost looks like a general effect of mood and alertness from music a person enjoys.||Rauscher, Shaw & Ky, 1993; Pietschnig et al., 2010|contested
Pareidolia | The brain finds faces and patterns in random shapes. | The human brain is tuned to spot faces, and it does so fast, even in clouds, electrical outlets and burned toast. In 1976 the Viking 1 orbiter photographed a mesa in the Cydonia region of Mars that looked like a human face, and it became a sensation. When NASA's later spacecraft took much sharper pictures in 1998 and 2001, the "face" was clearly an ordinary hill. Seeing faces everywhere is an easy mistake for a species that depends on faces.||NASA/JPL Cydonia images, 1976 and 2001
The lie-detection limit | People are hardly better than chance at spotting lies. | If you've ever thought you can tell when someone is lying, the research isn't encouraging. In 2006 Charles Bond and Bella DePaulo combined 206 studies and found that people correctly judged truth and lies about 54% of the time, only a little better than a coin flip. Police officers, judges and others who do this for a living did no better than students. The signals we think we see, like looking away, turn out not to be reliable.|54%|Bond & DePaulo, 2006
The truth default | We start by assuming people are telling the truth. | Tim Levine's research suggests that our tendency to believe others isn't a weakness so much as a reasonable way to live, since most people tell the truth most of the time. Checking everything would make social life exhausting. We normally switch from trust to doubt only when something strongly suggests we should. The downside is that a skilled liar can take advantage of our habit. Thoughtful skepticism means knowing which situations call for checking.||Levine, 2014
The end-of-history illusion | We know we changed a lot in the past, yet expect to stay about the same from now on. | In 2013 Jordi Quoidbach, Daniel Gilbert and Timothy Wilson surveyed about 19,000 people between 18 and 68. Some were asked how much they'd changed in the past ten years, and others how much they expected to change in the next ten. At every age, people said they'd changed a lot but expected to change little. Yet people ten years older reported more change than the younger ones predicted. We tend to treat who we are now as the finished version.|About 19,000|Quoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson, 2013
