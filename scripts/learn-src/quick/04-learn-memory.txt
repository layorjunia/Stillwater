The method of loci | Memorize a list by placing each item in a familiar place, then mentally walking through the place. | Greek and Roman speakers who had to deliver long speeches with no notes used this trick, and it's still the one memory champions rely on. You pick a place you know well, like your house, and place each thing to remember at a spot along a route through it, ideally as a vivid, odd image. To recall the list, you walk the route in your mind. In a 2017 study, people trained for six weeks went from remembering about 26 of 72 words to about 62.|26 to 62|Dresler et al., Neuron, 2017
Chunking | Grouping items into meaningful units lets you hold more in mind at once. | Working memory can only hold a few items at once, so the trick is to make each item bigger. A phone number grouped as 555-0142 is easier than seven loose digits. In a classic study, chess masters could recall the pieces on a board from a real game far better than beginners, but they were no better with pieces placed randomly. They weren't remembering more pieces. They were seeing familiar patterns, and each pattern counted as one item.||Miller, 1956; Chase & Simon, 1973
Dual coding | Words together with a picture give memory two routes in. | In 1971 the psychologist Allan Paivio proposed that we store words and pictures in two linked systems. If you learn something both ways, you've got two routes back to it. That's why it helps to sketch a diagram of what you've read, or to picture an idea, instead of only rereading the words. The picture doesn't need to be good. Making it forces you to think about what the thing means, which is half the benefit.||Paivio, 1971
Levels of processing | Thinking about meaning makes a stronger memory than noticing how something looks or sounds. | In 1975 Fergus Craik and Endel Tulving showed people words and asked one of three kinds of question: does it appear in capitals, does it rhyme with something, or does it fit in a sentence? Later, those who had thought about meaning remembered far more words than those who had only looked at the letters or the sound. The deeper you think about what something means, the better it sticks. Rereading is shallow, and explaining is deep.||Craik & Tulving, 1975
The self-reference effect | We remember what we link to ourselves better than what we link to others. | A 1977 study asked people to judge words in different ways. One question was "Does this word describe you?" The people who had asked themselves that question later remembered more of the words than those who had asked about the word's meaning, or whether it described someone else. Linking new information to your own life is a strong aid to memory. When you study, ask yourself how an idea connects to something you've lived or done.||Rogers, Kuiper & Kirker, 1977
Encoding specificity | A memory is easiest to retrieve when the cues at recall match those at learning. | Endel Tulving and Donald Thomson found in 1973 that memory works best when the situation at recall resembles the situation at learning. In their experiments, a cue word that was present when someone learned a word helped them recall it better than a strong cue they hadn't seen before. It suggests that you should practice under conditions like the test. Studying while sitting at a desk, with silence, may serve you better than studying lying on a couch if the exam is in a quiet room.||Tulving & Thomson, 1973
Context-dependent memory | Do you recall better in the place where you learned? | In 1975 Duncan Godden and Alan Baddeley had scuba divers learn lists of words either on a beach or underwater, then recall them either in the same place or in the other one. The divers did better when they were in the same setting where they'd learned. It sounds appealing, but later attempts to repeat it have found the effect is small and doesn't always show up. It's more reliable when the place actually shapes what you were thinking about at the time.||Godden & Baddeley, 1975; Murre, 2021|mixed
Retrieval-induced forgetting | Recalling one memory can make related ones harder to recall. | Practicing some of what you know can make you worse at related things you didn't practice. In a 1994 experiment, Michael Anderson and colleagues had people practice recalling some items from a category, like fruits. Later they were worse at remembering the other fruits than they were at items from categories they hadn't practiced at all. The idea is that when your mind picks one memory, it suppresses its competitors. It's a reason to review all the material, not just the parts you find easy.||Anderson, Bjork & Bjork, 1994
The misinformation effect | Information that arrives after an event can change how we remember it. | In 1974 Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed people films of car accidents. They asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed into" each other, or "hit" each other. The "smashed" group gave higher speeds, an average of 40.8 miles per hour versus 34.0. A week later, they were also more likely to say they'd seen broken glass, though there wasn't any. A single word in a question changed what they remembered.|40.8 vs 34.0 mph|Loftus & Palmer, 1974
Implanted false memories | A detailed memory of something that never happened can be created by suggestion. | In 1995 Elizabeth Loftus and Jacqueline Pickrell asked adults to read four short stories about their own childhoods, written with help from their relatives. Three were true, and one was invented: that they'd been lost in a shopping mall as a child. About a quarter of the participants came to remember the made-up event, and some added details of their own. Vivid, detailed memories feel reliable, but a detailed memory can still be wrong.|About 1 in 4|Loftus & Pickrell, 1995
Flashbulb memories | Memories of shocking events feel vivid and exact but change like others. | When something shocking happens, we feel we'll never forget exactly where we were. In 1986, the day after the Challenger explosion, Ulric Neisser and Nicole Harsch asked students to write down how they'd heard. Two and a half years later, they asked again. About a quarter of the later accounts were wrong in nearly every detail, and the students were still very confident. The memory felt vivid, but the details had been rebuilt over time.||Neisser & Harsch, 1992
Source amnesia | We remember a fact but forget where we learned it. | You can remember a fact and forget where you learned it. Daniel Schacter and colleagues documented it in the 1980s, in patients and in healthy people. It's why a rumor you heard once, from someone you wouldn't have trusted, can seem like common knowledge months later. The content survives, but the tag that says "unreliable source" doesn't. If something seems like it's just something "everyone knows," it's worth asking where you first heard it.||Schacter, Harbluk & McLachlan, 1984
Cryptomnesia | Mistaking something you heard for an idea of your own. | Sometimes you can come up with an idea or a tune that you think is new, and it's something you heard before. In 1976 a US court decided that George Harrison had "subconsciously" copied the melody of the Chiffons' "He's So Fine" when he wrote "My Sweet Lord." The judge didn't think he'd done it on purpose. A stored tune had simply come back to him without the label. Writers and musicians sometimes worry about it, and it's a reason to keep track of where your ideas come from.||Bright Tunes Music v. Harrisongs Music, 1976
The tip of the tongue | You know the word, but it will not come. | You know the word, you can almost taste it, and it won't come. In 1966 Roger Brown and David McNeill studied the state. People in it can often tell you the first letter and how many syllables the word has, showing that memory stores words in pieces. Pushing harder often makes it worse. The usual advice is to move on and let it come back by itself, which it often does a minute later in the shower.||Brown & McNeill, 1966
Prospective memory | Remembering to do something later is a separate challenge. | Remembering to do something in the future, like taking medicine or sending a message, is a different task from remembering the past. It usually fails because there was no cue at the moment you needed it. The solution is to attach the intention to something you'll definitely encounter, such as "when I pour my morning coffee, I'll take the pill." Psychologists call these implementation intentions, and they work much better than just resolving to remember.||Einstein & McDaniel, 1990
Memory consolidation | New memories take hours and days to stabilize. | A new memory is fragile at first and takes hours and days to settle. In 1900 Georg Müller and Alfons Pilzecker showed it with a simple experiment. People who learned a list of nonsense syllables and then immediately learned a second list remembered the first one worse than people who got a rest in between. Sleep is one of the periods when memories seem to firm up, which is a good reason not to cram all night.||Müller & Pilzecker, 1900
Reconsolidation | A recalled memory can become briefly changeable before it is stored again. | The old picture was that once a memory is stored, it stays fixed. In 2000 Karim Nader, Glenn Schafe and Joseph LeDoux gave rats a fear memory, then reminded them of it and blocked protein production in the brain. The memory was weakened, as if it had to be rebuilt each time it was recalled. It suggested that recalling a memory opens a window when it can be changed. Scientists are still working out how far this applies to people.||Nader, Schafe & LeDoux, 2000|mixed
Patient H.M. | The man who could not form new memories, yet could learn new skills. | In 1953 a surgeon removed parts of Henry Molaison's brain, including most of the hippocampus on both sides, to treat his severe epilepsy. The seizures eased, but he could no longer form new memories of facts or events. He met his doctors again and again as if for the first time. Yet he could learn a new skill, like tracing a star in a mirror, and he improved day by day without remembering practicing it. It showed that memory has more than one system.|1953|Scoville & Milner, 1957
Declarative and procedural memory | Knowing that is different from knowing how. | You can know that something is the case, like the capital of France, and you can know how to do something, like ride a bicycle. They seem to depend on different brain systems. You might be able to ride perfectly without being able to explain how you balance. Or you might know the steps of a skill and be unable to do it. People with amnesia often keep their skills while losing their memory of learning them.||Squire, 1987
Episodic and semantic memory | Memory for events in your life versus memory for facts about the world. | In 1972 Endel Tulving suggested that we have two kinds of long-term memory for information. Episodic memory holds events from your own life, such as your last birthday party, with the feeling of being there. Semantic memory holds facts about the world, such as that Paris is the capital of France, without remembering when you learned them. In some forms of amnesia one is damaged more than the other.||Tulving, 1972
The working memory model | Short-term thinking uses several parts, not one store. | In 1974 Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch proposed that thinking in the moment uses several parts. There's a phonological loop for sounds and words, a visuospatial sketchpad for images and locations, and a central executive that directs attention. The model explains why you can listen to music while reading a map but find it hard to read an email while someone is talking to you. Tasks using the same part compete, and tasks using different parts don't.||Baddeley & Hitch, 1974
Cognitive load theory | Working memory is small, so instruction should not waste it. | John Sweller suggested in the 1980s that because working memory is so small, teaching should avoid wasting it. Making someone hold too much at once leads to confusion and poor learning. One common problem is split attention, such as a diagram on one page and an explanation three pages later, so the learner has to keep flipping back. Putting the explanation right beside the picture lets the learner use their limited memory for learning.||Sweller, 1988
The worked-example effect | Novices learn more from studying solved problems than from struggling to solve them. | If you're new to a subject, studying solved problems can teach you more than trying to solve them yourself. John Sweller and Graham Cooper showed it in algebra in 1985. Seeing every step frees your mind from searching for a method, and lets you notice the pattern. As you get better, you should switch to solving problems on your own. The best sequence usually starts with an example and moves gradually toward independence.||Sweller & Cooper, 1985
The expertise reversal effect | What helps beginners can slow experts down. | Something that helps a beginner can get in the way of an expert. Step-by-step guidance with detailed explanations supports a novice, but for someone who already knows the material it's redundant and can slow them down. Slava Kalyuga and colleagues described the effect in 2003. It's a reminder that good teaching adapts to the learner. The same worksheet that rescues one student bores another, so it's worth checking where you stand.||Kalyuga et al., 2003
The zone of proximal development | The gap between what you can do alone and what you can do with help. | The Russian psychologist Lev Vygotsky, writing in the 1930s, described the distance between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with help. He thought the best teaching works in that gap. If a task is too easy, nothing is learned. If it's too hard, the learner gives up. In between, with a little guidance that is gradually withdrawn, is where growth happens.||Vygotsky, 1930s
Scaffolding | Temporary support that lets a learner do more than they could alone. | A scaffold on a building holds things up until the structure can stand without it. In 1976 David Wood, Jerome Bruner and Gail Ross used the word for the support a tutor gives a child. The tutor does the parts the child can't yet manage, such as holding the pieces in place, and hands over more and more as the child improves. Good scaffolding is meant to be temporary. If it never comes down, the learner never learns to stand.||Wood, Bruner & Ross, 1976
Mastery learning | Master each step before moving on. | Benjamin Bloom suggested in 1968 that students should master each step before moving to the next, with extra help and time if they need it, rather than moving on with gaps. In 1984 he reported a "two sigma" finding: students tutored one-on-one with mastery methods did about two standard deviations better than those in a regular class. Later studies have found smaller gains, but the effect is real, and it's why practicing basics until they're solid pays off.||Bloom, 1968, 1984|mixed
The Leitner box | A paper system for spaced flashcards. | In 1972 the German science writer Sebastian Leitner described a simple way to schedule flashcards using a few boxes. All new cards start in box one. When you get one right, it moves up to the next box, which you review less often. When you get one wrong, it goes back to box one. The cards you know well get less of your time, and the ones you struggle with come back more. Many flashcard apps use the same idea.||Leitner, 1972
Writing by hand or typing? | Does taking notes by hand beat a laptop? | In 2014 Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer found that students who took notes by hand did better on conceptual questions than those who typed. Their explanation was that typists copy more word for word, while writers have to summarize. Later attempts to repeat the study found smaller differences, or none. The result may depend on how you take notes. Typing and thinking about the meaning probably beats handwriting and copying.||Mueller & Oppenheimer, 2014; Morehead et al., 2019|mixed
Speed reading | Reading much faster than normal costs you understanding. | Courses promise to teach you to read at 1,000 words a minute or more with full understanding. In 2016 a team of reading researchers led by Keith Rayner reviewed the evidence and concluded that it doesn't hold up. There's a trade-off between speed and comprehension. You can skim, and skimming is useful for finding the main points. But it's a different task from reading, and you miss details. A normal speed for adults is about 200 to 300 words a minute.||Rayner et al., 2016
The production effect | Reading aloud helps you remember it. | In 2010 Colin MacLeod and his colleagues found that people remembered words better when they read them aloud than when they read them silently. Hearing your own voice adds another feature to the memory, something distinctive about that item. It works best for the things you want to remember, so reading key points aloud, even quietly to yourself, can help. It's an easy trick to try when you're studying alone.||MacLeod et al., 2010
The Pomodoro technique | Work in short bursts of 25 minutes with short breaks. | In the late 1980s Francesco Cirillo, then a university student, used a tomato-shaped kitchen timer to break his work into 25-minute blocks with short breaks. The technique, named for the Italian word for tomato, gives a clear start and end to each session and can make it easier to begin. It may help some people focus. But there's little controlled research to show that 25 minutes is the right length, so adjust it to what works for you.||Cirillo, late 1980s|mixed
Transfer of learning | Skills often do not carry over to different tasks. | Does learning one skill help with another? In 1901 Edward Thorndike and Robert Woodworth tested it and found that practicing one kind of judgment, such as estimating the area of rectangles, did little for others. They suggested that transfer happens mostly when two tasks share the same elements. It's a sober point for anyone hoping a hobby, such as chess or Latin, will make them smarter in general. Skills usually carry over only to similar tasks.||Thorndike & Woodworth, 1901
Overlearning | Extra practice right after you master something adds little lasting memory. | It seems sensible to keep practicing after you've got something right, to be sure. In a 2005 study by Doug Rohrer and colleagues, students learned geography facts and then either stopped or kept drilling the same ones in the same session. A week later, the extra drilling made no difference to how much they remembered. The better use of time is to come back to the material later, after some forgetting has set in, and practice it again.||Rohrer et al., 2005
The keyword method | Link a foreign word to an English word it sounds like, then picture the two together. | To learn a foreign word, find an English word that sounds like it, then make a picture that connects the two. For the Spanish word "pato," meaning duck, you might imagine a duck wearing a pot on its head. Richard Atkinson and his colleagues tested the method in the 1970s and found that students using it learned vocabulary faster at first. It works best for concrete words, and it's less helpful for abstract ones.||Atkinson & Raugh, 1975
Memory champions have ordinary brains | The best memorizers were not born with special brains. | In 2003 Eleanor Maguire and her colleagues scanned the brains of people who rank among the best memorizers in the world. They found no unusual structure. What distinguished them was how they remembered: almost all used the method of loci, imagining a familiar journey and placing items along it. In other words, their results came from technique and practice, not from some special gift.||Maguire et al., 2003
Interference | Old and new memories get in each other's way. | Forgetting isn't always fading. Often one memory gets in the way of another. In 1924 John Jenkins and Karl Dallenbach had two students learn lists of nonsense syllables and then either sleep or stay awake. After the same number of hours, they remembered more after sleep. When you're asleep, less new material comes in to interfere. Old things can disturb new ones, and new things can disturb old ones, and both happen all the time.||Jenkins & Dallenbach, 1924
Savings in relearning | You may recall nothing, yet relearn it faster than before. | Hermann Ebbinghaus, who started the scientific study of memory in the 1880s, noticed something. A list of syllables he could no longer recall at all still took him fewer repetitions to learn a second time. He called the difference "savings." The memory had faded, but not vanished. It's encouraging: if you've studied something before, even years ago, you'll relearn it faster than someone starting from scratch.||Ebbinghaus, 1885
Attention is a spotlight | You can focus on one stream and miss the others. | In 1953 Colin Cherry studied how people follow one conversation in a noisy room, which became known as the cocktail-party effect. You can concentrate on one speaker and tune out the others. Yet if someone across the room says your name, you often notice. So your attention filters, but not completely. Some of what you're ignoring is still being processed, which is one reason it's hard to concentrate in a busy place.||Cherry, 1953
Inattentional blindness | We can fail to see something obvious when we are focusing on something else. | In the famous invisible gorilla study, people counting basketball passes often failed to see a person in a gorilla suit walk through the scene. A similar thing was found in medicine. In 2013 researchers asked radiologists to look for lung nodules in a set of scans. In the last scan, they'd put a picture of a gorilla about 48 times the size of a typical nodule. Most of the radiologists, 83 percent, didn't see it.||Simons & Chabris, 1999; Drew et al., 2013
Change blindness | We miss big changes to a scene when they happen during a blink or a cut. | We tend to think we'd notice a big change in our surroundings. In a 1998 field study by Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin, an experimenter stopped a pedestrian and asked for directions. While they talked, two men carrying a door walked between them, and the experimenter was swapped with a different person. About half of the pedestrians didn't notice that the person they were talking to had changed.|About 50%|Simons & Levin, 1998
The attentional blink | A second target is missed if it comes about half a second after the first. | If you're watching a fast stream of images and looking for two particular targets, you'll often notice the first but miss the second when it comes within about half a second. In 1992 Jane Raymond, Kimron Shapiro and Karen Arnell described the effect. Processing the first target seems to use up your attention for a moment. It's a fine illustration of how limited our attention is, even when we're alert.|200 to 500 ms|Raymond, Shapiro & Arnell, 1992
Task switching has a cost | Switching between tasks slows you and adds errors. | People often believe they can do two things at once. Mostly, they're switching rapidly between them, and every switch costs something. In lab experiments, people were slower and made more mistakes on the first trial after a change of task, even when they knew the switch was coming. Doing one task at a time and finishing it generally takes less total time than bouncing between several.||Monsell, 2003
Mind wandering | Our minds drift about half the time, and usually not happily. | In 2010 Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert built an app that pinged thousands of people at random moments and asked what they were doing and thinking about. People's minds were wandering in 46.9% of the samples, whatever the activity. They also reported being less happy when their minds wandered than when they were focused on what they were doing. Even when the daydream was pleasant, it didn't make people happier.|46.9%|Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010
Flow | A state of full absorption when challenge matches skill. | The psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi studied people who got completely absorbed in activities, such as rock climbers, chess players and surgeons. He called the state flow. It tends to appear when a task is challenging enough to need all your attention but not so hard that it overwhelms you, and when the goals are clear and you get quick feedback. Time seems to disappear. It's one reason that matching difficulty to your skill makes work more enjoyable.||Csikszentmihalyi, 1990
The Yerkes-Dodson law | Performance rises with arousal up to a point, then falls. | In 1908 Robert Yerkes and John Dodson trained mice to avoid shocks and found that moderate shocks led to the best learning. Too weak or too strong did worse. Psychologists extended the idea into a rule that performance rises with arousal up to a point and then falls. Later work suggests the best level depends on the task: harder tasks do best with lower arousal. It's a rough guide, not a precise law.||Yerkes & Dodson, 1908|mixed
Sleep and the next day's learning | Sleep before learning prepares the brain to absorb, and sleep after learning helps keep it. | In 2007 Seung-Schik Yoo, Matthew Walker and colleagues kept some volunteers awake all night and let others sleep, then showed everyone pictures to remember. The next day, the people who'd been up all night remembered about 40 percent fewer of the pictures. Scans showed less activity in the hippocampus, a region important for forming memories. Sleep matters for learning before you study as well as after.|About 40% fewer|Yoo et al., 2007
Naps help | A short sleep in the afternoon can restore learning. | In 2003 Sara Mednick and her colleagues tested people on a visual task that gets better with practice. Those who had a nap of 60 to 90 minutes improved as much as people who'd had a full night's sleep. Those who didn't nap got worse over the day. A well-timed nap can restore some of your ability to learn. A short one of about 20 minutes may be better for just feeling alert.||Mednick et al., 2003
The Baby Einstein question | Do educational videos make babies smarter? | Videos for babies were sold in the 2000s as a way to boost their learning. In 2007 Frederick Zimmerman and colleagues surveyed parents of infants aged 8 to 16 months. For each daily hour of baby videos, the children knew a few fewer words than babies who didn't watch them. Researchers can't be sure the videos caused it. What does help babies learn words is talking, reading and playing with the people around them.||Zimmerman, Christakis & Meltzoff, 2007|mixed
Retrieval cues | A cue that was linked to the memory when it was formed can bring it back. | Endel Tulving and Zena Pearlstone showed in 1966 that a memory can seem lost until the right cue appears. People who couldn't recall a list of words suddenly could when they were given category names as hints. The memory was there but they couldn't reach it. It's a reason to leave yourself cues when you study, such as a keyword or a note in the margin. A smell or a song can bring back a scene because it was part of the original memory.||Tulving & Pearlstone, 1966
Overconfidence in learning | After reading something twice, we feel we know it. | In a well-known 2006 study, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students read a passage and then either read it again or take a test on it. The students who had reread felt more prepared. A week later, the ones who'd been tested remembered far more. Rereading creates a feeling of familiarity, which is easy to mistake for knowledge. Testing yourself feels harder, and that's partly why it works.||Roediger & Karpicke, 2006
Learning from mistakes | Getting something wrong and then seeing the right answer can help. | You might think that guessing wrong would plant the wrong answer in your mind. In a 2009 study, Nate Kornell, Matthew Hays and Robert Bjork had people try to guess answers before being shown the correct one. Even when the guesses were wrong, they learned the right answer better than people who just read it. A wrong guess followed by quick feedback seems to prepare the mind to take in the answer.||Kornell, Hays & Bjork, 2009
The hypercorrection effect | The more sure you were of a wrong answer, the better you remember the correction. | Surprisingly, the more confident you are in a wrong answer, the more likely you are to remember the correction. In 2001 Brady Butterfield and Janet Metcalfe found that high-confidence mistakes were corrected more often than low-confidence ones. The surprise of learning you were wrong when you were sure seems to grab your attention. So there's no need to feel embarrassed about being wrong. A confident mistake is one of the best ways to learn.||Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2001
