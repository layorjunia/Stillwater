Cell theory | All living things are made of cells, and all cells come from other cells. | In 1838 the botanist Matthias Schleiden suggested that all plants are made of cells, and in 1839 Theodor Schwann said the same of animals. In 1855 Rudolf Virchow added the third part, that every cell comes from another cell. Before that, many people believed living things could arise from nonliving matter. A human body has around 30 trillion cells, and they all trace back to a single fertilized egg.||Schleiden, 1838; Schwann, 1839; Virchow, 1855
Mitochondria | The parts of the cell that make most of its usable energy. | Mitochondria are the small structures in your cells that turn food into usable energy. They have their own little ring of DNA, and you inherit them from your mother. In 1967 the biologist Lynn Margulis argued that they began as free-living bacteria that were taken in by a larger cell, and the two ended up living together. Most biologists now accept this idea, called endosymbiosis, which also applies to plant chloroplasts.||Margulis (Sagan), 1967
Photosynthesis | Plants and algae use sunlight to turn carbon dioxide and water into sugar and oxygen. | Plants, algae and some bacteria capture sunlight and use its energy to turn carbon dioxide and water into sugar, giving off oxygen as a byproduct. The pigment chlorophyll absorbs mostly red and blue light and reflects green, so leaves look green. Tiny algae in the oceans produce roughly half of the oxygen we breathe, which means a lot of every breath comes from the sea.||Campbell Biology
Cellular respiration | Cells burn sugar with oxygen to make ATP, their energy currency. | Your cells burn sugar with oxygen to make ATP, a molecule that works as the cell's energy currency. One molecule of glucose can yield about 30 ATP. The body uses it so quickly that it recycles about its own weight in ATP each day, rebuilding it again and again. It's the reason you need to keep breathing and eating: the supply of ATP in your cells lasts only seconds.||Campbell Biology
The double helix | DNA is two strands twisted around each other, joined by paired bases. | In 1953 James Watson and Francis Crick published their model of DNA's structure in a one-page paper in Nature: two strands twisted around each other like a spiral staircase. It was built using X-ray images made by Rosalind Franklin and her student Raymond Gosling, and measurements by Maurice Wilkins. Watson, Crick and Wilkins won the Nobel Prize in 1962. Franklin had died in 1958, and the prize isn't given after death.|1953|Watson & Crick, 1953
Base pairing | A always pairs with T, and G with C. | DNA is built from four letters, A, T, G and C. In 1950 Erwin Chargaff discovered that in any organism's DNA, the amount of A matches the amount of T, and the amount of G matches C. Watson and Crick used this to see that the letters pair up, A with T and G with C, across the two strands. That means each strand holds the information to rebuild the other, which is how DNA can copy itself.||Chargaff, 1950
We are all mutants | Each new baby has about 60 new changes in their DNA that neither parent had. | Every baby is born with some brand-new changes in their DNA that neither parent had. A study in Iceland in 2012 counted about 60 new changes per person on average. Most land in parts of the DNA that don't matter. A few may be harmful, and a very few help. Over many generations, these random changes are the raw material that natural selection works on.|About 60|Kong et al., 2012
Mendel's peas | An Augustinian monk found the rules of inheritance by crossing garden peas. | Gregor Mendel was an Augustinian monk in what is now the Czech Republic. In the 1850s and 60s, he crossed thousands of pea plants and carefully counted the traits, such as tall versus short, in the offspring. He found consistent ratios, often about 3 to 1, and concluded that traits are passed on in distinct units, which we now call genes. His 1866 paper was ignored until 1900, years after he died.||Mendel, 1866
The genetic code | Three-letter DNA words (codons) spell out the 20 amino acids that make proteins. | DNA is read in words of three letters, called codons. There are 64 possible codons, and they spell out the 20 amino acids that are strung together to make proteins, plus signals for start and stop. Since there are more codons than amino acids, several codons can mean the same thing. The code is almost the same in every living thing, from bacteria to humans, which is strong evidence that we share a common ancestor.|64 codons|Nirenberg & Khorana, 1960s
CRISPR | A tool that edits DNA at a chosen spot, adapted from a bacterial defense. | Bacteria have an immune system that cuts up the DNA of invading viruses, called CRISPR. In 2012 Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier showed that this system could be reprogrammed to cut DNA at any chosen spot, making gene editing much easier and cheaper. They won the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. In late 2023, the UK and the US approved the first medicine based on CRISPR, for sickle cell disease.|2012|Jinek et al., 2012
Epigenetics | Chemical tags on DNA change which genes are active without changing the letters. | Your cells all carry the same DNA, yet a liver cell is very different from a neuron. The difference lies in which genes are switched on, and chemical tags on the DNA and its packaging help control that. Diet, stress and age can alter them. Some people claim these changes can be inherited by grandchildren. Evidence for that in humans is limited and debated, though a few animal studies support it.||Standard genetics text|mixed
Convergent evolution | Unrelated species evolve similar solutions to similar problems. | Sometimes unrelated species end up with similar features because they face similar challenges. Eyes have evolved independently dozens of times. Dolphins, which are mammals, and sharks, which are fish, have similar streamlined bodies. Birds, bats and insects all developed wings separately. It's strong evidence that natural selection is a powerful force, and that certain solutions work again and again.||Standard evolution text
Homology | The same bones form a human arm, a bat wing and a whale flipper. | Look at the skeleton inside a human arm, a bat's wing and a whale's flipper. The same bones, a single upper bone, two lower bones, wrist bones and fingers, appear in all three, though used for very different purposes. Charles Darwin saw this as strong evidence of common descent: different jobs, same blueprint, modified over time. It's a bit like the same recipe adapted for different meals.||Darwin, On the Origin of Species (1859)
Evolution is not progress | Natural selection fits a species to its local conditions, not toward a goal. | It's common to imagine evolution as a ladder, with humans at the top. But natural selection only fits creatures to their local conditions. It has no goal. Cave fish living in total darkness have lost their eyes, and tapeworms have lost their guts, and both are perfectly suited to their lives. The biologist Stephen Jay Gould argued that life is better pictured as a bush with many branches.||Gould, Full House (1996)
Antibiotic resistance | Bacteria evolve to survive the drugs we use, in a few years. | Bacteria reproduce so fast that they can evolve resistance to drugs within years. Each time antibiotics are used, they kill the vulnerable bacteria and leave the resistant ones to multiply. A 2022 study estimated that drug-resistant bacterial infections directly caused 1.27 million deaths worldwide in 2019. Using antibiotics only when needed, and completing the course, slows the process.|1.27 million|Murray et al., Lancet, 2022
Darwin's finches | Beak sizes of finches on Galápagos changed within a few years of a drought. | On the Galápagos Islands, Peter and Rosemary Grant spent four decades measuring finches. In 1977 a severe drought killed most of the small, soft seeds, leaving mainly big, hard ones. Birds with larger beaks could crack them and survived better. The next generation had measurably larger beaks. It showed natural selection at work within a single human lifetime, not just over millions of years.|1977|Grant & Grant, 2002
Lenski's long experiment | Twelve E. coli populations have been evolving in a lab since 1988. | In 1988 the biologist Richard Lenski started twelve separate populations of E. coli bacteria in flasks in his lab. Every day since, a sample has been moved to fresh food. After more than 30,000 generations, one population suddenly evolved the ability to feed on citrate, which E. coli normally can't do. The experiment, still running, shows evolution as it happens, and can be replayed with frozen samples.|Since 1988|Blount, Borland & Lenski, 2008
Our common ancestor | All living things on Earth descend from one population that lived 3.5 to 4 billion years ago. | All the life on Earth that we know of, from bacteria to blue whales, shares DNA, the same genetic code and similar basic cell machinery. That points to descent from a single ancestral population that lived somewhere between 3.5 and 4 billion years ago. A formal statistical test in 2010 found that this common-ancestry idea fits the data better than the alternatives. The differences between species are just branches of one tree.||Theobald, Nature, 2010
Humans and chimps | We share about 98.8% of our DNA with chimpanzees in the parts that line up. | When scientists compared our genome with the chimpanzee's in 2005, they found that, where the sequences line up, about 98.8 percent of the letters are the same. That figure counts single-letter differences. When you include stretches that are inserted or missing, the difference is larger, about 4 to 5 percent. Either way, our closest living relatives are chimpanzees and bonobos.|98.8%|Chimpanzee Genome Consortium, 2005
Neanderthal DNA | Most people with ancestry from outside Africa carry 1 to 2% Neanderthal DNA. | In 2010 the Swedish geneticist Svante Pääbo and his team published a draft of the Neanderthal genome, pieced together from tiny fragments of ancient bone. It showed that people whose ancestors lived outside Africa carry about 1 to 2 percent Neanderthal DNA. That means our ancestors met Neanderthals and had children with them. Pääbo won the 2022 Nobel Prize in Medicine for this work.|1-2%|Green et al., 2010
Where our species began | Modern humans emerged in Africa at least 300,000 years ago. | For a long time, we thought modern humans emerged about 200,000 years ago. In 2017 researchers reported fossils from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, which they dated to roughly 300,000 years ago, with features of both modern humans and earlier forms. Our species spread through Africa and left in waves, and the main migration out was about 60,000 years ago.|300,000 years|Hublin et al., 2017
Viruses and bacteria | Bacteria are living cells that antibiotics can kill; viruses are not cells and need a host to reproduce. | Bacteria are living single-celled organisms, and many are harmless or helpful, while a few cause disease. Viruses are much smaller and aren't cells at all. They're packets of genetic material that can only reproduce by hijacking a host's cells. Antibiotics target bacteria, so they do nothing against colds or the flu, which are caused by viruses. For viruses, we use vaccines and, in some cases, antiviral drugs.||Standard microbiology text
Penicillin | A mold on a forgotten dish led to the first antibiotic. | In 1928 the Scottish doctor Alexander Fleming came back from vacation to find that a mold had contaminated one of his bacteria dishes, and the bacteria around it had died. He'd noticed the effect, but turning it into a drug took a decade of work by Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and their team at Oxford, who made it in the early 1940s. The three shared the 1945 Nobel Prize in Medicine.|1928|Fleming, 1929; Florey & Chain
Keystone species | One species that holds a whole community together. | In 1966 the ecologist Robert Paine experimented on the rocky coast of Washington state by removing all the starfish from a patch of shore. Without them, mussels took over and crowded out other species, and the variety of life dropped sharply. A single predator turned out to be holding the whole community in balance. He called such species keystone species, like the stone at the top of an arch.||Paine, 1966
Wolves in Yellowstone | Bringing back wolves in 1995 changed elk behavior and may have helped plants and rivers. | Wolves were removed from Yellowstone National Park by the 1920s and brought back in 1995. Some of the effects are well documented, such as fewer elk in some areas, and the elk behaving more warily. A popular story says that the wolves also changed the courses of the rivers, by letting trees grow back along the banks. That part is debated, since the effects on plants vary and other factors matter.||Ripple & Beschta, 2012|mixed
The ten percent rule | Only about a tenth of the energy passes from one level of a food chain to the next. | Energy is lost at each step up a food chain. A plant captures sunlight, a rabbit eats the plant, and a fox eats the rabbit. At each step only about a tenth of the energy gets passed on, since most is used for living and moving, and given off as heat. That's why there are far fewer lions than zebras, and why food chains rarely have more than four or five links. The real figure varies between about 5 and 20 percent.|About 10%|Lindeman, 1942
The Great Dying | The end-Permian extinction killed most species on Earth. | About 252 million years ago, the biggest mass extinction in Earth's history happened at the end of the Permian period. Massive volcanic eruptions in what's now Siberia pumped out huge amounts of carbon dioxide and other gases, and changed the climate and the chemistry of the oceans. An estimated 80 to 96 percent of marine species, and about 70 percent of land vertebrates, died. It took millions of years for life to recover.|252M years ago|Standard paleontology text
The largest organism | A honey fungus in Oregon covers about 2,400 acres. | The largest known living organism by area is a honey fungus, Armillaria ostoyae, in the Malheur National Forest in Oregon. Most of it is underground, spreading through the soil and the roots of trees as thin threads. It covers about 2,400 acres, an area as big as well over a thousand football fields. It may be thousands of years old, and the part that you see is just a few mushrooms in the fall.|~2,400 acres|US Forest Service
The wood wide web | Do trees share food and warnings through underground fungi? | Popular books have described forests as having an underground "wood wide web" in which trees share food and send warnings through fungi connected to their roots. It's a beautiful idea. But in 2023 a team led by Justine Karst reviewed the research and found that the evidence for forest-wide sharing of nutrients and signals is far weaker than the stories suggest. Fungi do connect to roots, but what they do with the connection is less clear.||Karst et al., 2023|contested
Phototropism | Plants bend toward light. | Have you noticed a houseplant bending toward a window? That's phototropism. In 1880 Charles Darwin and his son Francis experimented with grass seedlings, covering different parts of them, and found that the tip senses the light and sends a signal to the part below, which bends. The signal turned out to be a plant hormone, which was later named auxin. It was one of the first plant hormones to be identified.|1880|Darwin & Darwin, 1880
The waggle dance | A honeybee that finds food can tell the others its direction and distance. | When a honeybee finds a good source of flowers, it returns to the hive and dances on the honeycomb. The Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch decoded the dance in the 1940s. The angle of the straight run shows the direction of the food compared with the sun, and how long it lasts shows the distance. He won the 1973 Nobel Prize for it, one of the few Nobels in the study of animal behavior.||von Frisch, 1967
The octopus | An octopus has three hearts, blue blood and about two-thirds of its neurons in its arms. | An octopus has three hearts: two pump blood through the gills and one through the body. Its blood is blue, because it carries oxygen with copper instead of iron. About two-thirds of its neurons are in its arms, and each can taste, touch and even make simple decisions on its own. Octopuses have been seen solving puzzles, opening jars and escaping from tanks.|3 hearts|Hochner, 2012
Tardigrades | Microscopic "water bears" can survive extreme conditions. | Tardigrades are microscopic animals, less than a millimeter long, nicknamed water bears. They can survive being dried out for years, frozen near absolute zero, heated above boiling and exposed to high radiation. In 2007 a European Space Agency experiment exposed them to the vacuum and solar radiation of space for ten days. Some survived and even laid eggs after returning to Earth.||Jönsson et al., 2008
Naked mole rats | Small underground rodents that live about 30 years and rarely get cancer. | Naked mole rats are small, hairless rodents that live underground in colonies in East Africa. They live about 30 years, far longer than mice of similar size, which live about three. They also rarely get cancer. One reason may be that their cells make an unusually thick, sugary substance that stops cells from crowding together and forming tumors. Researchers study them for clues about aging and cancer.|About 30 years|Buffenstein, 2008
The Greenland shark | The longest-lived vertebrate known, living for centuries. | The Greenland shark is the longest-lived vertebrate we know of. In 2016 a team in Denmark used radiocarbon dating on proteins in the lens of the eyes of 28 sharks and estimated that the biggest was about 392 years old, give or take 120 years. These sharks grow very slowly, only about a centimeter a year, and are thought to reach maturity at about 150 years of age.|About 400 years|Nielsen et al., 2016
The immortal jellyfish | Turritopsis dohrnii can revert to an earlier stage of life. | Turritopsis dohrnii is a tiny jellyfish that, when it's injured or stressed, can turn back into an earlier stage of life. The adult reverts to a polyp, a kind of larval form, and starts its life cycle again. In theory, it could do it indefinitely. In practice, most of them still get eaten or die of disease, but they are a subject of intense study in aging research.||Piraino et al., 1996
Crows remember faces | Crows can recognize a person who threatened them, and tell others. | In a 2010 study, John Marzluff and colleagues at the University of Washington wore a rubber mask while they trapped and banded wild crows. For years afterward, crows scolded and swooped at anyone wearing the same mask, though not at people in other masks. Even young crows that had never seen the trapping joined in, which means that crows can pass on information about dangerous people.||Marzluff et al., 2010
Birds are dinosaurs | Birds are the living descendants of feathered theropod dinosaurs. | Birds are not just related to dinosaurs, they are dinosaurs: the living descendants of a group of feathered, two-legged dinosaurs called theropods. Since the 1990s, spectacular fossils in China have shown many dinosaurs with feathers of various kinds. In that sense, a sparrow is a small dinosaur, in the same way that you are a mammal.||Xu et al., 2014
Blue whales | The largest animal that has ever lived, up to 30 meters long. | The blue whale is the largest animal that has ever lived, bigger than any dinosaur. It can grow up to 30 meters long, and weigh around 150 tons. It's astonishing that such an enormous animal feeds mainly on tiny shrimp-like creatures called krill, and it can eat up to about four tons of them a day during the feeding season.|Up to 30 m|NOAA Fisheries
The dog's long history | Dogs were the first domesticated animal, at least 15,000 years ago. | Dogs were the first animal people domesticated, long before farming began. Genetic studies suggest that their ancestors split from wolves some time between 15,000 and 40,000 years ago, though experts debate exactly when. Dogs have evolved a talent that wolves, and even chimpanzees, lack: the ability to read human gestures such as pointing and to look to our faces for guidance.||Bergström et al., 2020
Earth's layers | A thin crust, a thick mantle, a liquid outer core and a solid inner core. | Earth has four main layers. There's the thin outer crust that we live on, a thick, slowly flowing mantle, a liquid outer core of molten iron and nickel, and a solid inner core. The Danish seismologist Inge Lehmann discovered the inner core in 1936, by noticing that some earthquake waves bounced off something solid deep inside the planet. It's about as hot as the surface of the Sun.||Lehmann, 1936
Continental drift | Wegener proposed in 1912 that the continents move. | In 1912 the German scientist Alfred Wegener proposed that the continents had once been joined together and have slowly moved apart. He pointed to the way South America and Africa fit together, and to matching fossils and rocks on both sides of the ocean. Geologists mocked him because he couldn't explain what could move continents. In the 1950s and 60s, discoveries about the seafloor provided the answer, in the theory of plate tectonics.|1912|Wegener, 1915
The Ring of Fire | A horseshoe around the Pacific where most earthquakes and volcanoes happen. | Around the edge of the Pacific Ocean lies a horseshoe-shaped zone of volcanoes and earthquake faults, 40,000 kilometers long, known as the Ring of Fire. About 90 percent of the world's earthquakes, and about 75 percent of its active volcanoes, occur there. The reason is that several tectonic plates meet along the Pacific's edge, where one plate slides under another.|90% of quakes|USGS
The magnitude scale | Each whole step up in earthquake magnitude is about 32 times more energy. | The numbers used to describe earthquakes are logarithmic, so each whole step up means about 32 times more energy released. A magnitude 7 earthquake releases roughly 32 times as much energy as a magnitude 6, and about 1,000 times as much as a magnitude 5. The strongest earthquake ever recorded was a magnitude 9.5 in Chile in 1960.|32×|USGS
Krakatoa | The 1883 eruption was heard 4,800 kilometers away. | On August 27, 1883, the volcano Krakatoa in Indonesia exploded. The sound was heard on Rodrigues Island, 4,800 kilometers away, and is considered the loudest sound in recorded history. The giant tsunamis it generated killed more than 36,000 people. The ash and sulfur it pushed high into the atmosphere dimmed sunsets around the world and lowered global temperatures for several years.|4,800 km|Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program
The year without a summer | A volcano in Indonesia changed the weather around the world in 1816. | In April 1815 Mount Tambora in Indonesia had the largest eruption in recorded history. It threw a huge cloud of ash and sulfur into the atmosphere, blocking some sunlight. In 1816, the following year, snow fell in New England in June and frosts came through the summer, and crops failed and food ran short across Europe. It was known as the "year without a summer."|1816|Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program
The rock cycle | Rocks are formed from magma, worn down into sediment and baked or squeezed into new rock. | Rocks are constantly being recycled. Magma that cools becomes igneous rock. Wind and water wear rocks down into sediment, which gets pressed into layers of sedimentary rock. Heat and pressure transform either type into metamorphic rock. Any of the three can be melted, buried, uplifted or worn down to become another, over millions of years, in a slow cycle with no beginning or end.||Standard geology text
Deep time | James Hutton found "no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end" in 1788. | In 1788 the Scottish farmer and geologist James Hutton studied rock layers at a place called Siccar Point and realized that they showed the same processes we see today, slow erosion and deposition and uplift, repeating over and over. He wrote that he found "no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end." It made people realize that the Earth is vastly older than the thousands of years that had been assumed.|1788|Hutton, 1788
Earth's history in one day | If Earth's 4.5 billion years were 24 hours, humans arrived about six seconds before midnight. | Imagine all 4.5 billion years of Earth's history squeezed into 24 hours. The planet forms at midnight, and the first life appears around 4 a.m. Dinosaurs arrive around 10:40 p.m., and they die out at about 11:40 p.m. Modern humans show up only in the last six seconds before midnight. Written history would be a flicker in the last fraction of a second.|6 seconds|Standard geology text
The Mohs scale | Mineral hardness from 1 to 10, from talc to diamond. | In 1812 the German mineralogist Friedrich Mohs created a scale for the hardness of minerals, from 1 for talc, which you can scratch with a fingernail, to 10 for diamond. A harder mineral scratches a softer one. Your fingernail is about 2.5, a steel knife about 5.5 and quartz is 7. The scale shows order only, since the steps aren't equal: diamond is far harder than corundum, which is 9.|1812|Mohs, 1812
Earth's water | The oceans hold 97% of Earth's water, and only about 1% is fresh and easy to reach. | Oceans hold about 97 percent of Earth's water, and it's salty. Of the remaining 3 percent that's fresh, most is locked up in glaciers and ice sheets, so only about 1 percent of all the water is fresh water that's easy for us to reach. Of the liquid fresh water, most is underground, and rivers and lakes hold only a tiny fraction.|97%|USGS
The ocean floor | We have mapped the Moon in more detail than our own seafloor. | We know more about the surface of the Moon than about our own seafloor. In 2023, the Seabed 2030 project, which is working to map all of it, reported that about a quarter of the ocean floor had been mapped to high resolution. The deepest known point is Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench, nearly 11 kilometers down, deeper than Mount Everest is tall.|About 25%|Seabed 2030
Layers of air | The atmosphere has layers, and almost all weather is in the lowest one. | The atmosphere is made up of layers. The lowest, the troposphere, reaches about 12 kilometers up and holds most of the air and almost all weather. Above it is the stratosphere, which holds the ozone layer that absorbs harmful ultraviolet light. Space has no sharp edge, but by convention, it starts at the Kármán line, 100 kilometers above the surface.||NASA; FAI
The ozone hole | A success story: the world agreed to stop the chemicals that were destroying it. | In the 1980s scientists discovered a hole in the ozone layer over Antarctica, caused by chemicals called CFCs, used in aerosol sprays and refrigerators. In 1987 countries signed the Montreal Protocol, banning them. It worked: the UN reported in 2022 that the ozone layer is on track to recover to 1980 levels by about 2066 over Antarctica, much sooner elsewhere.|1987|UNEP, 2022
The greenhouse effect | Gases like carbon dioxide trap heat, keeping Earth warmer than it would be otherwise. | Gases in the atmosphere, such as carbon dioxide, water vapor and methane, trap some of the heat that Earth radiates, keeping the planet warmer than it would otherwise be. Without any greenhouse effect, the average temperature would be about -18 degrees Celsius. John Tyndall measured it in the lab in 1859, and in 1896 Svante Arrhenius calculated how much extra carbon dioxide would warm the planet.|1896|Tyndall, 1859; Arrhenius, 1896
Carbon dioxide in the air | The level has risen from about 280 parts per million to over 420. | Before the Industrial Revolution, the concentration of carbon dioxide in the air was about 280 parts per million. Today it's over 420. Ice cores, which trap ancient air in bubbles, show that for 800,000 years before industrialization, it stayed between about 180 and 300. Burning coal, oil and gas, and clearing forests, account for the recent rise.|280 to 420+|NOAA; IPCC AR6
Warming so far | Earth's average surface temperature has risen about 1.1 degrees Celsius since the late 1800s. | Earth's average surface temperature has risen by about 1.1 degrees Celsius since the late 1800s. The 2021 report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change states that it's unequivocal that human influence has warmed the planet. Most of the increase has happened since about 1970, and the last decade has been the warmest on record.|+1.1 °C|IPCC AR6, 2021
Ice ages | Slow wobbles in Earth's orbit trigger cycles of ice and warmth. | For the last couple of million years, Earth has swung between long ice ages and warmer interglacial periods. The Serbian scientist Milutin Milanković worked out that slow changes in Earth's orbit and tilt, in cycles of about 100,000, 41,000 and 23,000 years, alter how much sunlight reaches different latitudes and trigger the cycles. The last glacial maximum was about 20,000 years ago.||Milanković, 1941; Hays et al., 1976
Hurricane fuel | Hurricanes form over ocean water at least 26.5 degrees Celsius. | Hurricanes need warm ocean water, at least about 26.5 degrees Celsius, as fuel. The warm water gives them heat and moisture. When a hurricane moves over land or cooler water, it loses its fuel and weakens quickly. The Saffir-Simpson scale rates hurricanes from category 1 to 5 by their wind speed, with category 5 starting at 157 miles per hour.|26.5 °C|NOAA
Rainbows | Sunlight bends and reflects inside raindrops, and the colors always appear 42 degrees from the point opposite the Sun. | A rainbow forms when sunlight enters raindrops, bends, reflects off the back and bends again as it leaves, spreading into colors. The red light always exits at an angle of about 42 degrees from the direction directly opposite the Sun. Every raindrop sends colored light in many directions, but you see only the drops at the right angle. So no two people see exactly the same rainbow.|42°|Standard optics text
Snowflakes | Each starts as a six-sided ice crystal because of the shape of water molecules. | A snowflake starts as a tiny ice crystal, and because of the shape of water molecules, it has six sides. As it falls, the shape it grows into depends on the temperature and humidity along the way, so each flake has its own history. Wilson Bentley, a farmer in Vermont, began photographing them with a microscope camera in 1885, and took more than 5,000 images.||Libbrecht, 2005
The Coriolis effect | Earth's spin bends the path of winds and ocean currents. | Because Earth spins, moving air and water curve to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern. This is the Coriolis effect, and it's why storms rotate counterclockwise in the north. But it's far too weak to decide which way water swirls down a sink or toilet, which depends on the shape of the bowl and any currents in the water.||Standard meteorology text
El Niño | Every two to seven years the central Pacific warms and changes the weather worldwide. | Every two to seven years, the surface of the central and eastern Pacific Ocean warms, trade winds weaken, and weather patterns shift around the world. This is called El Niño. It can bring floods to Peru, droughts to Australia and Indonesia and milder winters in parts of North America. Its cooler opposite phase is called La Niña.||NOAA
The pH scale | A scale from 0 to 14 for acidity; 7 is neutral. | The pH scale measures how acidic or basic a liquid is, from 0 to 14, with 7 neutral, like pure water. The Danish chemist Søren Sørensen introduced it in 1909. Each step is a tenfold change, so lemon juice, at about pH 2, is 100,000 times more acidic than water. Lye is at the other end, about 13.|1909|Sørensen, 1909
Why soap works | Soap molecules have a water-loving end and a grease-loving end. | A soap molecule has two ends: one that's attracted to water and one that's attracted to grease. When you wash, the grease-loving ends stick into oil and dirt, and the water-loving ends face outward, so that water can carry the oil away. Soap can also break apart the fatty outer envelope of some viruses, including coronaviruses, which is why handwashing works so well.||Standard chemistry text
Salt melts ice | Dissolved salt lowers the temperature at which water freezes. | Dissolving salt in water lowers the temperature at which it freezes. Seawater freezes at about -2 degrees Celsius, and a saturated salt solution won't freeze until about -21. That's why roads are salted in winter, so that ice melts and doesn't form. It stops working when it gets extremely cold, below about -10 degrees Celsius, when other chemicals are used.||Standard chemistry text
Avogadro's number | A mole contains 6.022 x 10^23 particles. | Atoms are far too small to count one at a time, so chemists count them in huge groups called moles. A mole is 6.022 times 10 to the 23rd particles, known as Avogadro's number. It's set up so that a mole of carbon-12 atoms weighs 12 grams. That lets chemists, by weighing, count atoms. Since 2019, the number has been fixed as an exact value.|6.022 × 10²³|BIPM, 2019
Conservation of mass | In a chemical reaction, nothing is lost or gained, only rearranged. | In a chemical reaction, nothing is created or destroyed. In 1789 Antoine Lavoisier carefully weighed everything before and after burning things in sealed vessels and showed that the total mass didn't change. When something burns, it gains the weight of the oxygen it combines with. His work ended the old theory that burning releases a substance called phlogiston.||Lavoisier, 1789
Boyle's law | Squeeze a gas into half the space and its pressure doubles. | If you squeeze a gas into half the space, its pressure doubles, as long as the temperature stays the same. Robert Boyle worked this out in 1662, with air trapped in a bent glass tube and mercury. It explains why a bicycle pump heats up when you use it and why your ears pop in an airplane when the pressure of the air around you changes.|1662|Boyle, 1662
Brownian motion | Pollen in water jiggles because unseen molecules keep bumping it. | In 1827 the botanist Robert Brown noticed that tiny grains of pollen in water jiggle around constantly. He couldn't explain it. In 1905 Albert Einstein showed it happens because invisible water molecules are bumping into the grains from all sides. The French physicist Jean Perrin confirmed it with experiments in the following years, which also convinced skeptics that atoms and molecules really exist.||Brown, 1827; Einstein, 1905
The first plastic | Bakelite, invented in 1907, was the first fully synthetic plastic. | In 1907 the Belgian-American chemist Leo Baekeland invented Bakelite, the first fully synthetic plastic, from phenol and formaldehyde. Unlike earlier plastics, it didn't melt once it was set, so it could be used for things that got hot, like telephone housings, radios, and electrical parts. It was nicknamed "the material of a thousand uses."|1907|Baekeland, 1907
Forever chemicals | PFAS are man-made chemicals that persist in the body and environment for decades or longer. | PFAS are a large family of man-made chemicals that were used for decades in non-stick pans, waterproof fabrics and firefighting foam. They're called "forever chemicals" because their carbon-fluorine bonds, among the strongest in chemistry, mean they don't break down, so they build up in the environment and our bodies. High levels have been linked to health problems including some cancers and reduced immune response.||US EPA; ATSDR
Catalysts | A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up. | A catalyst speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up itself. Enzymes are the catalysts of living things, making the reactions of life happen fast enough at body temperature. In a car's exhaust system, the catalytic converter uses platinum and related metals to turn harmful gases into less harmful ones. Without catalysts, many reactions in your cells would be too slow to keep you alive.||Standard chemistry text
The fire triangle | Fire needs fuel, heat and oxygen; remove one and it goes out. | Fire needs three things: fuel, heat and oxygen. Take away any one, and it goes out. Water cools a fire, removing heat. A fire blanket or lid cuts off the oxygen. A firebreak removes the fuel. Fire safety experts now often use a fire tetrahedron, which adds a fourth element, the chemical chain reaction that keeps a flame going.||NFPA
Rust | Iron slowly reacts with oxygen and water to form iron oxide. | Rust is what happens when iron slowly reacts with oxygen and water to form iron oxide, which is flaky and weak. Salt speeds up the process, which is why cars rust faster where roads are salted. Stainless steel resists rust because the chromium in it forms a thin, invisible protective layer. The damage from corrosion costs the world's economies trillions of dollars a year.||Standard chemistry text
