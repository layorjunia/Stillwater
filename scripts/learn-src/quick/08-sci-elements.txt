# The periodic table: all 118 elements, and the ideas behind it.
@series sci
@src CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics; IUPAC
@conf solid

The periodic table | Elements sorted by atomic number fall into repeating families. | Dmitri Mendeleev, a Russian chemist, published his table in 1869. He arranged the known elements by weight and noticed that their properties repeated in a pattern. Where the pattern broke, he left a gap and boldly predicted that an element would be found to fill it, along with what it would be like. Gallium, scandium and germanium were discovered over the next seventeen years, and each matched his descriptions closely. That's what convinced chemists he was right.|1869|Mendeleev, 1869
Atomic number | The number of protons in an atom's nucleus decides which element it is. | What makes an element what it is? Not its weight, as early chemists thought, but the number of protons in its nucleus. In 1913 the young English physicist Henry Moseley measured the X-rays given off by different elements and found that their frequencies followed a neat series of whole numbers. That fixed the true order of the table, and showed exactly where the gaps were. Change the number of protons in an atom and you've changed the element.||Moseley, 1913
Noble gases | Helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon barely react with anything. | Helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon sit in the last column of the table, and they hardly react with anything. Their outer shells of electrons are full, so they have no reason to take part in chemistry. That's why airships and balloons now use helium instead of flammable hydrogen, and why the glowing gas in a neon sign doesn't burn or wear out. They were nicknamed "noble" because they keep to themselves.||Standard chemistry text
Alkali metals | The soft, shiny metals in the first column react violently with water. | The metals in the first column, lithium, sodium, potassium and the rest, are soft enough to cut with a knife and react strongly with water. Lithium fizzes, sodium skates around and burns, and potassium bursts into a lilac flame. Each atom has a single loose electron on the outside that it's eager to give away. The further you go down the column, the looser the electron and the more violent the reaction.||Standard chemistry text
Halogens | Fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine and astatine, one electron short of a full shell. | Fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine and astatine are one electron short of a full outer shell, so they're eager to grab one from another atom. That makes them very reactive, and they readily form salts. In fact "halogen" comes from Greek words meaning "salt-former." Fluorine is the most reactive of all the elements, and will even react with glass. Chlorine and iodine are used as disinfectants.||Standard chemistry text
Rare earths are not rare | The 15 lanthanides are fairly common in Earth's crust but hard to separate. | The fifteen elements called the lanthanides, plus two relatives, are called rare earths, but the name is misleading. Cerium is about as common in Earth's crust as copper. What's rare is finding them concentrated in a spot where mining is worthwhile, and they're hard to separate from one another. They're in many things we use daily: magnets in headphones and electric motors, phone screens, lasers and lighting.||USGS, Rare Earth Elements
Hydrogen, element 1 | The lightest element and the most common in the universe. | Hydrogen has one proton and one electron, the simplest atom there is. It accounts for about three-quarters of all ordinary matter by mass, and it's what stars burn to make light. The French chemist Antoine Lavoisier gave it its name in 1783, from the Greek words for "water-former," because when hydrogen burns, it makes water. It's also being developed as a clean fuel, since burning it produces only water.|H · 1|Lavoisier, 1783
Helium, element 2 | A gas first found on the Sun before it was found on Earth. | In 1868, during a solar eclipse, astronomers saw a bright yellow line in the Sun's spectrum that didn't match any known element. They named the new element after Helios, the Greek word for the Sun. Only in 1895 did the chemist William Ramsay find it on Earth, in a uranium mineral. On Earth it forms from radioactive decay deep underground and gets trapped in natural gas, from which we collect it. Once it escapes, it floats off to space.|He · 2|Lockyer, 1868; Ramsay, 1895
Lithium, element 3 | The lightest metal; it is soft enough to cut with a knife and floats on water. | The lightest metal is so soft that you can cut it with a knife, and it floats on water. A Swedish chemist, Johan Arfwedson, found it in 1817. Today most people carry some in their pocket, in the lithium-ion battery that powers a phone. Lithium salts have also been used since the mid-twentieth century to treat bipolar disorder, as a mood stabilizer.|Li · 3|Arfwedson, 1817
Beryllium, element 4 | A light, stiff metal that is toxic as dust. | Beryllium is a light, stiff metal that is toxic as dust, so it's handled with care. Its qualities make it ideal for space: the 18 mirror segments of the James Webb Space Telescope are made of beryllium, coated with a thin layer of gold to reflect infrared light. In nature it also colors gems. Emerald and aquamarine are both forms of the mineral beryl.|Be · 4|NASA JWST
Boron, element 5 | An element that makes tough glass and plant cell walls. | Boron compounds are in more everyday things than you might guess. Borosilicate glass, the type used for lab glassware and some oven-safe dishes, can handle sudden changes in temperature without cracking. Borax, a boron mineral, has been mined for thousands of years and was used in ancient times for soldering gold. Plants also need a trace of boron to build healthy cell walls.|B · 5|Standard chemistry text
Carbon, element 6 | The backbone of all known life, in diamond, graphite and graphene. | Carbon atoms can link up in an astonishing variety of ways, which is why the chemistry of life is built on it. About 18 percent of your body's mass is carbon. Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon, differently arranged. In 2004 Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov peeled single-atom-thick layers of graphite from a lump using sticky tape, making graphene, and they won the 2010 Nobel Prize for it.|C · 6|Geim & Novoselov, 2004
Nitrogen, element 7 | Makes up 78% of the air we breathe, but plants cannot use it directly. | Nitrogen makes up 78 percent of the air, but plants can't use it in that form. For centuries farmers depended on manure and natural deposits. Between 1909 and 1913 the chemists Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch worked out how to turn nitrogen from the air into ammonia on a large scale, making artificial fertilizer possible. Roughly half the nitrogen atoms in your body were fixed by the Haber-Bosch process.|N · 7|Haber, 1909; Smil, 2001
Oxygen, element 8 | The gas that makes up 21% of air and about 65% of your body's mass. | Oxygen is about 21 percent of the air, and about 65 percent of your body's mass, mostly as the oxygen in water. The Swedish chemist Carl Scheele and the English minister Joseph Priestley each found it independently in the 1770s, and Antoine Lavoisier worked out that it was what made things burn. Most of the oxygen in the air was produced by photosynthesis, by plants, algae and bacteria, over billions of years.|O · 8|Lavoisier, 1777
Fluorine, element 9 | The most reactive of all the elements. | Fluorine is the most chemically reactive of all the elements, so it's hard to isolate. For decades, chemists were injured, and some were killed, by the poisonous fumes while trying. The French chemist Henri Moissan finally succeeded in 1886 and won a Nobel Prize for it. Fluoride, a compound of fluorine, is added to many toothpastes and water supplies because it strengthens tooth enamel and reduces cavities.|F · 9|Moissan, 1886
Neon, element 10 | The gas that glows red-orange in a sign. | Neon was found in 1898 by William Ramsay and Morris Travers, who chilled air until it turned liquid and then let it boil off piece by piece. A noble gas, it glows a bright red-orange when electricity passes through it. It's not used in all signs called neon, though. Many use other gases, such as argon, or coatings inside the glass tubes, to produce other colors.|Ne · 10|Ramsay & Travers, 1898
Sodium, element 11 | A soft metal that reacts violently with water and is half of table salt. | Sodium is a soft, silvery metal that reacts violently with water. In 1807 Humphry Davy was the first to isolate it, by passing an electric current through molten sodium hydroxide. Table salt is sodium combined with chlorine, a gas that's poisonous by itself. It's a striking example of how two dangerous substances can join to make something we eat every day. Your nerves also use sodium to carry signals.|Na · 11|Davy, 1807
Magnesium, element 12 | A light metal that burns with a blinding white flame. | Magnesium burns with a dazzling white flame, which is why it was used in the old camera flashes and in fireworks. It's also at the center of every chlorophyll molecule, the green pigment plants use to catch sunlight. In your body, it helps more than 300 enzymes work, and it's found in foods like nuts, seeds and leafy greens.|Mg · 12|Standard chemistry text
Aluminum, element 13 | The most abundant metal in Earth's crust, about 8% of it. | Aluminum is the most abundant metal in the Earth's crust, but it's always combined with other elements, and was very difficult to extract. For a time in the 1800s it was more valuable than gold. In 1886 two young inventors, Charles Hall in the US and Paul Héroult in France, independently found a way to extract it using electricity, and the price collapsed within a few years.|Al · 13|Hall & Héroult, 1886
Silicon, element 14 | The second most abundant element in Earth's crust, and the basis of computer chips. | Silicon is the second most abundant element in Earth's crust after oxygen. Sand and most rocks are silicon compounds. In its ultrapure crystal form it's a semiconductor, meaning it can be made to conduct electricity or not, which makes it the foundation of computer chips. California's "Silicon Valley" is named for it.|Si · 14|USGS
Phosphorus, element 15 | An element essential to DNA, cell membranes and the energy molecule ATP. | Phosphorus is essential to life: it's in DNA, in cell membranes and in ATP, the molecule that carries energy within cells. It was discovered in 1669 by Hennig Brand, a German alchemist who was searching for the philosopher's stone and boiled down enormous quantities of urine. He ended up with a white substance that glowed in the dark. Today, much of it goes into fertilizer.|P · 15|Brand, 1669
Sulfur, element 16 | A yellow solid whose compounds smell like rotten eggs. | Sulfur is a yellow solid that has been known since ancient times. The "rotten egg" smell we associate with it comes from hydrogen sulfide gas, not from pure sulfur. In 1839 Charles Goodyear discovered that heating rubber with sulfur made it stronger and less sticky, a process called vulcanization. It made possible things like tires, shoe soles and rubber hoses.|S · 16|Goodyear, 1839
Chlorine, element 17 | A gas that disinfects water and was also used as a weapon. | Chlorine is a greenish, poisonous gas, and it's one of the most useful chemicals in public health. Adding small amounts to drinking water kills germs and has dramatically reduced diseases such as cholera and typhoid. But it also has a dark history. The German army first used it as a weapon on April 22, 1915, at the Second Battle of Ypres in the First World War.|Cl · 17|Standard chemistry text
Argon, element 18 | About 1% of the air; its name means "lazy." | In 1894 Lord Rayleigh noticed that nitrogen taken from the air was slightly heavier than nitrogen made from chemicals. He and William Ramsay tracked down the difference to a previously unknown gas making up about 1 percent of the air. They named it argon, from the Greek for "lazy," because it won't react with anything. Light bulbs are often filled with it, to protect the filament.|Ar · 18|Rayleigh & Ramsay, 1894
Potassium, element 19 | A metal your nerves and heart need to work. | Potassium is a soft metal that reacts even more violently with water than sodium, and the hydrogen it releases ignites with a lilac flame. In your body it works with sodium to carry nerve signals and keep your heartbeat steady. A banana has about 420 milligrams, but many other foods, including potatoes, beans and avocados, have more, and most people don't get enough.|K · 19|NIH Office of Dietary Supplements
Calcium, element 20 | The most abundant metal in your body, with about 99% in bones and teeth. | Calcium is the most abundant metal in the human body, and about 99 percent of it is stored in bones and teeth. The other one percent plays an important role in making muscles contract, nerves send signals and blood clot. Chalk, limestone and marble are all forms of calcium carbonate, often formed from the shells of tiny sea creatures long ago.|Ca · 20|NIH Office of Dietary Supplements
Scandium, element 21 | A light metal found in 1879, which Mendeleev had predicted in 1871. | Scandium is a good example of Mendeleev's predictions. In 1871 he described a missing element beneath boron in his table, "eka-boron," and said what it should be like. In 1879 the Swedish chemist Lars Nilson found a new element in a Scandinavian mineral, named it after Scandinavia and it matched. Today it's used in small amounts to strengthen aluminum in bicycle frames and aircraft parts.|Sc · 21|Nilson, 1879
Titanium, element 22 | A strong, light metal that resists corrosion and is accepted by the body. | Titanium is as strong as many steels but about 40 percent lighter, and it doesn't corrode. In the 1950s the Swedish surgeon Per-Ingvar Brånemark found by accident that bone grows right into titanium and holds it firmly in place, a property he named osseointegration. That discovery led to dental implants and hip and knee replacements, and it's why those implants are made of titanium.|Ti · 22|Brånemark, 1950s
Vanadium, element 23 | A metal that makes steel tougher. | Vanadium is a hard, silvery metal that makes steel much tougher. It's named for Vanadis, a Norse goddess of beauty, because its compounds come in so many beautiful colors. Henry Ford used vanadium steel for the frame of the Model T because it was stronger and lighter than ordinary steel, which helped make the car affordable and durable.|V · 23|Standard chemistry text
Chromium, element 24 | The metal that makes steel stainless. | Chromium is what makes stainless steel stainless. Steel with at least about 10.5 percent chromium forms an invisible, self-repairing layer of oxide on its surface that prevents rust. The metal's name comes from the Greek word for color, because its compounds are so vivid, including the green in emeralds and the red in rubies. It's also the shiny coating on "chrome" car parts.|Cr · 24|Standard metallurgy text
Manganese, element 25 | A metal that hardens steel and is a trace nutrient. | About 90 percent of the manganese mined goes into making steel, where it makes the metal harder and easier to work. In small amounts, it's an essential nutrient: it helps enzymes build bone and break down food. But too much, as workers in some industries have been exposed to, damages the brain, causing symptoms like those of Parkinson's disease.|Mn · 25|NIH
Iron, element 26 | The metal at the heart of Earth, of blood and of civilization. | Iron has shaped history. Earth's core is mostly iron and nickel, which generates the planet's magnetic field. In your blood, hemoglobin uses iron to carry oxygen from the lungs to the rest of the body, and it's what makes blood red. In the Middle East, the Iron Age began around 1200 BCE, when people learned to make tools stronger than those of bronze.|Fe · 26|Standard chemistry text
Cobalt, element 27 | A metal that makes blue pigment and is a part of vitamin B12. | For centuries, cobalt has given pottery and glass a brilliant deep blue, and "cobalt blue" is still a favorite pigment. Today, it's also an essential ingredient in the rechargeable batteries in phones and electric cars. About 70 percent of the world's supply comes from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and its mining has been linked to child labor and unsafe conditions.|Co · 27|Standard chemistry text
Nickel, element 28 | A metal used in coins, steel and batteries. | The name comes from German miners, who found an ore that looked like copper but yielded none. They blamed a mischievous spirit, "Old Nick," and called it Kupfernickel, "copper devil." The metal was isolated in 1751. Today it's used in stainless steel, batteries and coins. The American five-cent coin, called a nickel, is actually 75 percent copper and 25 percent nickel.|Ni · 28|Standard chemistry text
Copper, element 29 | One of the first metals people used, around 10,000 years ago. | Copper was one of the first metals people used, about 10,000 years ago, because it can be found in a pure form and is easy to hammer. It conducts electricity almost as well as silver, so it's in nearly every wire in your house. It also kills many germs, and in 2008 the US Environmental Protection Agency approved copper alloys as the first solid surfaces that can claim to be antimicrobial.|Cu · 29|US EPA, 2008
Zinc, element 30 | A metal that protects steel from rust and helps your immune system. | Zinc is a bluish metal used to coat steel in a process called galvanizing, protecting it from rust even when scratched, since the zinc corrodes first. Mixed with copper, it makes brass. In your body, it's needed by hundreds of enzymes and plays a role in immunity and healing. Foods like oysters, beef and beans are good sources.|Zn · 30|NIH
Gallium, element 31 | A metal that melts in your hand at 29.8 degrees Celsius. | Gallium is a soft metal that melts at 29.8 degrees Celsius, just below body temperature, so a lump of it will melt in your hand. In 1871 Mendeleev predicted it as "eka-aluminum," and in 1875 Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran discovered it, and measured properties that matched. Today, compounds of gallium are used in blue and white LED lights and in some of the most efficient solar cells.|Ga · 31|Lecoq de Boisbaudran, 1875
Germanium, element 32 | The element of the first transistor. | In December 1947, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain at Bell Labs built the first working transistor, and its crystal was germanium. The transistor replaced bulky vacuum tubes and launched the age of electronics, and it won a Nobel Prize for them and William Shockley. Silicon later took over as the main material because it's cheaper and works at higher temperatures, but germanium is still found in some fiber-optic and infrared devices.|Ge · 32|Bell Labs, 1947
Arsenic, element 33 | A classic poison that was hard to detect until 1836. | For centuries, arsenic was the classic murder poison. It was odorless, tasteless and its symptoms resembled those of common illnesses, so it earned the nickname "inheritance powder." In 1836 the chemist James Marsh devised a sensitive test that could detect even tiny traces in a body. It was the first time that courts had reliable forensic proof of arsenic poisoning, and it made such murders much harder to get away with.|As · 33|Marsh, 1836
Selenium, element 34 | A trace element needed in tiny amounts and toxic in larger ones. | Selenium is a trace element that your body needs in tiny amounts to make antioxidant enzymes, but too much is toxic. Brazil nuts are unusually rich in it, and a single nut can contain more than a day's supply. Its name comes from Selene, the Greek goddess of the Moon, chosen because it was found along with tellurium, whose name comes from the Latin for Earth.|Se · 34|NIH
Bromine, element 35 | One of two elements that are liquid at room temperature. | Bromine and mercury are the only two elements that are liquid at room temperature. Bromine is a dark reddish-brown liquid that gives off a harsh, irritating vapor, and its name comes from the Greek for "stench." Compounds of bromine have been used in photographic film, in fire retardants and in some medicines.|Br · 35|Standard chemistry text
Krypton, element 36 | A gas that once defined the meter. | For 23 years, the meter was defined by this gas. From 1960 to 1983, one meter was officially 1,650,763.73 wavelengths of the orange-red light given off by krypton-86 atoms. That was more precise and reproducible than the old metal bar kept in Paris. In 1983 it was replaced by a definition based on the speed of light. Its name comes from the Greek for "hidden."|Kr · 36|BIPM
Rubidium, element 37 | A soft metal used in atomic clocks. | Atoms of rubidium can be made to flip between two energy states at an extremely precise frequency, so they make excellent timekeepers. Small rubidium atomic clocks are cheap and compact enough to be used in cell phone towers, in satellites and in telecommunications equipment, where accurate timing is vital. The name comes from the Latin for deep red, from the color of its spectral lines.|Rb · 37|NIST
Strontium, element 38 | The element that makes fireworks red. | Strontium salts burn with a brilliant crimson color, and they're used in red fireworks and emergency flares. It also has a more worrying form. Strontium-90, a radioactive isotope released by nuclear weapons tests and accidents, is dangerous because the body mistakes it for calcium and deposits it in the bones, where it irradiates the marrow for years.|Sr · 38|Standard chemistry text
Yttrium, element 39 | One of four elements named for the Swedish village of Ytterby. | Yttrium is one of four elements named for the village of Ytterby in Sweden, where a quarry yielded a black mineral that contained many new elements. The other three are ytterbium, terbium and erbium. Yttrium compounds are used in LED lights, in lasers and in superconductors, and they also helped create the red in old television screens.|Y · 39|Standard chemistry text
Zirconium, element 40 | A metal that lets neutrons pass, so it holds nuclear fuel. | Zirconium is remarkable because it barely absorbs neutrons, which makes it ideal for holding nuclear fuel. The fuel rods in most nuclear reactors are covered in a zirconium alloy. The mineral zircon is also the source of cubic zirconia, the sparkling diamond lookalike used in jewelry, which is made from zirconium dioxide.|Zr · 40|Standard chemistry text
Niobium, element 41 | A metal that is superconducting when cold, used in MRI magnets. | Alloys of niobium and titanium become superconductors when cooled below about 10 kelvin, meaning they carry electricity with no resistance at all. They're used to make the powerful magnets in MRI machines and in particle accelerators. The element was named for Niobe, the daughter of Tantalus in Greek mythology, since it's always found with tantalum and is chemically very similar.|Nb · 41|Standard chemistry text
Molybdenum, element 42 | A metal that strengthens steel and is vital to enzymes. | Molybdenum has one of the highest melting points of all metals, about 2,620 degrees Celsius, and it makes steel stronger at high temperatures. It's also essential to life: plants need it for the enzyme that turns nitrogen into forms they can use, and many enzymes in your own body contain it. It's named from the Greek word for lead, because it was once confused with it.|Mo · 42|Standard chemistry text
Technetium, element 43 | The first element made by people, in 1937. | Technetium was the first element to be made artificially. In 1937 Emilio Segrè, an Italian physicist, got a strip of molybdenum that had been bombarded in a cyclotron at Berkeley, and with Carlo Perrier, found the new element in it. It has no stable isotopes, so it's nearly absent in nature. One form, technetium-99m, is widely used in medicine: it's involved in about 40 million diagnostic scans every year.|Tc · 43|Perrier & Segrè, 1937
Ruthenium, element 44 | A hard metal used to harden platinum and in electronics. | Ruthenium is a rare, hard metal that's named for Ruthenia, the Latin name for Russia. The Russian-Baltic chemist Karl Claus identified it in 1844, from platinum ore from the Ural Mountains. It's used to harden platinum and palladium, and for tough electrical contacts. It's also used in electronics, for the layers in hard drives.|Ru · 44|Claus, 1844
Rhodium, element 45 | One of the rarest and most valuable metals, used in car exhaust cleaners. | Rhodium is one of the rarest and most valuable metals. About 80 percent of it goes to make catalytic converters, which turn the most harmful gases in car exhaust into less harmful ones. The name comes from the Greek for rose, because many of its salts are pink or red. Its price has at times been much higher than gold's.|Rh · 45|Standard chemistry text
Palladium, element 46 | A metal that can soak up hydrogen like a sponge. | Palladium has a remarkable ability to absorb hydrogen, in some cases hundreds of times its own volume. It's also an important metal for catalytic converters, and it's used in electronics and jewelry. William Wollaston discovered it in 1802 and named it for the asteroid Pallas, which had been found the same year, itself named after the Greek goddess Athena.|Pd · 46|Wollaston, 1803
Silver, element 47 | The best conductor of electricity and heat among all metals. | Silver is the best conductor of both electricity and heat among all the metals. It tarnishes because it reacts with tiny traces of sulfur in the air, forming black silver sulfide. Silver compounds were once the light-sensitive part of all photographic film and paper, because they darken when exposed to light. It's also a natural antibacterial, which is why silver was used in medicine.|Ag · 47|Standard chemistry text
Cadmium, element 48 | A metal used in old rechargeable batteries and a hazard to health. | Cadmium was used in rechargeable batteries and in pigments, but it's a poison. In the early 1900s, people in parts of Japan along the Jinzu River developed itai-itai disease, which means "ouch-ouch," a painful bone disease caused by mine waste containing cadmium that got into rice and water. It led to stricter limits on cadmium, and today its use is much more limited.|Cd · 48|Standard toxicology text
Indium, element 49 | A soft metal in the transparent coating on touchscreens. | Indium is a soft metal that, when combined with tin and oxygen, forms indium tin oxide, a coating that is both transparent and electrically conductive. That's the layer that senses your touch on the screen of almost every phone, tablet and flat-panel TV. The name comes from the bright indigo line in its spectrum, which is how it was identified in 1863.|In · 49|Standard chemistry text
Tin, element 50 | The metal that, with copper, made the Bronze Age. | Tin was one of the keys to early civilization. About 3300 BCE people discovered that mixing tin with copper makes bronze, which is much harder than copper alone, and the Bronze Age began. When you bend a bar of pure tin, it makes a faint crackling noise called "tin cry," caused by the crystals inside it rubbing against each other.|Sn · 50|Standard chemistry text
Antimony, element 51 | A metalloid that ancient people used as eye makeup. | Antimony has been known since ancient times. In Egypt and elsewhere, a black powder from the mineral stibnite was ground up and used as kohl, an eye cosmetic. The chemical symbol Sb comes from "stibium," the Latin name for the mineral. Today, antimony is used to make flame retardants and to harden lead in batteries.|Sb · 51|Standard chemistry text
Tellurium, element 52 | A rare element used in solar panels. | Tellurium is one of the rarest stable elements in Earth's crust, rarer than platinum. It's used in solar panels made with cadmium telluride, the second most common type of solar panel after silicon. It was first isolated in 1782 by a Hungarian-Austrian mineralogist, Franz-Joseph Müller von Reichenstein, and named by Martin Klaproth for the Latin word for Earth, tellus.|Te · 52|USGS
Iodine, element 53 | A trace element your thyroid needs to make hormones. | Your thyroid gland needs iodine to make the hormones that regulate your metabolism. Without enough, it swells into a goiter. In 1924 Michigan began adding iodine to table salt, and the practice spread across the US, nearly eliminating goiter. Iodine deficiency is still a problem in parts of the world, and is a leading preventable cause of intellectual disability.|I · 53|NIH; WHO
Xenon, element 54 | A heavy noble gas whose name means "stranger." | Xenon is a heavy noble gas, and the name comes from the Greek for "stranger." It was discovered in 1898 by William Ramsay and Morris Travers. It produces a bright, bluish-white light in car headlights and flash lamps. It's also used in ion engines for spacecraft, including NASA's Dawn mission to the asteroid belt, because its heavy atoms give more thrust.|Xe · 54|Ramsay & Travers, 1898
Cesium, element 55 | The element that defines the second. | The length of a second is defined by cesium. Since 1967, one second has been 9,192,631,770 cycles of the microwave radiation given off by a cesium-133 atom. Atomic clocks based on it are accurate to within about a second over tens of millions of years. It's the reason your phone's time is right, and satellite navigation systems depend on that precision.|Cs · 55|BIPM
Barium, element 56 | A heavy metal whose compounds block X-rays. | Barium compounds block X-rays, so patients are given a "barium meal," a chalky drink, to make the digestive tract show up clearly on an X-ray image. Barium salts also burn a bright green in fireworks. Although soluble barium compounds are poisonous, the one used for X-rays, barium sulfate, doesn't dissolve and passes through safely.|Ba · 56|Standard chemistry text
Lanthanum, element 57 | The first of the lanthanides, used in camera lenses and hybrid-car batteries. | Lanthanum is the first of the lanthanides, a row of 15 similar elements. Glass that contains lanthanum bends light strongly, which lets lens makers produce thinner and sharper camera and telescope lenses. It's also used in some batteries for hybrid cars. The name comes from the Greek for "to lie hidden," because it was hidden within other minerals when it was found in 1839.|La · 57|Standard chemistry text
Cerium, element 58 | The most abundant rare earth, as common as copper. | Cerium is the most abundant of the rare earths, about as common in the Earth's crust as copper. When cerium is mixed with iron, it makes ferrocerium, the material in lighter flints: scrape it, and tiny hot particles fly off as sparks. It's also in catalytic converters and in some self-cleaning oven walls, where it helps break down grease.|Ce · 58|USGS
Praseodymium, element 59 | A rare earth that colors glass and ceramics yellow-green. | The name means "green twin," from the Greek for green and twin. For about forty years, scientists believed that "didymium," meaning "twin," was a single element. In 1885 the Austrian chemist Carl Auer von Welsbach separated it into two, praseodymium and neodymium. Praseodymium gives glass and ceramics a yellow-green color, and is used in some strong magnets and welding goggles.|Pr · 59|Auer von Welsbach, 1885
Neodymium, element 60 | The metal in the strongest permanent magnets. | The neodymium-iron-boron magnet, developed in 1982, is the strongest type of permanent magnet commercially available. A small one can lift hundreds of times its own weight. You'll find them in headphones, computer hard drives, electric car motors and wind turbine generators. The name means "new twin," because it was split off from "didymium" in 1885.|Nd · 60|Sagawa, 1982
Promethium, element 61 | A radioactive element with no stable form, found in only tiny amounts in nature. | Promethium has no stable isotopes, so it's extremely rare in nature, with only a tiny amount at any moment in Earth's crust. It was first identified in 1945 by scientists at Oak Ridge in Tennessee, from the fission products of uranium. It's named for Prometheus, the Titan who stole fire from the gods for humans. It was once used to make luminous paint for watch dials.|Pm · 61|Marinsky et al., 1945
Samarium, element 62 | A rare earth used in strong magnets and in a cancer drug. | Samarium is a silvery metal named for the mineral samarskite, which was in turn named for a Russian mining official, Colonel Vasili Samarsky-Bykhovets, making it the first element named, indirectly, for a person. Samarium-cobalt magnets stay strong at very high temperatures. A radioactive form, samarium-153, is used as a medicine to relieve pain from cancer that has spread to the bones.|Sm · 62|Standard chemistry text
Europium, element 63 | A rare earth that glows red under ultraviolet, used to secure euro notes. | Europium glows brightly red under ultraviolet light, and euro banknotes use it as a security feature. Under UV, the genuine notes show glowing marks and fibers that counterfeit paper lacks. The element was named by the French chemist Eugène-Anatole Demarçay in 1901 for the continent of Europe.
