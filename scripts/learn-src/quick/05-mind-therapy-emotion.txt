# Emotion, coping, therapy skills, and what psychology knows about getting through hard times.

Cognitive behavioral therapy | Thoughts, feelings and actions influence each other, so changing one can shift the others. | Aaron Beck was a psychiatrist who started out trying to test a Freudian theory of depression in the early 1960s and ended up noticing something else. His patients had a running stream of negative thoughts they hardly noticed, and those thoughts seemed to drive their mood. He built a short, practical therapy around spotting and testing them. It has been studied in hundreds of trials, and it helps with depression and anxiety. Many of its tools can be learned from a good book.||Beck, 1979; Hofmann et al., 2012
The thought record | Write the thought, the evidence for it and against it, then a more balanced thought. | This is one of the core exercises of cognitive therapy, and it's simple. You write down a situation, the thought that came to mind, and how strongly you felt it. Then you list the evidence for the thought and the evidence against, and come up with a more balanced version. The aim isn't to replace a bad thought with a positive one. It's to check whether the first thought was accurate, since it's usually only one of several ways to read what happened.||Beck, Cognitive Therapy of Depression (1979)
All-or-nothing thinking | Seeing things in two categories, perfect or failure. | David Burns, who popularized Beck's ideas in his 1980 book Feeling Good, listed it first among the common thinking traps. Things are either perfect or a failure, with nothing between. "I missed one step in the plan, so the whole day is ruined." Real life is mostly in the gray area, and so is most of what we do. A good habit is to catch words like "always," "never" and "completely," and ask what a more accurate description would be, such as "mostly went well, with one miss."||Burns, Feeling Good (1980)
Catastrophizing | Jumping to the worst possible outcome. | A headache becomes a brain tumor, a late reply becomes the end of the friendship. Catastrophizing means leaping to the worst possible outcome and treating it as likely. One standard way to deflate it is to ask three questions: what's the worst that could happen, what's the most likely thing, and how would I cope if the worst did happen? Answering them honestly usually shows that the worst case is unlikely, and that you'd manage better than you fear.||Beck, 1979; Burns, 1980
Mind reading | Assuming you know what others think of you, with no evidence. | "She didn't text back, so she must be angry with me." We often feel sure we know what others are thinking, especially about us, and the guess is usually negative. In truth, people are busy, distracted and mostly thinking about their own lives. When you notice yourself mind reading, it can help to list other explanations for what you saw, and to ask the person if it matters. A guess is not a fact, even when it feels like one.||Burns, 1980
Emotional reasoning | Treating a feeling as proof: "I feel guilty, so I must have done something wrong." | "I feel guilty, so I must have done something wrong." "I feel anxious, so something bad is about to happen." Emotional reasoning treats a feeling as evidence about the world. Feelings do tell you something, but mostly about your body and your state of mind. Guilt can come from a rule you were taught, and anxiety from tiredness. A helpful move is to name the feeling and then check the facts separately, as if you were a fair-minded friend.||Burns, 1980
Should statements | Rigid rules about how you or others must behave. | Words like "should," "must" and "ought" set rigid rules for how we or others have to behave. Aimed at ourselves, they produce guilt: "I should be over this by now." Aimed at others, they produce anger: "They should know better." The psychologist Albert Ellis called it "musturbation." Swapping "should" for "I would like to" or "it would help if" tends to keep the standard but drop the whip.||Ellis, 1962; Burns, 1980
Labeling | Describing a whole person by one act: "I'm a failure." | One mistake becomes a whole identity: "I'm a failure," "I'm an idiot," "I'm unlovable." It's an extreme form of overgeneralizing, and it's harsh in a way we'd never be with a friend. It also makes change seem impossible, because you can't change what you are. Instead, describe the behavior: "I made a mistake on that task." A mistake is something you did, and you can learn from what you did.||Burns, 1980
Personalization | Taking blame for things that were not yours alone. | When something goes wrong, we may assume it's our fault, even when it wasn't mainly. A child has a bad day at school. A partner is in a mood. A team misses a target. Usually there are many causes: other people's choices, circumstances, luck. One way to see this is to write a pie chart of everything that contributed, and put your own share last. Most people find it's much smaller than the share they'd been blaming themselves for.||Burns, 1980
The ABC model | An event (A) does not directly cause your feelings (C); your belief about it (B) does. | Albert Ellis taught in the 1950s that what upsets us isn't events themselves but our beliefs about them. A is the activating event, B is the belief, and C is the consequence, the emotion. Two people miss the same train. One thinks, "This is a disaster, I'll be late and in trouble," and feels panic. The other thinks, "Well, time for a coffee," and feels fine. The event was the same, and the beliefs made the difference. Beliefs can be questioned.||Ellis, 1962
Behavioral activation | When you feel low, do small valued activities first, and the mood follows. | When you're low, it's natural to wait until you feel better to do things. But the lack of activity makes the low mood worse, and a cycle starts. Behavioral activation reverses it: you schedule small activities that matter to you, such as a walk or a phone call, and do them whether or not you feel like it. Mood often follows action. In a 1996 trial, activation alone worked about as well as full cognitive therapy for depression.||Jacobson et al., 1996; Dimidjian et al., 2006
Opposite action | Act opposite to what the emotion urges when the emotion doesn't fit the facts. | Marsha Linehan's dialectical behavior therapy includes a skill for when an emotion pushes you to do something that isn't helpful. Sadness says to hide in bed. Anger says to lash out. Fear says to avoid. Opposite action means doing the reverse in small ways: reaching out to a friend, being gentle, approaching what you fear. It works when the feeling is out of proportion to the situation. It isn't meant to override feelings that fit the facts.||Linehan, DBT Skills Training Manual (2015)
Radical acceptance | Fully accept what has happened, without approving it, and stop fighting reality. | Marsha Linehan draws a line between pain and suffering. Pain is what happens: the loss, the diagnosis, the end of the marriage. Suffering is what we add by fighting the fact that it happened, with "this shouldn't be happening" or "it isn't fair." Radical acceptance means fully acknowledging what is, in your mind and body. It doesn't mean approving, liking it or giving up on changing things. It means no longer spending your energy on the argument with reality.||Linehan, 2015
Wise mind | The overlap of emotional mind and reasonable mind. | In dialectical behavior therapy, there are said to be three states of mind. Emotional mind is driven by feelings and tends to be impulsive. Reasonable mind is driven by facts and logic, and can miss what matters. Wise mind is where they overlap, the place where you know something is right and can feel it too. Linehan teaches people to pause, breathe and ask from that place, "What do I really want to do here?"||Linehan, 1993
TIPP skills | Four fast ways to calm a surge of distress: temperature, intense exercise, paced breathing, paired relaxation. | When distress spikes so hard that thinking clearly is out of reach, Linehan's group suggests changing the body first. TIPP stands for Temperature, Intense exercise, Paced breathing and Paired muscle relaxation. Cold water on your face, a minute of hard exercise or a long slow exhale quickly shifts your body's alarm state. They're meant for the moments when you're flooded, not for solving the underlying problem, which you can tackle once you're calmer.||Linehan, DBT Skills Training Manual (2015)
Cognitive defusion | Notice a thought as a thought, not a fact. | In acceptance and commitment therapy, you learn to treat thoughts as passing events in the mind, not as truths. Instead of "I'm worthless," you say, "I'm having the thought that I'm worthless." The thought may not vanish, but it loosens its grip, because you're now watching it instead of being it. It can sound like a small trick, but with practice it creates a little space between you and what your mind says, and in that space you can choose how to act.||Hayes, Strosahl & Wilson, 1999
Values versus goals | A goal can be finished; a value is a direction you keep walking in. | In acceptance and commitment therapy, a goal is something you can finish, like attending your child's recital. A value is a direction you keep moving in and never complete, like being a loving parent. Goals can be missed, and then it can feel like failure. Values can always be lived, in some small way, today. When life has been knocked off course, asking what you value, and what one small step fits it, can show you where to go.||Hayes, Strosahl & Wilson, 1999
Mindfulness-based stress reduction | An eight-week course of meditation and gentle movement. | In 1979 Jon Kabat-Zinn started a stress clinic at the University of Massachusetts Medical School. Patients came for an eight-week course combining meditation, body awareness and gentle yoga. It has been tested in many trials. A large review in 2014 found moderate evidence that it helps with anxiety, depression and pain, about as much as other active treatments. It's not a cure-all, but it gives many people a skill.||Kabat-Zinn, 1982; Goyal et al., 2014
Mindfulness and relapse | A mindfulness course can help keep depression from returning. | Depression often comes back. Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale developed a version of mindfulness training to help prevent that. In a 2000 trial, people who had had three or more episodes of depression were followed for a year. The relapse rate was 37% for those who took the course and 66% for those who got usual care. For people with fewer episodes, there was no difference.|37% vs 66%|Teasdale et al., 2000
Expressive writing | Writing about a hard experience for 15 to 20 minutes on a few days can help. | In the 1980s James Pennebaker asked people to write about the most upsetting experience of their lives for 15 to 20 minutes on each of several days. Some later showed improvements in health, such as fewer doctor visits. Later reviews found the average effect is small and varies from person to person. Still, it seems safe, and many people find that putting something into words helps them make sense of it.||Pennebaker & Beall, 1986; Frattaroli, 2006|mixed
Scheduled worry time | Set aside 15 to 30 minutes a day to worry, and postpone worries until then. | If you can't stop worrying, one method is to give worry its own appointment. Thomas Borkovec's work in the 1980s led to a technique where you pick a time, say 15 to 30 minutes in the early evening, and postpone worries until then. When one comes up during the day, you note it down and tell yourself "later." Many people find that the urge fades by the time the appointed hour comes.||Borkovec et al., 1983
Motivational interviewing | A style of conversation that helps someone talk themselves into change. | William Miller and Stephen Rollnick developed this style of conversation in the 1980s while working with people with addictions. Arguing for change usually makes people argue back. Instead, the helper asks open questions, listens carefully, and invites the person to talk about both sides of their mixed feelings. When people hear themselves say why they might change, they're more likely to do it. It works well when someone is unsure.||Miller & Rollnick, 1991
The stages of change | People move through readiness stages before a lasting change. | In the 1980s James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente studied how people quit smoking and described a series of stages. In precontemplation they aren't thinking about it. In contemplation they're weighing it up. Then come preparation, action and maintenance. Relapse is common and counts as part of the cycle, not as failure. Researchers debate whether the stages are truly distinct, but the idea that readiness matters is widely used.||Prochaska & DiClemente, 1983|mixed
Self-determination theory | People thrive when three needs are met: autonomy, competence and relatedness. | Edward Deci and Richard Ryan studied what keeps people motivated and found three basic needs. Autonomy is the sense that you're making your own choices. Competence is feeling capable. Relatedness is feeling connected to others. When these needs are met, people are more motivated, more creative and happier. The pattern holds across many cultures. It helps explain why pressure works poorly for long: it takes away autonomy.||Deci & Ryan, 2000
Locus of control | Do you believe your results come from you or from outside forces? | In 1966 Julian Rotter described a difference between people who believe their lives are shaped mainly by their own actions, and those who believe it's mainly luck, fate or other people. An "internal" locus tends to go with better coping, health habits and achievement. But the world isn't fully controllable, and an accurate sense of what's in your power matters too. The useful skill is telling the two apart.||Rotter, 1966
Self-efficacy | Believing you can do a particular task predicts whether you try and keep going. | Albert Bandura introduced the term in 1977 for your belief in your ability to do a specific task. It's different from general confidence. It predicts whether you'll try, how hard you'll work and whether you'll keep going after a setback. It grows mainly from small successes, from watching someone like you succeed, from encouragement and from managing your stress. Setting a goal you can actually reach is a good way to build it.||Bandura, 1977
Learned helplessness | After uncontrollable pain, animals and people can stop trying. | In the 1960s Martin Seligman and Steven Maier gave dogs shocks they couldn't escape. Later, when the dogs could easily avoid shocks by jumping a barrier, many just lay down. It looked like they'd learned that nothing they did mattered. In 2016 Maier and Seligman revised the theory. Passivity is the brain's default response to pain, and what has to be learned is that you do have control. That's a hopeful reading.||Seligman & Maier, 1967; Maier & Seligman, 2016
Explanatory style | How you explain bad events predicts how you cope. | Martin Seligman found that people differ in how they explain bad events to themselves. Pessimists tend to see causes as permanent, personal and pervasive: "It will always be like this, it's my fault, and it ruins everything." Optimists see them as temporary, specific and partly outside their control. Explanatory style predicted sales success at an insurance company and how people recovered from low mood. It can be changed with practice.||Seligman, Learned Optimism (1990)
Resilience is the usual outcome | Most people recover well after a loss or trauma. | George Bonanno studied how people cope with losses like the death of a spouse. Many expected most to go through a long period of deep distress. He found that the most common pattern was a short time of grief followed by a return to normal functioning, and a minority did have prolonged difficulty. Being resilient doesn't mean not hurting. It means that people are, on the whole, sturdier than they expect.||Bonanno, 2004
The dual process model of grief | Healthy grieving swings between facing the loss and managing the rest of life. | In 1999 Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut proposed that grieving well isn't a straight line. People swing back and forth between two kinds of coping. In loss-oriented moments, they face the pain, think about the person and miss them. In restoration-oriented moments, they deal with daily life and new roles, and take breaks from grief. The back and forth is normal and healthy. Taking a break from sadness isn't avoiding it.||Stroebe & Schut, 1999
Prolonged grief disorder | Intense grief that does not ease after a year or more, and gets in the way of living. | Grief is not an illness. Most people find that the sharpest pain eases over time. For some, intense longing and distress continue for a year or more and make daily life difficult. In 2022 the diagnostic manual added prolonged grief disorder for that situation. It is a name for grief that's stuck, not a judgment about how much someone loved. It responds to treatment, which can help people live with the loss.||DSM-5-TR, 2022
Post-traumatic growth | Some people report new strength or meaning after adversity. | In 1996 Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun described how some people report positive changes after a crisis: closer relationships, a greater appreciation of life, new priorities or a stronger sense of purpose. It's a real experience for many. But not everyone has it, and no one should feel they have to. Researchers also point out that people's own reports of growth don't always match how they're actually doing later.||Tedeschi & Calhoun, 1996|mixed
Divorce is a process | A divorce is a series of changes over years, not a single event. | Paul Amato, who has studied divorce for decades, describes it as a long series of changes, not a single event. Conflict before the split, the move, the finances, parenting arrangements and new relationships each affect how people cope. Most adults adjust over time. But the path varies a lot, and the stress people feel depends heavily on what follows the separation, so how things go afterward is partly within your influence.||Amato, 2000
Most children of divorce do fine | The majority of children adapt well, though some face more problems than children in intact families. | E. Mavis Hetherington followed families through divorce for decades in the Virginia Longitudinal Study. About 75 to 80 percent of the children from divorced families were functioning well as adults, compared with about 90 percent of those from intact families. So most children cope, though a minority struggle more. What helped them was steady parenting, low conflict between the parents and a warm relationship with at least one adult.|75-80%|Hetherington & Kelly, 2002
Social support buffers stress | Having people to lean on softens the harm of hard events. | In 1985 Sheldon Cohen and Thomas Wills reviewed the research on how social support affects health. They found that having people to lean on protects against the harm of high stress. What matters most is the sense that if you needed help, someone would be there. That perception may be as protective as actually receiving help. It's one reason that staying in touch with the people who care about you pays off during hard times.||Cohen & Wills, 1985
Loneliness and health | Weak social ties carry a health risk as large as some well-known ones. | In 2010 Julianne Holt-Lunstad and colleagues combined 148 studies covering more than 300,000 people. They found that people with strong social relationships had a 50 percent greater likelihood of survival over the follow-up periods. The size of the effect was comparable with quitting smoking, and larger than that of obesity or inactivity. It's not just that the lonely are sicker to begin with. Connection seems to matter for staying well.|50%|Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010
Gratitude practices | Writing down things you are thankful for gives a small mood boost. | Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough asked people to write weekly about things they were grateful for. In 2003 they reported that, compared with people who wrote about hassles, the gratitude group felt better about their lives. A later review found the average benefit is modest. So it's a helpful habit that costs nothing, though it isn't a cure for depression. It seems to work best if you write about specifics and people, not generalities.||Emmons & McCullough, 2003; Davis et al., 2016|mixed
Three good things | Each night write three good things that happened and why. | Martin Seligman and colleagues asked people to write down three things that went well each day and why, for one week. In a 2005 study, those who did so were happier and less depressed for up to six months. Later studies have found smaller or shorter-lived effects, so the results are mixed. It takes only a few minutes a night, and many people find it shifts their attention toward what's good.||Seligman et al., 2005|mixed
Savoring | Deliberately attending to a pleasant moment makes it last longer. | Fred Bryant and Joseph Veroff, who wrote the book on savoring, describe it as deliberately paying attention to a pleasant experience so that you enjoy it more and it lasts longer. They suggest sharing it with someone, taking mental snapshots, and thinking about it afterward. You can savor the past through memories, the present through full attention, and the future through anticipation. It's a skill that can be practiced.||Bryant & Veroff, 2007
Awe | Standing before something vast can shrink worries and widen your view. | The psychologist Dacher Keltner studies awe, the feeling you get before something vast that changes how you see the world. It could be a mountain range, a piece of music or a big idea. In his research, awe makes people feel smaller in a good way, more connected to others and more generous. It also seems to quiet self-focused worry. You don't need a trip to the Grand Canyon, and walks in nature and looking at the night sky can do it.||Keltner & Haidt, 2003; Piff et al., 2015
Emotional granularity | People who can describe feelings precisely cope better than those who use only "bad". | Lisa Feldman Barrett found that some people describe their feelings with precision, telling "anxious" from "disappointed" from "ashamed," and others lump everything together as "bad" or "awful." The first group copes with stress more flexibly. They seem to have more options, since a precise label points to a particular response. Building your emotional vocabulary can be as simple as pausing to ask what exactly you're feeling.||Barrett et al., 2001
The stress response | The body's alarm makes the heart race and gets muscles ready, whether the danger is a bear or an email. | Walter Cannon, a physiologist at Harvard, described in 1915 how the body prepares for fight or flight. Your heart races, your breathing quickens and your muscles tense. The system evolved for physical danger, but it responds to an urgent email just as well. Freezing is another common response. The good news is that the system can be switched off. Slow breathing, a walk and talking with a friend all tell the body that it's safe to stand down.||Cannon, 1915
Allostatic load | Repeated or long stress wears the body down. | Bruce McEwen introduced the term in the 1990s for the wear and tear that builds up when the body's stress systems are working too much, or never switch off. Over time it can show up as higher blood pressure, weakened immunity and sleep problems. It isn't just about how big a stressor is. How long it lasts and how well you recover also matter, so rest and recovery are part of managing stress, not a reward for finishing it.||McEwen, 1998
Burnout | A state of exhaustion, cynicism and a sense of ineffectiveness, most often from chronic work stress. | Christina Maslach, a psychologist, developed the best-known measure of burnout in the early 1980s. It has three parts: exhaustion, cynicism or detachment from the work, and a feeling of being ineffective. It usually grows out of chronic stress at work, including overload, little control and a lack of recognition. The World Health Organization calls it an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition. It's often addressed by changing the conditions, not just the person.||Maslach & Jackson, 1981; WHO ICD-11
The impostor phenomenon | Successful people who believe they are frauds about to be found out. | In 1978 Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes described high-achieving women who felt like frauds who'd fooled everyone and were about to be found out. Later work showed it happens to men too, and it's very common. It isn't a diagnosis. People who feel it often attribute success to luck and discount their abilities. One of the best remedies is talking about it with others, since you'll often discover that people you admire feel the same.||Clance & Imes, 1978
Cyclic sighing | Two inhales through the nose and a long exhale through the mouth, for five minutes a day, lifted mood. | The technique is two breaths in through the nose, the second a small top-up, followed by a long slow breath out through the mouth. In a 2023 Stanford trial with 108 people, those who did five minutes of it a day for a month improved their mood and lowered their resting breathing rate more than people who did mindfulness meditation. It's simple to try, and the long exhale seems to be what calms the body.|5 minutes|Balban et al., 2023
Therapy works | The average person who receives therapy ends up better off than most who don't. | In 1977 Mary Lee Smith and Gene Glass combined the results of 375 studies of psychotherapy in one of the first meta-analyses. They found that the average person who had therapy ended up better off than about 75 percent of people who hadn't. Later reviews have reached similar conclusions. It doesn't mean every therapy works for every person. It does mean that, on average, talking with a trained person helps.|75%|Smith & Glass, 1977; Wampold, 2015
The therapeutic alliance | The bond between client and therapist predicts results across kinds of therapy. | Researchers have found that the quality of the bond between client and therapist, including agreement on the goals and the tasks of therapy, predicts how well the treatment works. A 2018 review of nearly 300 studies found a consistent link, whatever the type of therapy. So if you're thinking about seeing someone, it matters that you feel understood and comfortable. It's fine to try a different therapist if the first isn't a good fit.||Flückiger et al., 2018
The Dodo bird verdict | Different therapies often work about equally well. | In Alice in Wonderland, after a race, the Dodo declares, "Everybody has won, and all must have prizes." In 1936 Saul Rosenzweig borrowed the line to describe a puzzling finding that different forms of psychotherapy seem to work about equally well. Later researchers, including Lester Luborsky, found similar results. Not everyone agrees. For some conditions, such as phobias, particular treatments do better. But the overall similarity suggests that shared ingredients, such as the relationship, matter a lot.||Luborsky et al., 1975; Wampold, 2015|mixed
Defense mechanisms | Mental habits that protect us from painful feelings. | In 1936 Anna Freud, Sigmund's daughter, described the mind's habits for protecting us from painful feelings, such as denial, projection, displacement and rationalization. The psychiatrist George Vaillant later ranked them from immature, like denial, to mature, like humor and sublimation. The ideas are useful for describing how people cope, and many therapists use them. But the research behind them is mostly observation, not controlled experiment.||A. Freud, 1936; Vaillant, 1977|mixed
Displacement | Taking frustration out on a safer target. | When we can't safely express a feeling at its source, we sometimes aim it at someone safer. A man is humiliated by his boss, says nothing, and then snaps at his family when he gets home. It's one of the classic defense mechanisms. Spotting it can help. When your reaction seems bigger than the situation, ask yourself who or what the feeling is really about. Often the answer is somewhere else.||A. Freud, 1936|mixed
Freud's legacy | Freud shaped how we talk about the mind, but most of his specific theories are not supported. | Sigmund Freud shaped the vocabulary of psychology, and we still talk about the unconscious, repression and the importance of early childhood. Some of those ideas hold up in modified forms. Others, such as the Oedipus complex or the idea that dreams are disguised wishes, have not been supported. A big problem is that many of his claims can't be tested, since any result can be explained within the theory. He's more important as a historical figure than as a scientific authority.||Standard psychology reference|contested
The Myers-Briggs test | Sorts people into 16 types, but it is not a reliable scientific tool. | The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator sorts people into 16 types, such as INFJ or ESTP, from four either-or choices. It's very popular in workplaces. But scientists generally don't regard it as reliable. In some studies, about half of people got a different type when they retook the test five weeks later. The reason is that traits like introversion fall along a continuum, and the test cuts it in the middle. The Big Five model has a much better record.||Pittenger, 1993; McCrae & Costa, 1989|contested
The Big Five | Five broad personality traits: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism. | After decades of research, many personality psychologists settled on five broad dimensions: openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism. Researchers have found the same five in many countries and languages. They stay fairly stable through adult life and predict real outcomes, such as job performance, relationship satisfaction and health. Unlike the Myers-Briggs types, each is measured on a scale, so most people are somewhere in the middle.||Costa & McCrae, 1992
Traits are partly heritable | About half of the differences in personality and many behaviors trace to genes. | In 2015 Tinca Polderman and colleagues combined nearly fifty years of twin studies, covering over 14 million pairs of twins. Across all the traits measured, about 49 percent of the differences between people could be traced to genes. The other half came from the environment, mostly the kind that siblings in the same family don't share. It means neither genes nor upbringing alone decide who we become.|49%|Polderman et al., 2015
The Flynn effect | Average IQ scores rose about 3 points per decade through the 20th century. | James Flynn discovered that the average score on IQ tests rose about three points every decade through most of the 20th century, in many countries. That's a big change in a few generations, too fast to be genetic. Possible causes include more schooling, better nutrition, and growing familiarity with abstract thinking and tests. In some countries, scores have stopped rising or have even gone down in recent years, and experts aren't sure why.|+3 per decade|Flynn, 1987
Multiple intelligences | Howard Gardner proposed several separate types of intelligence. | In 1983 the psychologist Howard Gardner proposed that people have several separate kinds of intelligence, such as musical, bodily, interpersonal and logical. The theory is very popular among teachers. But researchers haven't found good evidence that the types are independent. People who score high on one kind of mental test tend to score higher on others, which supports the idea of a general factor. The ideas are better seen as a way of appreciating different strengths.||Gardner, 1983; Visser et al., 2006|contested
The marshmallow test | Did a child's wait for a second marshmallow predict their success? | In the 1960s and 70s Walter Mischel offered young children a choice: one marshmallow now, or two if they waited. Follow-up studies suggested that the ones who waited did better in life. In 2018 Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan and Haonan Quan repeated it with a larger and more varied group. After controlling for family background, the link was about half as strong, and mostly disappeared. Waiting seems to reflect circumstances as much as willpower.||Mischel et al., 1972; Watts, Duncan & Quan, 2018|mixed
Grit | Passion and perseverance for long-term goals. | In 2007 Angela Duckworth and colleagues defined grit as passion and perseverance for long-term goals, and found it predicted who stayed at West Point and who won the national spelling bee. Later reviews found that grit overlaps a lot with conscientiousness, a long-known trait, and predicts success only modestly. Perseverance does matter, but the idea that grit is a separate and powerful predictor has been oversold.||Duckworth et al., 2007; Credé et al., 2017|mixed
Maslow's hierarchy | Needs from food and safety up to meaning, arranged as a pyramid. | In 1943 Abraham Maslow proposed that human needs form a pyramid, from physical needs and safety at the bottom to belonging, esteem and self-fulfillment at the top. The idea that needs matter is useful. The strict order isn't supported. A survey across more than 120 countries found that people pursue love and purpose even when basic needs aren't fully met. The pyramid is better thought of as a rough guide.||Maslow, 1943; Tay & Diener, 2011|mixed
The self-esteem myth | Boosting self-esteem does not by itself improve grades or performance. | In the 1980s and 90s it was widely believed that raising children's self-esteem would improve grades and behavior. In 2003 Roy Baumeister and colleagues reviewed the research. They found self-esteem tends to follow success more than cause it, and boosting it directly did little for performance. Praise for effort and strategy works better than praise for being smart, because it teaches that the outcome depends on what you do.||Baumeister et al., 2003|mixed
The Milgram experiments | Ordinary people obeyed instructions to give what they thought were painful shocks. | In 1961 Stanley Milgram, at Yale, had volunteers play the part of a teacher who gave electric shocks to a "learner" for wrong answers, with the shocks increasing to a dangerous 450 volts. The shocks were fake, and the learner was an actor. In the baseline version, 65 percent of participants went all the way to the maximum. Later analyses question how realistic the setup was. Still, it showed how far ordinary people may go under instruction from an authority.|65%|Milgram, 1963; Haslam et al., 2014|mixed
The Asch conformity experiments | People gave obviously wrong answers to match a group. | In the early 1950s Solomon Asch asked people to say which of three lines matched a standard line, an easy task. But everyone else in the room was an actor and had been told to give the wrong answer. About 75 percent of the participants went along with the group at least once, and about a third of all their answers conformed. If one other person gave the right answer, conformity dropped sharply. A single ally makes it easier to speak up.|75%|Asch, 1951
The Stanford prison experiment | A famous 1971 study said roles turn ordinary students into cruel guards. | In 1971 Philip Zimbardo had college students play guards and prisoners in a mock prison in a basement. It was said to show that the situation turns ordinary people cruel. But later evidence showed that the guards were coached on how to behave, and some participants were acting. Most psychologists now regard it as a vivid demonstration, not proof of what happens naturally. It shows how powerful a setup can be, but it's a shaky basis for conclusions.||Zimbardo, 1971; Le Texier, 2019|contested
The Robbers Cave experiment | Two groups of boys at a camp became hostile; shared goals ended it. | In 1954 Muzafer Sherif ran a summer camp for twenty-two boys in Oklahoma. He split them into two groups, the Rattlers and the Eagles, and set up competitions with prizes. Hostility rose quickly. Later he created problems that could only be solved by working together, such as fixing the water supply, and the animosity eased. The study suggests that rivalry creates conflict and shared goals reduce it.||Sherif et al., 1954|mixed
The foot-in-the-door effect | People who agree to a small request are more likely to agree to a larger one later. | In 1966 Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser asked homeowners to put a large, ugly "Drive Carefully" sign on their lawns. Only about 17 percent agreed. But when another group had earlier been asked to display a small sign, and had agreed, 76 percent said yes to the large one. A small yes changes how you see yourself, as someone who helps with this kind of thing, and it makes the next request easier.||Freedman & Fraser, 1966
The door-in-the-face effect | After refusing a big request, people are more likely to agree to a smaller one. | In 1975 Robert Cialdini and his colleagues asked college students to spend two years as volunteer counselors for juvenile offenders. Almost everyone said no. When the same students were then asked to spend two hours taking the kids to the zoo, about half agreed, compared with 17 percent of students who were asked only about the zoo trip. After turning down a big request, people feel a pull to meet you partway.||Cialdini et al., 1975
Cialdini's principles | Six levers of influence: reciprocity, commitment, social proof, authority, liking and scarcity. | In his 1984 book Influence, Robert Cialdini described six levers that make people say yes: reciprocity (we repay favors), commitment (we stay consistent), social proof (we follow others), authority, liking and scarcity. He worked out the list by training with salespeople, fundraisers and advertisers. Knowing them doesn't make you immune. But it helps you notice when someone is pulling one, and ask yourself whether you'd agree without it.||Cialdini, Influence (1984)
The Hawthorne effect | People change their behavior when they know they are watched. | The name comes from experiments in the 1920s and 30s at the Hawthorne Works, a Western Electric factory near Chicago. Workers' output seemed to rise when lighting was changed, whichever way, which was taken to mean people work harder when they're being watched. In 2011 Steven Levitt and John List went back to the original data and found little evidence of such an effect in those studies. Being observed may change behavior, but this famous example isn't solid.||Levitt & List, 2011|contested
Rosenhan's pseudopatients | A 1973 study claimed healthy people faked symptoms to enter psychiatric hospitals and were not detected. | In 1973 David Rosenhan published "On Being Sane in Insane Places." He said that eight healthy people faked a symptom, hearing voices, to be admitted to psychiatric hospitals, and then acted normally but weren't discovered. A 2019 investigation by the journalist Susannah Cahalan found evidence that he misreported some of the data and details. Doubts about how reliably psychiatric diagnosis was made then were reasonable, but the study itself isn't trustworthy.||Rosenhan, 1973; Cahalan, 2019|contested
Harlow's monkeys | Baby monkeys preferred a soft cloth mother to a wire one that gave milk. | In the 1950s Harry Harlow raised baby rhesus monkeys with two artificial mothers: one made of wire that held a bottle, and one covered in soft cloth that didn't. The babies clung to the cloth mother and went to the wire one only to feed. It showed that attachment grows from comfort and contact, not just from food. It changed how child-care experts thought about babies, though the experiments were cruel and would not be done today.||Harlow, 1958
The Strange Situation | A short lab procedure that shows a baby's attachment style. | In the 1970s Mary Ainsworth devised a short laboratory test. A one-year-old is in a playroom with a parent, then a stranger comes in, the parent leaves, and then comes back. What the baby does at the reunion shows a pattern. About 65 percent were securely attached, upset when the parent left and quickly comforted on return. The rest showed avoidant or anxious patterns. The test has been used for decades to study attachment.|About 65%|Ainsworth et al., 1978
Adult attachment styles | Attachment patterns may show up in how we handle closeness as adults. | In 1987 Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver proposed that adult romantic relationships follow the same patterns as the bond between a baby and a caregiver. People are described as secure, anxious or avoidant in how they handle closeness. Attachment styles are partly stable but not fixed, and they can change with new relationships or therapy. Researchers now measure them on spectrums, not as fixed types.||Hazan & Shaver, 1987|mixed
Romanian orphans | Early severe deprivation harms development, and earlier rescue helps more. | After the fall of the Ceaușescu regime in 1989, thousands of Romanian children were found in overcrowded orphanages with very little care. The English and Romanian Adoptees study followed those who were adopted into British families. Children adopted before six months of age mostly caught up. Those adopted later had more lasting difficulties with attention, relationships and thinking. It showed that severe early neglect has real effects, and that earlier rescue helps.||Rutter et al., 2007
Classical conditioning | A neutral signal that is paired with something that matters comes to trigger the same response. | Ivan Pavlov, a Russian physiologist studying digestion, noticed that his dogs started to salivate when they heard the footsteps of the assistant who fed them. He then paired a bell with food and found the bell alone would produce salivation. A neutral signal that's linked with something meaningful comes to trigger the same response. The same thing happens in people: a song linked to a memory can bring back the feeling.||Pavlov, 1927
Operant conditioning | Behavior followed by reward becomes more likely; followed by punishment, less likely. | In 1898 Edward Thorndike put cats in puzzle boxes and found that actions that led to escape were repeated. He called it the law of effect. B. F. Skinner developed the idea in the 1930s with his "Skinner box." Behavior that's followed by something rewarding becomes more likely, and behavior followed by something unpleasant becomes less likely. Rewards usually change behavior faster and more lastingly than punishment does.||Thorndike, 1898; Skinner, 1938
Variable reinforcement | Unpredictable rewards produce the most persistent habits. | B. F. Skinner found that how often you reward matters. When rewards come on an unpredictable schedule, like a slot machine, animals keep pressing the lever the longest, even after the rewards stop altogether. We can see the same thing in many places: checking your phone to see whether anything new has arrived, or pulling to refresh a feed. The unpredictability makes the habit stick, which is why it's so hard to break.||Ferster & Skinner, 1957
Extinction | A learned response fades when the reward or the pairing stops, but it can come back. | When a learned response stops being rewarded, it fades. A dog stops salivating to a bell that no longer predicts food. But it's not erased. After a rest, it often returns, which is called spontaneous recovery. In people, a fear or habit that has faded can return with stress or in a new place. Researchers like Mark Bouton find that practicing the new learning in many settings makes it last longer.||Bouton, 2004
Little Albert | A 1920 experiment on conditioning fear in an infant. | In 1920 John Watson and Rosalie Rayner at Johns Hopkins showed a nine-month-old baby a white rat while making a loud noise behind him. After several pairings, the baby cried at the sight of the rat, and at other furry things. It's a famous example of conditioned fear. It's also an ethically troubling study with weak methods, and later accounts of what it proved are often exaggerated. It wouldn't be allowed now.||Watson & Rayner, 1920|mixed
The Bobo doll | Children copied adults' aggression toward an inflatable doll. | In 1961 Albert Bandura and his colleagues showed nursery-school children an adult either hitting a large inflatable doll or ignoring it. When the children were later left alone with the doll, those who had seen the adult attack it copied the same actions and even the same words. The study showed that children can learn by watching, without being rewarded themselves. It started a long debate on media violence.||Bandura, Ross & Ross, 1961
Cognitive maps | Rats and people build mental maps, not just chains of habits. | In 1948 Edward Tolman showed that rats don't simply learn a chain of left and right turns. After they'd learned a maze, he blocked the usual route. Instead of getting confused, they chose a new route in the direction of the goal, as if they had a map in their heads. The finding was important because it showed that animals, and so people, build mental representations of space and not only habits.||Tolman, 1948
Latent learning | We learn without any reward and show it only when it is useful. | In 1930 Edward Tolman and Charles Honzik put hungry rats in a maze. One group was fed at the end every time and got better quickly. A second group was never fed. A third wasn't fed until the eleventh day, and then suddenly performed as well as the rewarded rats. They had been learning the layout all along but showed it only when it became worth showing. We learn a lot without reward.||Tolman & Honzik, 1930
Contact comfort and touch | Warm touch lowers stress responses in people. | In 2006 James Coan and colleagues put married women in a brain scanner and told them they might get a mild electric shock. Those who held their husband's hand showed less activity in brain areas linked with threat than those who held a stranger's hand or no one's. Physical contact with someone you trust seems to calm the body's alarm system, and the effect was bigger in happier marriages.||Coan, Schaefer & Davidson, 2006
