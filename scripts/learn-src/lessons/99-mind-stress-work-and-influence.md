# Stress and the speed of healing
@src Kiecolt-Glaser et al., Archives of General Psychiatry, 2005; Kiecolt-Glaser et al., 1995

Can an argument slow the healing of a cut? In 2005 Janice Kiecolt-Glaser and her colleagues at Ohio State University invited married couples to the laboratory, where a small blister was raised on each person's arm. Then the couples spent time talking, once about a topic that caused conflict and once about a way in which they supported each other. The researchers checked the blisters each day, and measured chemical signals of inflammation in the blood.

The wounds healed more slowly after the conflict than after the support talk. The effect was greatest in the couples who were most hostile in their talk. Their wounds healed at about 60 percent of the rate of the others, and they had higher levels of a signal of inflammation called IL-6. A study in 1995 found that women caring for a relative with dementia took about 24 percent longer to heal a standard wound. The samples were small, but they show that stress and conflict can reach the body.

# The five stages that were not a map
@conf mixed
@src Kübler-Ross, On Death and Dying, 1969; Bonanno, 2004

In 1969 the psychiatrist Elisabeth Kübler-Ross wrote that people who are dying pass through five stages. They are denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance. The idea was soon applied to people who are grieving, and it became one of the best known ideas in psychology. Many people have been told that they should expect to pass through the stages, in order, and have worried when their own grief did not fit.

The model rested on limited evidence, which was a series of interviews. Kübler-Ross herself later said that the stages are not a linear or predictable progression. George Bonanno followed people from before they were bereaved, and found that the most common pattern was resilience, in which people grieved but kept functioning. Longer and more severe grief affected a minority, perhaps one in ten. Grief does not come in a fixed order, and there is no single right way to do it.

# What makes work satisfying
@conf mixed
@src Herzberg, Mausner & Snyderman, The Motivation to Work, 1959

What makes people satisfied at work, and what makes them unhappy? In the 1950s the psychologist Frederick Herzberg and his colleagues interviewed 203 engineers and accountants in Pittsburgh. They asked each person to recall a time when they felt exceptionally good about their job, and a time when they felt exceptionally bad, and to say what had caused it.

The good times were linked to achievement, recognition, interesting work and responsibility, which he called motivators. The bad times were linked to things like company policy, supervision, pay and working conditions, which he called hygiene factors. His point was that removing a cause of unhappiness does not create satisfaction. Critics noted that people tend to credit themselves for good events and blame their surroundings for bad ones, and later studies using other methods did not find the clean split. Still, the idea has shaped how managers think.

# Theory X and Theory Y
@conf mixed
@src McGregor, The Human Side of Enterprise, 1960

In 1960 the management professor Douglas McGregor described two sets of beliefs that managers hold about their workers. He called them Theory X and Theory Y. A manager who holds Theory X believes that people dislike work, avoid responsibility, and have to be controlled, directed and threatened. A manager who holds Theory Y believes that people find work natural, will direct themselves toward goals that they accept, and will seek responsibility.

His main argument was that a manager's beliefs shape what happens. A manager who assumes the worst will supervise closely, and workers will do just what they are told, which seems to prove the belief. A manager who assumes the best will give people freedom, and most will repay it. McGregor offered it as a way of thinking, and not as a result of experiments, and it has been criticized as too simple. But it is a useful question to ask about oneself.

# What makes people try
@conf mixed
@src Vroom, Work and Motivation, 1964

Why do people put effort into some tasks, and not others? In 1964 the psychologist Victor Vroom proposed that a person asks three questions, usually without being aware of it. Can I do it, if I try, which is expectancy. If I do it, will I get a reward, which is instrumentality. And do I care about the reward, which is valence. The motivation is the product of the three, so if any one of them is zero, the motivation is zero.

It explains why a bonus does not work if people think they cannot reach it, or if they do not trust that it will be paid, or if they do not value it. Studies have found that the three parts do predict effort, though not very strongly. It is a useful checklist for anyone who wants to understand why a person is not trying. Often the answer is that one of the three questions has a no for an answer.

# Three styles of leading a club
@conf mixed
@src Lewin, Lippitt & White, Journal of Social Psychology, 1939

In 1938 the psychologist Kurt Lewin and his students at the University of Iowa set up after-school clubs for ten-year-old boys, who made masks and did other crafts. Each club had an adult leader who had been trained to lead in one of three ways. The authoritarian leader gave orders and decided everything. The democratic leader let the group discuss and choose. The laissez-faire leader gave almost no direction.

The boys under the authoritarian leader were either passive and obedient, or hostile to one another, and work tended to stop when the leader left the room. The boys in the democratic club were friendlier, and kept working when the leader was away. The clubs were few and small, and the study is better known than it is rigorous. But it was among the first experiments to show that the style of a leader changes how a group behaves.

# Why some people go along, and some agree
@src Kelman, Journal of Conflict Resolution, 1958

When people change their behavior because of someone else, are they changing their minds? In 1958 the psychologist Herbert Kelman said that there are three different processes, and that they have different effects. The first is compliance, in which a person goes along to gain a reward or avoid a punishment. They show it in public, but they do not believe it, and they stop when no one is watching.

The second is identification, in which a person adopts a view in order to be like someone they admire, or to keep a relationship. It lasts as long as the relationship does. The third is internalization, in which the person accepts the view because it fits their own values, and it lasts without any outside support. It matters because if you want a lasting change in someone, pressure and charm are weaker than reasons that they can make their own.

# Six parts of a good life
@src Ryff, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1989

Is a good life the same as a happy one? In 1989 the psychologist Carol Ryff argued that well-being has more parts than a feeling of pleasure. She went through writings of philosophers and psychologists, and from them she drew six dimensions. They are self-acceptance, positive relations with others, autonomy, mastery of one's surroundings, purpose in life, and personal growth.

She built a questionnaire for them, which has been used with thousands of people in many countries. People can score high on one dimension and low on another, so they do not always go together. Her findings include that purpose and personal growth tend to drop in older age, while mastery of one's surroundings and autonomy rise. The scale gives us a way to ask more questions than how happy a person feels.

# What the policemen did at Józefów
@conf mixed
@src Browning, Ordinary Men, 1992

On the morning of July 13, 1942, in the Polish village of Józefów, the men of Reserve Police Battalion 101 were told by their commander, Major Wilhelm Trapp, that they were to round up the Jews of the village. The men were mostly middle-aged, from Hamburg, and not trained soldiers. Trapp, who was visibly distressed, then did something unusual. He offered that any older man who did not feel up to the task could step out.

Only a small number stepped out. Most of the others took part in the killing of some 1,500 people that day. The historian Christopher Browning, in his book Ordinary Men, argued that the men were not fanatics, and that the forces at work included conformity, pressure from comrades, deference to authority and ambition. Other historians, such as Daniel Goldhagen, argued that antisemitism was the main cause. It remains one of the most studied cases of how ordinary people do terrible things.
