# The Peace of Westphalia
@src Standard European history

The treaties signed in 1648 in the German region of Westphalia ended the Thirty Years' War, one of the most destructive wars in European history, which killed perhaps a quarter of the people in the German lands. Whole regions had been laid waste, and armies had lived off the land.

The agreements said that each ruler would control his own territory, without outside interference, an idea that's often seen as the beginning of the modern system of sovereign states. The war had started as a religious conflict, but it ended as a political one. After it, the principle that a country's borders and its internal affairs are its own business became the basic rule of how nations deal with one another.

# The Great Fire of London
@src Porter, 2008

In September 1666 a fire that began in a baker's shop in Pudding Lane burned for four days and destroyed about 13,000 houses and 87 churches, including St Paul's Cathedral. The city was made largely of timber, and a dry summer and strong wind helped the flames to spread.

Only six deaths were officially recorded, though the true number may have been higher, since the poor weren't always counted. The disaster led to new building rules, which required brick and stone, and to the first fire insurance companies. The diarist Samuel Pepys buried his wine and a wheel of cheese in his garden to protect them. The architect Christopher Wren designed a new St Paul's, which still stands, and the city that rose from the ashes was safer.

# The social contract thinkers
@src Hobbes, Leviathan; Locke, Two Treatises

Why should we obey a government? Three philosophers gave different answers. In 1651 Thomas Hobbes argued that people give up some of their freedom in return for safety. Without a strong ruler, he said, life would be "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short."

In 1689 John Locke said that government exists to protect people's rights, and can be replaced if it fails to. In 1762 Jean-Jacques Rousseau said that legitimate government must express the general will of the people. Their ideas shaped the American and French revolutions and the way we think about political power. Each started from the same question of why free people would agree to be governed, and each gave a different answer.

# Two Enlightenment ideas: separated powers and daring to know
@src Montesquieu, 1748; Kant, 1784

In 1748 the French thinker Montesquieu published The Spirit of the Laws, in which he argued that liberty is safest when government power is divided among separate branches, so that each can check the others. His idea strongly influenced the framers of the US Constitution, who divided the government into executive, legislative and judicial branches.

In 1784 the German philosopher Immanuel Kant answered the question "What is Enlightenment?" in a short essay. He said that enlightenment is people's emergence from their self-imposed immaturity, which he defined as the inability to use one's own understanding without someone else's guidance. His motto was "Sapere aude," or "Dare to know," a phrase that he took from the Roman poet Horace. Together, the two ideas describe a world in which power is checked and people think for themselves.

# The Declaration of Independence
@src Declaration of Independence

On July 4, 1776, the Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence, which stated that "all men are created equal." Thomas Jefferson wrote most of the text, and Congress edited it. It was addressed partly to King George III, and partly to the world.

At the time, the words didn't extend to enslaved people or to women. Later movements, from abolitionists to suffragists to civil rights leaders, used them to demand that the promise be kept. Abraham Lincoln, in 1863, said that the nation was "dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal." The document is a statement of an ideal that the country has been working to meet, imperfectly, ever since.

# The US Constitution
@src Standard US history

In the summer of 1787 delegates met in Philadelphia, in secret, to write a new framework of government for the United States. They kept the windows shut in the heat, to keep the debates from being overheard. They debated for about four months, and the final document is only about 4,500 words long.

It has been amended 27 times, with the first ten amendments, known as the Bill of Rights, added in 1791. It's the oldest written national constitution still in use. Its brevity and its method of amendment are often seen as the reasons it has survived. It sets out the structure of government and leaves much to later generations, who have applied its words to situations that its authors could never have imagined.

# The French Revolution
@src Standard European history

On July 14, 1789, a Paris crowd stormed the Bastille, a royal prison and a symbol of tyranny, and the French Revolution began. Later that summer, the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen set out ideas of equality and liberty.

The revolution overthrew the monarchy and later descended into the Terror, in which thousands were guillotined, and then the rule of Napoleon. It spread ideas of equal citizenship and national sovereignty across Europe. France still celebrates July 14 as its national day. The revolution is both inspiring and dreadful, and a warning that great ideals can be turned to terrible ends. It changed the way people thought about government, whether they liked it or not.

# The Haitian Revolution
@src James, The Black Jacobins (1938)

In 1791 enslaved people in the French colony of Saint-Domingue rose up, in what became the only successful slave revolt in history to found a nation. Led first by Toussaint Louverture, and after his capture in 1802 by Jean-Jacques Dessalines, they defeated French, Spanish and British forces.

Haiti declared independence on January 1, 1804, becoming the first Black-led republic and the second independent nation in the Americas. France later demanded a huge payment in exchange for recognizing it, which burdened the country for generations. The revolution frightened slave-owners everywhere, and inspired many others. It deserves to be better known as one of the pivotal events of the age of revolutions.

# Waterloo
@src Standard European history

On June 18, 1815, near the village of Waterloo in what is now Belgium, Napoleon's army was defeated by British and allied forces under the Duke of Wellington and by Prussian forces under Marshal Blücher. It was a close fight, and Wellington later called it "the nearest-run thing you ever saw in your life."

It ended 23 years of nearly constant war in Europe. Napoleon was exiled to the remote island of St. Helena, in the South Atlantic, where he died in 1821. Europe then enjoyed a relatively long peace, and the word "Waterloo" has since come to mean a decisive defeat. The battle is a reminder of how much depended, on a muddy field, on a few hours and a few decisions.

# Steam: Watt's engine and the first railways
@src Watt, 1769; Standard history of technology

Steam engines existed before James Watt, but they wasted most of their heat. In 1769 Watt, a Scottish instrument maker, patented a design with a separate condenser, which made the engine far more efficient. With the help of his business partner Matthew Boulton, steam power spread to mills, mines and later to railways and ships, and powered the Industrial Revolution.

The Stockton and Darlington Railway in northern England opened in 1825, using George Stephenson's steam locomotive to haul coal and passengers. Within a few decades, railways spread across the world. By 1850 Britain had about 6,000 miles of track. Trains made it necessary to standardize time, since each town had previously kept its own local time. The unit of power, the watt, is named for the man who started it all.

# Ending the slave trade
@src Hochschild, Bury the Chains (2005)

In 1807 Britain banned its involvement in the transatlantic slave trade, and in 1833 it abolished slavery throughout its empire. The United States abolished it with the 13th Amendment in 1865. Millions of people had been taken from Africa and sold into bondage, and the trade had been one of the biggest sources of wealth of the age.

Campaigners like William Wilberforce, Olaudah Equiano and Thomas Clarkson used pamphlets, petitions and a boycott of slave-grown sugar to turn public opinion, and the enslaved people themselves resisted constantly. Hundreds of thousands of Britons signed petitions, in a time when most could not vote. It was one of the first great campaigns of ordinary people to change a law on moral grounds.

# Two books on liberty: Mill and Wollstonecraft
@src Mill, On Liberty (1859); Wollstonecraft, 1792

In his 1859 book On Liberty, the English philosopher John Stuart Mill argued that society may justly limit a person's freedom only to prevent harm to others. A person's own good, he said, isn't enough reason to force them. He defended free speech even for opinions that are wrong, because a false idea may contain some truth, and because truth that's never challenged becomes a dead dogma.

In 1792 Mary Wollstonecraft published A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, in which she argued that women appear to be less rational than men only because they're denied an education. She called for girls and boys to be taught the same things. It was widely criticized at the time, and later became one of the founding texts of feminism. Mill himself went on to write The Subjection of Women, in 1869.

# The telegraph, the telephone and the light bulb
@src Morse, 1844; US Patent 174,465

On May 24, 1844, Samuel Morse sent a message from the Capitol in Washington to Baltimore, 40 miles away: "What hath God wrought." Until then, news could travel only as fast as a horse, a train or a ship. The telegraph made communication almost instantaneous. By 1866, a cable laid under the Atlantic connected America and Europe.

Alexander Graham Bell filed for a patent for the telephone on February 14, 1876, the same day that another inventor, Elisha Gray, filed a notice for a similar device. In 1879 Thomas Edison perfected a practical electric light bulb with a long-lasting filament, and the British inventor Joseph Swan had made a working bulb at about the same time. Inventions often arrive in more than one place at once.

# The Red Cross
@src Dunant, 1862

In 1859 a Swiss businessman, Henry Dunant, happened to be near the battlefield of Solferino in Italy, and was horrified by the thousands of wounded left without help. He organized local villagers to care for them, regardless of which side the soldiers were on.

He wrote a book about it, and in 1863 helped to found the Red Cross. The first Geneva Convention in 1864 agreed that the wounded and those who care for them should be protected in war. Dunant later went bankrupt and lived in poverty, but in 1901 he shared the first Nobel Peace Prize. The Red Cross and the Geneva Conventions remain a basic part of how the world tries to limit the cruelty of war.

# Women's right to vote
@src Standard history

In 1893 New Zealand became the first self-governing country to give women the vote in national elections. Others followed slowly. In the United States, the 19th Amendment gave women the vote in 1920, and in France, women could not vote until 1944.

Suffragists campaigned with petitions, marches and, sometimes, hunger strikes in prison. In Britain, women over 30 got the vote in 1918, and women over 21 in 1928. Switzerland did not give women the vote in national elections until 1971. It took decades of work and sacrifice, and it's a reminder that rights we take for granted were won by people who refused to give up. The change was one of the biggest in the history of democracy.

# The Wright brothers
@src Library of Congress

On December 17, 1903, at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, Orville Wright made the first powered, controlled flight in a heavier-than-air machine. It lasted 12 seconds and covered 120 feet. Later that day, Wilbur flew 852 feet in 59 seconds.

Just 66 years afterward, humans walked on the Moon. The brothers were bicycle makers from Ohio, and they built their own wind tunnel to test wing shapes. They'd figured out how to control a plane in the air, which was the real breakthrough, and not just how to get it off the ground. A piece of the original wing fabric traveled to the Moon with Apollo 11, as a tribute to them.

# The Titanic
@src British Wreck Commissioner's inquiry

The luxury liner Titanic, widely described as unsinkable, hit an iceberg on the night of April 14, 1912, on its first voyage, and sank in under three hours. About 1,500 of the 2,200 people aboard died, partly because there were lifeboats for only about half of them.

After the disaster, new rules required ships to carry enough lifeboats for everyone and to keep radios staffed around the clock. The International Ice Patrol was set up to watch for icebergs in the North Atlantic. The ship had met the rules of the time, which was part of the problem, because the regulations hadn't kept up with the size of ships. It's remembered as a story of human pride and of the heroism of some of the people on board.

# World War I
@src Standard history

The Great War lasted from 1914 to 1918 and killed about 20 million people, soldiers and civilians. It began after the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria in Sarajevo on June 28, 1914, and drew in the great powers through a web of alliances.

It saw the first large-scale use of tanks, poison gas and aircraft in war, and trench warfare that bogged down fronts for years. The fighting ended at 11 a.m. on November 11, 1918, which is why Veterans Day and Armistice Day are observed on that date. The harsh peace settlement that followed helped set the stage for another war. People called it "the war to end all wars," and sadly, it wasn't.

# The Russian Revolution
@src Standard history

In 1917 two revolutions overthrew Russia's government. In February, strikes and protests forced Tsar Nicholas II to abdicate. In October, the Bolsheviks under Vladimir Lenin seized power from a weak provisional government.

A bitter civil war followed, and in 1922 the Soviet Union was formally founded. It would last nearly 70 years and shape the 20th century. The Russian calendar at that time was thirteen days behind the one used in the West, so the "October" revolution actually happened in November by the Western calendar. The events of 1917 inspired revolutionaries around the world and frightened many governments, and they led to the long rivalry between the Soviet Union and the West.

# The 1918 flu pandemic
@src CDC; Johnson & Mueller, 2002

The influenza pandemic of 1918 to 1920 killed at least 50 million people worldwide, more than the First World War, and about a third of the world's population caught it. It's called the Spanish flu only because Spain, neutral in the war, reported freely on it, while other countries censored the news.

Unusually, it killed many healthy young adults. Troop movements at the end of the war helped it to spread. Cities that adopted measures such as closing schools and banning gatherings early had lower death rates. In 2005, scientists reconstructed the virus from preserved tissue samples. The pandemic is a lesson in how quickly an infection can spread in a connected world, and in the value of honest reporting.

# The Great Depression
@src Standard US history

In October 1929 the US stock market crashed, and the world sank into the worst economic downturn of the 20th century. By 1933 about a quarter of American workers were unemployed, and thousands of banks failed.

President Franklin Roosevelt's New Deal brought programs such as Social Security, bank deposit insurance and new labor laws, many of which remain in place. Banks were closed for a "bank holiday" in 1933, and Roosevelt spoke on the radio to calm the public. The Depression lasted until the Second World War, and it left lasting changes in how governments manage the economy. It's why bank accounts are insured, and why many people of that generation were thrifty all their lives.

# World War II
@src Standard history

The war that ran from 1939 to 1945 was the deadliest in history, killing about 70 to 85 million people. It began when Germany invaded Poland on September 1, 1939, and eventually involved most of the world's nations.

More than half of those who died were civilians, many of them in the Soviet Union and China. It ended with the surrender of Germany in May 1945 and Japan in August. The war transformed the world. It ended the old European empires, set up the United States and the Soviet Union as superpowers, and led to the creation of the United Nations. Those who lived through it often spoke little about it, and its lessons are still at the heart of how we think about war and peace.

# The Holocaust
@src US Holocaust Memorial Museum

Between 1941 and 1945 Nazi Germany and its collaborators murdered about six million Jews, along with millions of others, including Roma, disabled people, Poles and Soviet prisoners of war. They used shooting squads, ghettos and extermination camps such as Auschwitz.

The Nuremberg trials of 1945 and 1946 held leading Nazis to account, and helped to establish the idea of crimes against humanity. The word "genocide" was coined in 1944 by the lawyer Raphael Lemkin. Survivors, and the records the Nazis themselves kept, gave us a detailed account of what happened. It is remembered so that it will never be repeated, and because denial of it, still heard today, is a final insult to the dead.

# D-Day
@src National WWII Museum

On June 6, 1944, Allied forces landed on five beaches in Normandy, France, in the largest seaborne invasion in history. About 156,000 troops came ashore on the first day, supported by thousands of ships and aircraft.

It opened the Western Front against Nazi Germany, and the Allies pushed on to liberate Paris in August. The landing was delayed by one day because of bad weather, and the decision to go ahead was made by General Eisenhower, who had prepared a note taking the blame in case it failed. It was a vast operation that took years of planning, and it was a turning point of the war in Europe.

# Hiroshima and Nagasaki
@src Standard history

In August 1945 the United States dropped atomic bombs on the Japanese cities of Hiroshima, on August 6, and Nagasaki, on August 9. They remain the only use of nuclear weapons in war. Japan announced its surrender on August 15.

Estimates of the number killed in both cities by the end of 1945 range from about 150,000 to 250,000, most of them civilians. Many survivors suffered from radiation sickness for years. Historians still debate whether the bombings were necessary to end the war. Since then, the memory of them has been a powerful influence on the world's efforts to prevent nuclear weapons from being used again. Hiroshima has a peace park, and survivors still share their stories.

# The United Nations
@src UN Charter

The United Nations was founded in October 1945, after the Second World War, by 51 countries, to prevent another such war. It now has 193 member states.

In 1948 its General Assembly adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, drafted by a committee chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt. It set out, for the first time, rights that all people should enjoy. The Declaration is not a treaty and has no force of law, but it has influenced many constitutions and laws around the world. It has been translated into more than 500 languages, which makes it the most translated document in the world.

# Gandhi's Salt March
@src Standard history

In March 1930 Mohandas Gandhi led a 240-mile march to the sea at Dandi to make salt, in defiance of the British law that gave the government a monopoly on it. The walk lasted 24 days, and thousands joined along the way.

The salt tax hurt the poorest most. The march drew world attention to India's independence movement. At the end, Gandhi picked up a lump of natural salt from the beach, and thousands of Indians followed his example, being arrested in huge numbers. India became independent in 1947. The march is a famous example of nonviolent resistance, which later inspired Martin Luther King Jr. and many others.

# Brown, Rosa Parks and the March on Washington
@src Brown v. Board, 347 U.S. 483; National Archives

In 1954 the US Supreme Court ruled unanimously that racial segregation in public schools was unconstitutional, overturning the doctrine of "separate but equal." The case combined several lawsuits, led by the NAACP lawyer Thurgood Marshall, who later became the first Black Supreme Court justice. Real integration took many years, and in many places it's still incomplete.

On December 1, 1955, in Montgomery, Alabama, Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat on a bus to a white passenger, and was arrested. Her act led to a boycott of the city's buses by Black residents that lasted 381 days. It brought a young minister, Martin Luther King Jr., to national leadership. On August 28, 1963, about 250,000 people gathered at the Lincoln Memorial, where King gave his "I Have a Dream" speech, and the Civil Rights Act of 1964 soon followed.

# The Cuban Missile Crisis
@src Allison & Zelikow, 1999

In October 1962 an American spy plane found Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba, 90 miles from Florida. For 13 days, the world held its breath as American ships blockaded the island and leaders threatened war.

It ended when the Soviets agreed to remove the missiles in return for an American pledge not to invade Cuba and a secret promise to remove US missiles from Turkey. It's regarded as the closest the world has come to nuclear war. Afterward, a direct telephone line, the "hot line," was set up between Washington and Moscow, and the two sides signed a treaty banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere in 1963.

# The Berlin Wall
@src Standard history

In August 1961 East Germany began building a wall to stop its citizens from fleeing to the West through Berlin. At least 140 people died trying to cross it.

On November 9, 1989, after huge protests, East Germany announced that its borders were open, and crowds began to tear the wall down. The announcement was made by mistake, in a confusing press conference, and people rushed to the checkpoints in the evening. Germany was reunified in October 1990. The fall of the wall was the symbol of the end of the Cold War, and it came as a surprise to nearly everyone.

# The end of the Soviet Union
@src Standard history

On December 25, 1991, Mikhail Gorbachev resigned as president of the Soviet Union, and the next day the Soviet parliament voted the country out of existence. Fifteen new countries emerged, including Russia, Ukraine and Kazakhstan.

It ended 69 years of Soviet rule and the Cold War that had dominated world politics since the late 1940s. Few experts had predicted that it would happen so quickly or so peacefully. That evening, the red flag with the hammer and sickle was lowered over the Kremlin for the last time. The effects of the collapse are still being felt in the region, and it's a major reason for many of the tensions of today.

# The first message on the internet
@src Kleinrock

On October 29, 1969, a student programmer at UCLA, Charley Kline, tried to send the word "LOGIN" to a computer at Stanford, over the new ARPANET. The system crashed after the first two letters, so the first message was "LO."

An hour later the whole word got through. The network started with four computers, and has grown into billions of devices. It's a fitting beginning, since "lo" is also a word that means "behold." The ARPANET was funded by the US Defense Department, and it was designed to let researchers share computing resources. Its builders could not have imagined how widely it would be used, or what it would become.

# The personal computer
@src Standard history of computing

In 1975 the Altair 8800 kit let hobbyists build their own computers. In 1977 the Apple II, a ready-made machine with color graphics, became a hit, and in 1981 IBM introduced its PC. Together, they moved computers from air-conditioned rooms to ordinary desks and homes.

By the 1990s, the World Wide Web gave those computers a reason to be connected. Before the personal computer, a computer was a huge machine, owned by a company or a university, and the idea that every person might have one seemed odd to many experts. Bill Gates and Paul Allen wrote the first software for the Altair, and started a company that they named Microsoft. The change took only about twenty years.

# The iPhone
@src Apple, 2007

Steve Jobs unveiled the iPhone on January 9, 2007, describing it as three products in one: a phone, a music player and an internet device. It went on sale on June 29. Its multi-touch screen replaced the keypad.

The App Store opened in 2008, and changed how software is made and sold. Within a few years, smartphones had become part of daily life for billions of people. Today, the phone in your pocket is far more powerful than the computers that sent astronauts to the Moon. It's a camera, a map, a bank and a library, and it's changed how we work, shop, meet and remember.

# Smallpox is gone
@src WHO, 1980

Smallpox is the only human disease that has been completely eradicated. It killed about 300 million people in the 20th century alone. A global vaccination campaign led by the World Health Organization reduced cases until the last natural case was reported in Somalia in 1977.

In 1980 the WHO declared the world free of smallpox. It's regarded as one of the greatest achievements in public health. The campaign succeeded by finding every outbreak and vaccinating everyone around it, which is called ring vaccination. Samples of the virus are still held in two laboratories, in the US and Russia, and there is a continuing debate about whether to destroy them.

# Three slow improvements: poverty, lifespan and literacy
@src World Bank; Our World in Data; UNESCO

In 1990 about 36 percent of the world's population lived in extreme poverty, according to World Bank estimates. By 2019 that had fallen to about 9 percent. The World Bank defines it as living on less than about $2 a day in today's prices. Progress has been uneven, much of it in China and India, and the COVID-19 pandemic reversed some of the gains.

In 1900 the average person in the world could expect to live to around 32. By 2019 it was about 73. Much of the improvement came from fewer children dying young, thanks to clean water, vaccines and antibiotics. And in 1820 only about 12 percent of the world's adults could read and write, compared with about 87 percent by 2016. These huge changes happen gradually, so they rarely make the headlines, and it's easy to forget how much has changed for the better.

# COVID-19 and vaccines
@src Nobel Prize in Medicine, 2023

The first vaccines against COVID-19 to use messenger RNA were authorized in December 2020, less than a year after the virus was identified, faster than any vaccine before. That speed was made possible by decades of earlier research.

Scientists such as Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman found how to make mRNA work without triggering harmful inflammation. They received the 2023 Nobel Prize in Medicine. Karikó had struggled for years to get funding for her work, and at one point was demoted by her university. The vaccines saved millions of lives, according to estimates. The technique is now being studied for other diseases, including some cancers, flu and malaria.
