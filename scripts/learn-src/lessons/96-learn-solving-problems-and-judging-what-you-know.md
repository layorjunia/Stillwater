# When a prize makes you slower
@src Glucksberg, Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1962

You are given a candle, a box of tacks and a book of matches, and you must fix the candle to a wall so that the wax does not drip on the floor. This is the candle problem, which the psychologist Karl Duncker devised. The solution is to tack the box to the wall and stand the candle in it. Most people are slow to see this, because they think of the box as a container for the tacks, and not as a shelf. It is called functional fixedness.

In 1962 Sam Glucksberg tried the problem with and without cash prizes for speed. In some versions the tacks were inside the box, which made it harder to see the box as a shelf. In those versions the people who were offered prizes did worse than those who were not. Wanting hard to succeed can make us cling to the obvious use of a thing. When the answer needs a new view, a calmer mind may help more than a bigger motive.

# The fortress and the tumor
@src Gick & Holyoak, Cognitive Psychology, 1980

A patient has a tumor that cannot be removed by surgery. Rays strong enough to destroy it would also destroy the healthy tissue around it, and weak rays would do nothing to the tumor. What can the doctor do? Duncker posed this puzzle too. The answer is to aim several weak rays at the tumor from different directions, so that they add up only at the tumor.

In 1980 Mary Gick and Keith Holyoak had people read a story first. In it, a general wanted to capture a fortress at the center of a country, but the roads were mined so that a large force could not pass. He divided his army and sent small groups along different roads, so that they met at the fortress. When people were told that the story could help, about three quarters of them solved the tumor problem. Without the hint, far fewer did. We often hold the right idea in memory and fail to see that it applies.

# The swinging string
@conf mixed
@src Maier, Journal of Comparative Psychology, 1931

Two cords hang from the ceiling of a room, too far apart for a person to hold one while reaching the other. The task is to tie them together, and there are tools on a table, such as a pair of pliers. In 1931 Norman Maier gave this problem to people. The solution is to tie the pliers to one cord and swing it, then catch it while holding the other cord. Many people were stuck for a long time.

Maier's hint was that the experimenter walked across the room and, in passing, set one of the cords swinging. After that, many solved the problem within a minute. Some said afterward that the idea had come to them all at once, and did not mention the cord. Other researchers argued that the hint was not the only thing that helped, and the details are debated. But it is a good example of how a solution can arrive without our seeing where it came from.

# The quiet eye
@src Vickers, J. Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 1996

In sports like basketball, golf and shooting, what do the best performers look at, and for how long? In 1996 Joan Vickers tracked the eye movements of basketball players as they shot free throws. The experts fixed their gaze on the target, at the front of the rim, for about a second before they began to shoot, and held it there as the shot began. Players who were good but not elite looked for less than half as long.

Vickers called it the quiet eye. Longer fixations went with more accurate shots, in both groups, and when players missed they had often looked for a shorter time. Later studies tried to train it. Golfers who were taught to hold a steady gaze putted better, and held up better when they were made anxious. The training studies are smaller than the first findings, but the idea is simple. Calm, steady attention at the right moment is a skill that can be learned.

# Why a lesson looks easier than it is
@src Koriat & Bjork, J. Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 2005

When you study a pair of items, such as a word and its meaning, the answer is in front of you. It seems obvious, and you feel that you know it. But at the test, the answer will not be there. In 2005 Asher Koriat and Robert Bjork used word pairs to study this trap, which they called foresight bias. They varied how strongly the first word of a pair brought the second to mind.

Students did not take the strength of the link into account when they predicted how well they would remember. Their predictions were much less sensitive to it than their actual recall was. They were fooled by seeing the answer. The remedy that the authors suggested is to test yourself later, with the answer covered. If you cannot produce the answer, the feeling of knowing it was an illusion, and you know what to study.

# Waiting before you judge your reading
@src Thiede, Dunlosky, Griffin & Wiley, J. Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 2005

After reading a chapter, how well do you know it? People are often poor at judging this. In studies that tested their understanding of texts, their predictions of how well they would do on a test were only weakly related to how they really did. Keith Thiede, John Dunlosky and their colleagues asked whether a small task could make the judgments better.

The task was to write down a few keywords that capture the text. When people did it right after reading, it made no difference. When they did it after a delay, their predictions improved a good deal. The delay seems to matter, because it lets the feeling of having just read fade, and makes you rely on what you can recall. The practical advice is to wait a little, and then check what you can say about what you read, before deciding that you are done.

# When searching makes you feel smarter
@src Fisher, Goddu & Keil, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2015

If you look something up on the Internet, do you know it better, or does it only feel that way? In 2015 Matthew Fisher, Mariel Goddu and Frank Keil asked people to search the Internet for explanations to everyday questions. Then they asked them to rate how well they could explain quite different topics, which they had not searched for. A comparison group did not search.

People who had searched rated themselves as more able to explain the other topics than those who had not. The effect did not appear for people who were shown the same text without searching. It appeared even when the searches failed to find an answer. The authors suggested that we do not keep apart what we know and what we can reach. The risk is that we feel expert, and then find that the connection is down.

# What students do when they study alone
@src Karpicke, Butler & Roediger, Memory, 2009

Self-testing is among the best ways to learn, but do students use it? In 2009 Jeffrey Karpicke, Andrew Butler and Henry Roediger asked 177 college students how they study. They asked them to list the strategies that they used, and then to rank them.

The most common answer, given by about 84 percent, was to read notes or the textbook again. Fewer students said that they tested themselves, and those who did often spent little time on it. More than half rated rereading as the most important of their strategies. Rereading gives a feeling of fluency, which feels like learning. The authors argued that students choose it because of that illusion, and so they miss the better method.

# The charm of Dr. Fox
@conf mixed
@src Naftulin, Ware & Donnelly, Journal of Medical Education, 1973; Ware & Williams, 1975

In the 1970s a group of researchers hired an actor, and introduced him as Dr. Myron Fox, an expert on a topic of mathematics and education. They gave him a lecture to deliver, which was written to be full of double talk and made-up terms. He was charming and he spoke with enthusiasm. His audiences were educators and professionals, and most of them rated the lecture highly. Some said that it was stimulating.

The study was small, and it was not designed like a modern experiment. A later study in 1975 by John Ware and Reed Williams tested the idea with 207 students, who saw six lectures that differed in content and in how seductive the presentation was. Students gave higher ratings to the seductive lectures, and the ratings did not track the content when the style was seductive. A lecturer's charm can be mistaken for how much was taught, and ratings alone do not tell us how much was learned.

# Two weeks of mindfulness
@conf mixed
@src Mrazek, Franklin, Phillips, Baird & Schooler, Psychological Science, 2013

Can a short course in mindfulness improve attention and thinking? In 2013 Michael Mrazek and his colleagues at the University of California, Santa Barbara, assigned 48 undergraduates by chance to a two-week class in mindfulness, or to a class on nutrition. Before and after, they tested all of them on a reading section from the Graduate Record Examination, on a working memory task, and on how often their minds wandered.

The students who took the mindfulness class improved on the reading test and on working memory, and they mind-wandered less while they were tested. The students in the nutrition class did not change. The authors found that the better scores came with reduced mind wandering, among students who were prone to wander. The study was small and used students, and a single experiment is not proof. But it fits the idea that attention is a skill, which can be practiced.
