# The Indus cities
@src Kenoyer, Ancient Cities of the Indus Valley Civilization, 1998

About forty-five hundred years ago, one of the world's first urban civilizations arose along the Indus River, in what is now Pakistan and northwest India. Its great cities, Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, were carefully planned, with streets in a grid, and standard-sized bricks. Many houses had their own bathrooms and drains that emptied into covered sewers along the streets.

The people traded with Mesopotamia, and used a script that has never been deciphered. There are no known palaces, and no obvious temples or royal tombs, which makes the society hard to understand. The civilization declined from about 1900 BC, probably because of changes in climate and in the rivers. It was forgotten until the 1920s, when archaeologists dug up the ruins.

# Cleopatra and the pyramids
@src Standard ancient chronology

Cleopatra VII, the last active ruler of ancient Egypt, died in 30 BC. The Great Pyramid of Giza was built around 2560 BC, so it was already about two and a half thousand years old when she was born. She lived closer in time to the first Moon landing in 1969 than to the building of the pyramid. Counting from her death to 1969, it's about two thousand years.

It's a good way of feeling how long ancient Egypt lasted. Its history ran for about three thousand years before the Romans took it over. People in Cleopatra's time looked at the pyramids as ancient monuments, just as we look at Roman ruins. She was herself a Greek by descent, and a member of the Ptolemaic dynasty, founded by one of Alexander the Great's generals. She was reportedly the first of her family to learn the Egyptian language.

# The collapse of the Bronze Age
@src Cline, 1177 B.C.: The Year Civilization Collapsed, 2014

Around 1200 BC, a number of rich civilizations in the eastern Mediterranean fell within a few decades. The palaces of the Mycenaean Greeks were burned and abandoned. The Hittite empire in Turkey broke up. Cities along the coast of Syria were destroyed, and Egypt was weakened. Writing was lost in parts of Greece, and a dark age followed.

For a long time, the explanation was invasion by mysterious Sea Peoples, whom the Egyptians recorded fighting. Modern historians, such as Eric Cline, think that no single cause was enough. Droughts, earthquakes, famine, rebellions and the breakdown of trade may have combined, each making the others worse. The societies were closely linked, and so a failure in one place spread to others. The story is often used as a warning about interconnected systems.

# The Cyrus Cylinder
@src British Museum; Kuhrt, The Persian Empire, 2007
@conf mixed

In 539 BC Cyrus the Great of Persia captured Babylon. Soon afterward, a clay cylinder was made, inscribed in Babylonian, which describes how the Babylonian god chose Cyrus, and how he entered the city peacefully. It also says that he allowed the people who had been moved to Babylon to return to their homelands, and restored their temples. It was found in 1879 and is in the British Museum.

The Cylinder has been called the first charter of human rights, and a copy is displayed at the United Nations. Many historians think that is too much. It is a royal proclamation, in the tradition of Mesopotamian kings, and it praises its ruler. But it does reflect a policy that was unusual. The Persians governed a vast, diverse empire in part by tolerating local customs and religions. The Bible also credits Cyrus with allowing the Jews to return from exile.

# Thermopylae
@src Herodotus, Histories, Book 7

In 480 BC the Persian king Xerxes invaded Greece with an enormous army. At a narrow pass called Thermopylae, a Greek force held it for three days. The famous part is the three hundred Spartans led by King Leonidas, but they were the royal guard, and they were joined by several thousand other Greeks, according to Herodotus.

On the third day, a local man showed the Persians a path around the pass, and the Greeks were outflanked. Leonidas sent most of the army away, and stayed with the Spartans and some others to hold the pass. They fought to the death. The delay allowed Athens to be evacuated, and gave the Greek fleet time to prepare. The Greeks won at the sea battle of Salamis a few weeks later. The story has been told for twenty-five centuries as an example of courage in a lost cause.

# The plague of Athens
@src Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War, Book 2

In 430 BC Athens was at war with Sparta, and had brought the people of the countryside inside its walls for protection. The city became crowded and hot. A plague struck, and killed a large part of the population, including the leader Pericles, who died in 429 BC. The historian Thucydides, who caught the disease and survived, described it.

His account is careful. He recorded the symptoms, such as fever, red eyes, a rash and terrible thirst, and noted that people who recovered did not catch it again. He also described how law and morality broke down, as people spent money quickly, since they expected to die. The cause of the disease is still unknown, and typhoid fever has been suggested. The plague weakened Athens, and contributed to its defeat in the war. Thucydides wrote so that readers could recognize it if it returned.

# Hannibal and the elephants
@src Polybius, Histories; Livy, Ab Urbe Condita

In 218 BC the Carthaginian general Hannibal set out to attack Rome during the Second Punic War. Rather than going by sea, which the Romans controlled, he marched a large army from Spain, through southern Gaul, and across the Alps, with war elephants, in the fall. It was a very hard march, and he lost many men and animals to the cold, to landslides and to attacks by mountain tribes.

He arrived in Italy and won several battles. The greatest was at Cannae in 216 BC, where he surrounded a larger Roman army and destroyed it, perhaps killing around fifty thousand men in a day. But he could not take Rome itself, and he was in a hostile country with no reinforcements. Eventually the Romans took the war to Africa. He was defeated at Zama in 202 BC. His tactics are still studied in military schools.

# Roman concrete
@src Seymour et al., Science Advances, 2023
@conf mixed

The Romans built with concrete, and much of it still stands. The Pantheon in Rome, completed in about 125 AD, has the largest unreinforced concrete dome in the world. Harbors made of concrete are still there, after two thousand years in the sea. Modern concrete often cracks after a few decades.

For years, scientists have wondered why the Roman version lasted so long. One answer was volcanic ash, which reacts with lime to form strong compounds, including in seawater. In 2023 Admir Masic and his colleagues found small white chunks of lime in samples of Roman concrete, and proposed that the Romans mixed the lime with the other ingredients while it was still hot. The chunks could then dissolve in water that seeped into cracks, and fill them again. It's an interesting idea, though the full story is still being studied.

# Maya mathematics
@src Coe, The Maya, 2011

The Maya civilization flourished in Mexico and Central America for over a thousand years, from about 250 to 900 AD in its classic period. The Maya built stone cities with pyramids, and developed a system of writing with glyphs. They also had a number system with a base of twenty, and a symbol for zero, which they used from at least the fourth century AD, independently of the Old World.

Their numbers were written with dots, standing for one, and bars, standing for five. They tracked time with several interlocking calendars. One had 260 days, and another 365 days, and they combined to repeat every 52 years. They also kept a long count of days from a starting date in the distant past. In calculating the cycles of Venus and eclipses, the Maya astronomers were remarkably accurate.

# Finding islands without instruments
@src Lewis, We, the Navigators, 1972; Finney, Hokule'a, 1979

The Polynesians settled islands across the Pacific, an area larger than any other culture had explored. They crossed thousands of miles of open ocean in double-hulled canoes, and found tiny islands, from Hawaii to New Zealand to Easter Island. They had no compass, no charts and no metal tools.

Navigators memorized the positions of the stars as they rose and set, and used the swell of the waves, the flight of birds and the color of clouds to find land. In 1976 a replica canoe, the Hōkūle‘a, sailed from Hawaii to Tahiti, about four thousand kilometers, guided by a Micronesian navigator, Mau Piailug, using traditional methods. The voyage showed that the settlement of the Pacific could have been done deliberately, and it revived pride in the old skills.

# Marco Polo
@src Polo, The Travels; Wood, Did Marco Polo Go to China?, 1995
@conf mixed

Marco Polo was a Venetian merchant who traveled to Asia with his father and uncle, and spent about twenty years there, from 1271 to 1295. He claimed to have served the Mongol emperor Kublai Khan. After he returned, he was captured in a war between Venice and Genoa, and in prison dictated an account of his travels to a writer named Rustichello.

The book was popular, and it is said to have inspired Columbus. But from the beginning, some people doubted that Polo had been to the places described. Some scholars note that he does not mention the Great Wall, tea or Chinese writing. Others answer that much of what he wrote is confirmed by Chinese sources, and that he may have used the accounts of others for parts. The debate is unsettled. Most historians believe that he did travel widely, but that his book mixes first-hand and second-hand stories.

# Zheng He's treasure fleets
@src Levathes, When China Ruled the Seas, 1994

In the early fifteenth century, the Ming emperors of China sent out huge fleets under an admiral named Zheng He, a Muslim eunuch from Yunnan. Between 1405 and 1433, there were seven voyages. They sailed through Southeast Asia, to India, Arabia and the east coast of Africa. The fleets included hundreds of ships and tens of thousands of men.

The largest ships, the treasure ships, were much bigger than any European ship of the time, though their exact size is debated. The voyages were meant to display the power of the Ming and to collect tribute. After the death of the emperor who had sponsored them, they ended. Officials who favored a focus on the land frontiers and on the cost won the argument, and many records were destroyed. Seventy years later, Portuguese ships arrived in the same waters.

# The Taj Mahal
@src Koch, The Complete Taj Mahal, 2006

The Mughal emperor Shah Jahan had the Taj Mahal built in Agra, India, as a tomb for his wife, Mumtaz Mahal. She died in 1631 while giving birth to their fourteenth child. The work began in about 1632, and the main building was completed around 1643, with the grounds and other buildings finished in about 1653.

The tomb is made of white marble, brought from Rajasthan, and inlaid with semi-precious stones. It's built on a platform with four minarets, with a garden in front. Thousands of craftsmen and laborers worked on it, and the cost was enormous. The legend that the emperor cut off the hands of the workers is unsupported. Shah Jahan was later deposed by his own son, and held in a fort within view of the Taj, where he died in 1666. He was buried beside his wife.

# The little ice age
@src Fagan, The Little Ice Age, 2000; Owens et al., 2017
@conf mixed

From the fourteenth to the nineteenth centuries, Europe and some other parts of the world went through a cooler period known as the Little Ice Age. Winters were harder. Rivers such as the Thames froze, and frost fairs were held on the ice. Glaciers in the Alps advanced and swallowed farms. Harvests failed, and there were famines.

The causes are debated, and there was no single, uniform cooling. Possible causes include a reduced output from the Sun, in a period called the Maunder Minimum from about 1645 to 1715, and a series of large volcanic eruptions that put dust into the air. A decline in the population in the Americas after European contact may also have played a part, as forests regrew and took up carbon. The temperature change was small, perhaps a degree Celsius, but it had large effects.

# The first company with shares
@src Gelderblom & Jonker, Journal of Economic History, 2004

In 1602 the Dutch government granted a charter to the United East India Company, called the VOC, a trading company with a monopoly on Dutch trade with Asia. Companies had existed before, but the VOC was unusual. It raised money by selling shares to the public, and the shares could be traded. Investors could put in money for the long term, without having to wait for each voyage to end.

A market for the shares grew up in Amsterdam, which is regarded as the first stock exchange. The company was the biggest in the world for a century, with a fleet of ships and its own armies. It also took part in a brutal colonial rule in Indonesia, and the slave trade. It was dissolved in 1799, after years of debt and corruption. The structure that it pioneered, a company owned by shareholders, became the model of modern business.

# The Salem witch trials
@src Norton, In the Devil's Snare, 2002; Spanos & Gottlieb, Science, 1976
@conf mixed

In 1692 in Salem Village, in colonial Massachusetts, several girls began to suffer from fits, and said they were being tormented by witches. Over the next months, more than two hundred people were accused. Nineteen were hanged, and one man, Giles Corey, was pressed to death with stones for refusing to enter a plea. Several others died in jail.

Many explanations have been offered. In 1976 a psychologist named Linnda Caporael proposed that the fits were caused by ergot, a fungus that grows on rye and can cause hallucinations and convulsions. Other researchers, such as Nicholas Spanos and Jack Gottlieb, replied that the symptoms don't fit ergot poisoning well. Historians tend to emphasize local quarrels, fear of attack by Native American tribes in the area, and religious belief. The trials ended in 1693, and the colony later apologized.

# The Great Famine in Ireland
@src Kinealy, This Great Calamity, 1994

In 1845 a disease called potato blight struck the potato crop in Ireland. The potato was the main food of about a third of the population, especially the rural poor. The blight returned in the next years, and the harvests failed. Over the following years, about one million people died of hunger and disease, in a population of about eight million.

Another million or more emigrated, many to North America, in what were called coffin ships because of the deaths on board. During the famine, Ireland continued to export food, and the British government's response was widely seen as slow and inadequate, and shaped by the economic ideas of the day. The population of Ireland continued to decline for decades. It has still not returned to its level before the famine.

# Counting the dead of the Civil War
@src Hacker, Civil War History, 2011

For many years, the number of soldiers who died in the American Civil War was given as about 620,000, a figure that was worked out in the 1880s. It's an enormous number, bigger than the combined deaths of the United States in all its other wars up to Vietnam. In 2011 the historian J. David Hacker looked again at the census records, and used modern methods.

He estimated that the true toll was about 750,000, and that it might have been higher. The older count had missed deaths, particularly among Confederate soldiers and from disease, which killed more men than combat. A large share of them died from dysentery, typhoid and other illnesses in camp. As a share of the population, the war would be the equivalent of seven million deaths today.

# Japan's Meiji Restoration
@src Jansen, The Making of Modern Japan, 2000

In 1853 an American naval squadron under Commodore Matthew Perry arrived in Tokyo Bay, and demanded that Japan open itself to trade. For over two hundred years, the country had been ruled by the Tokugawa shogunate and had limited contact with the outside world. The arrival of the steam-powered warships showed the rulers how far behind Japan was in technology.

In 1868 a group of samurai and nobles overthrew the shogunate, and restored power to the emperor Meiji. They then set out on a rapid program of modernization. They created a conscript army, a national school system, banks and railways. They sent missions to Europe and America to study. Within about forty years, Japan had become an industrial power and defeated Russia in a war in 1905. It's one of history's most dramatic examples of a nation changing course.

# Chernobyl
@src IAEA, Chernobyl's Legacy, 2006; UNSCEAR, 2008

On April 26, 1986, during a safety test at the Chernobyl nuclear power plant in Ukraine, then part of the Soviet Union, a reactor exploded. A design flaw and mistakes by the operators led to a power surge. The explosion and fire released large amounts of radioactive material, which spread over much of Europe.

Two workers died in the explosion, and twenty-eight more firefighters and plant workers died within months from acute radiation sickness. Over a hundred thousand people were evacuated from the area, including the town of Pripyat, and an exclusion zone remains. Estimates of the eventual number of deaths from cancer vary widely, from a few thousand to many more, depending on the methods. The Soviet authorities were slow to disclose the accident. It has been called one of the factors in the loss of public trust in the government.

# The Berlin Airlift
@src Tusa, The Berlin Blockade, 1988

After the Second World War, Germany was divided into four zones, controlled by the United States, Britain, France and the Soviet Union. Berlin, which lay deep in the Soviet zone, was also divided. In June 1948 the Soviets blocked all road, rail and canal links to the western sectors of the city, hoping to force the Western powers out.

About two million people lived in West Berlin, and they needed food and coal. The United States and Britain began to supply them by air. At the peak, planes landed in Berlin every minute or so, around the clock, bringing in thousands of tons a day. In all, over fifteen months, about two and a third million tons of supplies were delivered. In May 1949 the Soviets ended the blockade. The airlift was a victory without a shot being fired.

# The Marshall Plan
@src Hogan, The Marshall Plan, 1987

After the Second World War, much of Europe was in ruins. Cities had been bombed, factories destroyed, and many people were hungry. In June 1947, the American Secretary of State, George Marshall, proposed that the United States should help Europe to rebuild. The plan was passed by Congress in 1948.

Over four years, the United States gave about thirteen billion dollars, a very large sum at the time, to sixteen European countries. The money bought food, fuel, machinery and raw materials. Production rose, and trade revived. The plan was motivated partly by generosity and partly by a fear that poverty would push countries toward communism. Historians debate how much it contributed to recovery. But it's remembered as an example of a victor helping its former enemies and allies alike.

# Mandela and the end of apartheid
@src Mandela, Long Walk to Freedom, 1994

In 1948 the white minority government of South Africa began a system of racial separation called apartheid. It classified people by race, forced millions to live in separate areas, and denied the black majority the vote. Nelson Mandela, a lawyer, joined the African National Congress, which resisted the system. In 1964 he was sentenced to life imprisonment, and spent twenty-seven years in jail, most of them on Robben Island.

International pressure and unrest at home eventually forced the government to negotiate. Mandela was released in 1990. In 1994, in the first election in which all South Africans could vote, he was elected president. He chose not to seek revenge, and set up a Truth and Reconciliation Commission, in which people could confess to crimes in exchange for amnesty. He served one term and then stepped down.

# Microcredit and Grameen
@src Yunus, Banker to the Poor, 1999

In 1976 Muhammad Yunus, an economics professor in Bangladesh, visited a village and met women who made bamboo furniture. They had to borrow from moneylenders at very high interest, and were left with almost nothing. Yunus lent twenty-seven dollars of his own money to forty-two villagers. They repaid him.

He went on to found Grameen Bank, which makes small loans, mostly to poor women, without collateral. Borrowers are organized into groups, and the group members encourage one another to repay. Repayment rates were reported to be high. Yunus and the bank shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006. Later studies have found that the benefits of microcredit for lifting people out of poverty are more modest than first thought. But the idea that the poor are good credit risks was influential.
