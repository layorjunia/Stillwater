# The default that doubled saving
@src Madrian & Shea, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2001

Many people mean to save for retirement and never get around to signing up. In 2001 Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea studied a large American company that changed how it enrolled new employees in its retirement plan. Before, employees had to choose to join. Afterward, they were joined automatically, at a set contribution rate, and could opt out if they wished.

Participation among new employees roughly doubled. But many of them stayed with the default amounts, which were low, at 3 percent of pay in a very safe fund, and which they might never have chosen on their own. The default seemed to serve as a signal of what the company thought was right, and as a path of least effort. The finding helped to inspire rules that made automatic enrollment easier for employers, and a debate on how high the default should be.

# What posting the calories did
@src Bollinger, Leslie & Sorensen, American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 2011

In 2008 New York City began to enforce a rule that chain restaurants must post the calories of their items. Bryan Bollinger, Phillip Leslie and Alan Sorensen obtained the records of every purchase at the Starbucks stores in New York, Boston and Philadelphia, from January 2008 to February 2009. Boston and Philadelphia had no such rule, so they could serve as a comparison.

In New York the average calories per purchase fell by about 6 percent, from 247 to 232. Almost all of the drop came from food, as people bought fewer items or lower-calorie ones, and not from drinks. The effect was larger among the customers who usually bought the most, who cut about a quarter. The authors cautioned that a drop of 6 percent is too small to make a big difference to the nation's health on its own.

# Buying time
@series mind
@src Whillans, Dunn, Smeets, Bekkers & Norton, PNAS, 2017

When people have a little spare money, they usually buy things. In 2017 Ashley Whillans, Elizabeth Dunn and their colleagues asked whether it is better to buy time, by paying someone to do the chores that we dislike. They surveyed more than six thousand adults in the United States, Canada, Denmark and the Netherlands about how they spent their money, and how satisfied they were with life.

People who spent money to save time, for example on cleaning or delivery, reported greater satisfaction, and this was so at different income levels. In an experiment with sixty working adults in Vancouver, each person was given forty dollars on two weekends, to spend on a time-saving purchase on one weekend and on a material purchase on the other. They felt happier after the time purchase. The effects were modest, but they suggest that for many people a chore is worth paying to avoid.

# The white wine that was dyed red
@conf mixed
@src Morrot, Brochet & Dubourdieu, Brain and Language, 2001

How much does the color of a drink change the way it smells? In 2001 Gil Morrot, Frédérique Brochet and Denis Dubourdieu, from Bordeaux, a center of wine research, asked wine tasters to describe the aromas of a white wine. Later they asked them to describe the same wine, after it had been colored red with an odorless dye.

The tasters described the dyed wine with the words that they usually use for red wines, such as cherry and prune, though it was the same white wine. They were fooled by the color. A later study, by Ballester and colleagues in 2009, found a similar result, but also noted problems with the method of the first. The study suggests that smell is not a clean channel, and that what we see changes what we perceive.

# The crunch you can hear
@src Zampini & Spence, Journal of Sensory Studies, 2004

Does the sound of food change how it tastes? In 2004 Massimiliano Zampini and Charles Spence asked people to bite into potato chips with their front teeth, and to rate how crisp and how fresh each one was. The people wore headphones. The sound of their own biting was picked up by a microphone, altered, and played back to them as they bit.

When the sound was louder, or when the high frequencies were boosted, the chips seemed crisper and fresher, even though they were the same chips. Only the sound had been changed. The study won an Ig Nobel Prize in 2008. It suggests that the makers of snacks have a reason to care about how their products sound, as well as how they look and taste.

# Beer with a label
@src Allison & Uhl, Journal of Marketing Research, 1964

Do people who prefer a brand of beer know it by its taste? In 1964 Ralph Allison and Kenneth Uhl asked beer drinkers to taste several beers, first with the labels hidden, and then with the brand names shown. The beers were the major brands. They asked the drinkers to rate the beers on taste, with measures such as strength and bitterness, and to give an overall rating.

With the labels hidden, the drinkers could not tell the brands apart, and could not pick out their usual brand. When they could see the labels, their ratings changed to fit what they believed about each brand. The taste that people reported was partly the reputation of the beer. It was an early demonstration that the brand, and not only the product, shapes the experience.

# Why sellers hold out
@src Genesove & Mayer, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2001

Imagine that you bought an apartment for a high price, and now the market has fallen. Should that change what you ask for it? Economists say that the price you paid is gone, and only the current market should matter. In 2001 David Genesove and Christopher Mayer looked at sales of condominiums in downtown Boston in the 1990s, and compared the owners who would sell at a loss with those who would not.

Owners who faced a loss on paper set higher asking prices. The markup was about a quarter to a third of the gap between what the unit was probably worth and what they had paid. They also tended to hold on longer, and got somewhat higher prices, but not as high as they asked. It is loss aversion at work, since people dislike locking in a loss. It helps to explain why housing sales slow down when prices fall.

# Five personalities of brands
@conf mixed
@src Aaker, Journal of Marketing Research, 1997

We talk about brands as if they were people. One is honest, another is daring, another is stuffy. In 1997 Jennifer Aaker asked whether people agree about this. She had people rate well-known brands on a long list of human traits, and then analyzed which traits go together, as is done in studies of human personality.

She found five groups of traits. They were sincerity, as in down-to-earth and honest, excitement, as in daring and spirited, competence, as in reliable and successful, sophistication, as in upper class and charming, and ruggedness, as in outdoorsy and tough. Later studies found that these five do not hold up in every country. But the idea that a brand has a personality that people can describe is now used widely in marketing.

# Do drivers of fancy cars give way?
@series mind
@conf mixed
@src Piff, Stancato, Côté, Mendoza-Denton & Keltner, PNAS, 2012

In 2012 Paul Piff, Dacher Keltner and their colleagues asked whether people with more money behave less ethically. Observers watched drivers at a busy intersection in the San Francisco Bay Area, and rated how expensive each car was. Then they watched the drivers at a crosswalk, where a person was waiting to cross.

Drivers of more expensive cars were more likely to cut off other cars, and less likely to stop for the person waiting to cross. In a laboratory study, students who said that they came from a higher social class were more likely to take candy that was meant for children. The authors suggested that having more resources makes people more focused on themselves. Other researchers have not always found the same, and the question is still debated.

# The scent of a store
@conf mixed
@src Spangenberg, Crowley & Henderson, Journal of Marketing, 1996

Do shops sell more if they smell good? In 1996 Eric Spangenberg, Ayn Crowley and Pamela Henderson tested the idea in a simulated store, in which they spread a pleasant scent in the air in some conditions, and used no scent in others. Mostly students were asked to evaluate the store and its products, and to say what they meant to do.

In the scented store, people rated the store and its products more highly, and said that they were more likely to visit and to buy. They also thought that they had spent less time there than they really had. The study was done in a laboratory with students, and later work suggests that the effect depends on the scent and the setting. Smell is a quiet part of how a place feels.

# The tax that the tags revealed
@src Chetty, Looney & Kroft, American Economic Review, 2009

In most American stores, the price on the shelf does not include the sales tax, which is added at the register. Do shoppers take it into account? In 2009 Raj Chetty, Adam Looney and Kory Kroft tested it in a grocery store. For three weeks they posted tags that showed the price with the tax included, for about 750 items in three categories, which were cosmetics, hair care accessories and deodorants.

Using scanner data, they compared the sales of those items with those of similar items, and with other stores. Demand for the tagged items fell by about 8 percent, which is about what a price rise of the size of the tax would do. So most shoppers had not been counting the tax. In a survey, nearly all of them ignored it when estimating the total of a basket, but when the tags were shown, most computed it correctly.

# How E-ZPass raised the tolls
@src Finkelstein, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2009

When you pay a toll with cash, you feel it. When a device in your car pays it for you, you hardly notice. In 2009 the economist Amy Finkelstein studied the roads, bridges and tunnels in the United States that adopted electronic toll collection, such as E-ZPass. She compared their toll rates before and after.

After a toll road adopted electronic collection, its toll rates rose. She estimated that in the long run they were 20 to 40 percent higher than they would have been. Drivers became less sensitive to the price, and the officials who set the rates were less affected by the election calendar. The payment had become less visible, so it was easier to raise. It shows how a method of payment can change the price.
