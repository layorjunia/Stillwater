# You use all of your brain
@src Beyerstein, 1999

You've probably heard that people use only ten percent of their brains. It's one of the most durable myths in popular psychology, and it's false. Brain scans show activity throughout the brain over the course of a day, even during sleep. If 90 percent of the brain were idle, damage to most areas would have no effect, and yet almost any injury to the brain causes some loss.

The brain is also expensive. It uses about a fifth of the body's energy, and evolution doesn't usually keep an organ that costly if most of it is unused. The neurologist Barry Beyerstein reviewed the evidence for the myth in 1999 and found no support for it. You don't use all of it at the same time, which would be a seizure. But over a day, you use it all.

# The myth of the left-brained person
@src Nielsen et al., PLoS ONE, 2013

Many people say that they're left-brained, meaning logical, or right-brained, meaning creative. The idea comes from real findings about the two halves of the brain, which do specialize in some ways. Language, for example, is usually handled mostly by the left side. But the idea that individuals are dominated by one hemisphere isn't supported.

In 2013 Jared Nielsen and his colleagues analyzed brain scans of more than 1,000 people, looking at activity across the brain in each. They found no sign that individuals had a stronger network on one side or the other. People used both hemispheres, which are constantly talking to each other through the corpus callosum. You're free to enjoy math and poetry both, and there's nothing in your brain that prevents it.

# The window that closes
@src Hubel & Wiesel, 1960s

In the 1960s David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel studied how the visual system develops, by sewing one eye of young kittens shut for a few weeks. When the eye was opened, the kittens were nearly blind in it, though the eye itself was healthy. The brain had given the space for that eye to the other one. If the same thing was done to an adult cat, there was little effect.

They had found a critical period, a stretch of early life in which the brain needs experience to wire itself properly. The work led to treatment for children with a lazy eye, which is most successful if it's started early. It earned Hubel and Wiesel a Nobel Prize in 1981. The brain stays flexible throughout life, but some things are learned best when we're young, like the sounds of a language.

# When the blind brain reads
@src Sadato et al., Nature, 1996

In 1996 Norihiro Sadato and his colleagues scanned the brains of people who had been blind from an early age while they read Braille with their fingers. The scan showed activity in the visual cortex, the part of the brain that normally processes what we see. The same region wasn't active in sighted people when they felt the Braille dots.

The finding was one of the first clear demonstrations of cross-modal plasticity, where an area of the brain takes on a new job when its usual input is missing. Later studies showed that the visual areas of blind people also help with language and memory. The brain isn't rigidly assigned. When one door closes, the neurons look for work somewhere else.

# Cells that fire together
@src Hebb, 1949; Bliss & Lømo, 1973

In 1949 the psychologist Donald Hebb proposed a simple rule for learning. When one neuron repeatedly helps to make another fire, the connection between them gets stronger. It's often summarized as "cells that fire together wire together." It was a guess about how experience could change the brain.

In 1973 Tim Bliss and Terje Lømo found evidence for it in rabbits. A burst of rapid stimulation to a pathway in the hippocampus made the connections more responsive for hours, or even weeks. They called it long-term potentiation, and it's now considered one of the main mechanisms of memory. It means that learning has a physical basis, with practice, repetition and attention making particular connections stronger.

# The memory you can switch on
@src Liu et al., Nature, 2012

Where is a memory stored? In 2012 Steve Ramirez, Xu Liu, Susumu Tonegawa and their colleagues used a technique called optogenetics to find out. In mice, they identified the specific cells in the hippocampus that were active while the animals learned that a particular box was associated with a mild shock. They made those cells sensitive to light.

Later, in a completely different box, they switched on those cells with a flash of light. The mice froze in fear, as if they were in the original box. They had activated a memory without any outside cue. The result supports the idea of the engram, a physical trace of a memory held in a particular group of cells. It's a long way from human memory, but it shows that memories are physical things.

# Prediction errors and dopamine
@src Schultz, Dayan & Montague, Science, 1997

In the 1990s Wolfram Schultz recorded the activity of dopamine neurons in monkeys. At first the cells fired when the monkey got a squirt of juice. After the monkey learned that a light always came before the juice, the cells fired when the light came on, and not when the juice arrived. And if the juice was expected and didn't come, the cells paused.

In 1997 Schultz, Peter Dayan and Read Montague showed that this pattern fits a learning rule, in which dopamine signals the difference between what you expected and what happened, a reward prediction error. Dopamine isn't simply a pleasure chemical. It tells the brain when things are better or worse than predicted, so that it can update. That helps to explain why surprise, novelty and unpredictable rewards, such as slot machines, can be so gripping.

# Wanting is not liking
@src Berridge & Robinson, 1998

Dopamine is often called the pleasure chemical, but Kent Berridge's experiments with rats suggest otherwise. When rats were deprived of dopamine, they stopped seeking food and would starve to death. But when sugar was put in their mouths, they showed the same pleased facial reactions as healthy rats. They still liked it, but didn't want it enough to look for it.

Berridge and Terry Robinson proposed in 1998 that wanting and liking are different systems. Wanting, which is driven by dopamine, is the pull toward something. Liking is the pleasure when you get it. In addiction, wanting can grow while liking fades, so that a person craves a drug that no longer gives much enjoyment. It's a useful distinction in everyday life, when you find yourself reaching for something that you don't really enjoy.

# The magic number is four
@src Cowan, Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 2001

The most famous limit in psychology is Miller's magical number seven, plus or minus two, which was proposed in 1956 as the capacity of short-term memory. In 2001 Nelson Cowan reviewed the evidence and argued that the true limit is lower, about four chunks, if you account for people's use of tricks like rehearsal and grouping.

Seven items can be held if you chunk and rehearse, as with the digits of a phone number. But the pure capacity of attention, when those strategies are blocked, seems to be around four. It's a reminder of how little we can hold in mind at once. That's why a long list of instructions is easy to forget, and why writing things down, or breaking a task into steps, is such a good idea.

# Where is the face area?
@src Kanwisher, McDermott & Chun, 1997

In 1997 Nancy Kanwisher and her colleagues scanned people's brains while they looked at pictures of faces, houses, objects and scrambled images. A small region on the underside of the temporal lobe, in the fusiform gyrus, responded much more to faces than to anything else. She called it the fusiform face area.

The discovery fits with clinical cases of prosopagnosia, or face blindness, in which people lose the ability to recognize faces after damage to this region, while they can see other objects perfectly well. They may fail to recognize their own family, and have to rely on voice, hair or clothes. The finding showed that the brain has specialized regions for things that matter a lot to us, and faces are among the most important.

# Two percent of us see sounds
@src Simner et al., Perception, 2006

For some people, the senses are linked. They see colors when they hear music, or they feel that each letter has a particular shade, or that Tuesday is green. The condition is called synesthesia. In 2006 Julia Simner and her colleagues tested more than a thousand people in Scotland and estimated that about 4 percent of them had some form of it.

The most common form is a link between letters or numbers and colors. The associations are consistent, so a person who sees the letter A as red will see it as red years later. It's not imagination or metaphor, as tests of speed and accuracy show. Synesthesia seems to run in families, and it suggests that the way we experience the world isn't the same for everyone.

# Seeing without knowing you see
@src Weiskrantz, 1974; de Gelder et al., 2008

Damage to the visual cortex can leave a person blind in part of their visual field. But in 1974 Lawrence Weiskrantz and his colleagues found that some of these patients could still point to a light, or guess the position of objects, in their blind area, better than chance. They insisted that they saw nothing and were just guessing. The condition was called blindsight.

In 2008 Beatrice de Gelder and her colleagues studied a man who had lost sight in both halves of his visual cortex, and who could not see at all. When he was asked to walk down a corridor cluttered with obstacles, he avoided them all, weaving around a trash can and a box, with no awareness of doing it. It shows that some visual processing goes on outside awareness, along older pathways in the brain.

# The patient who forgot half of Milan
@src Bisiach & Luzzatti, Cortex, 1978

In 1978 Edoardo Bisiach and Claudio Luzzatti described patients who had suffered strokes that damaged the right side of the brain, and who then ignored the left side of the world. They would eat food from the right half of a plate, shave half of the face, and draw only the right half of a clock. The condition is called neglect.

The two researchers asked such patients to imagine standing in a famous square in Milan, the Piazza del Duomo, and describe what they saw. They described the buildings on their right side only. Then they were asked to imagine standing at the other end of the square, facing the other way, and again they described what was on their right, naming the buildings that they'd missed before. The neglect was in their mental image, as well as in what their eyes saw.

# Did your brain decide before you did?
@src Libet et al., Brain, 1983
@conf contested

In 1983 Benjamin Libet asked people to flick their wrist whenever they felt like it, and to note the time on a fast clock when they first felt the urge. EEG recordings showed a slow build-up of electrical activity in the brain, the readiness potential, that began about half a second before the movement, well before the moment at which people said that they decided, which was about a fifth of a second before.

Many took it as evidence that free will is an illusion, because the brain seems to start the action before we're aware of choosing. But the finding is disputed. In 2012 Aaron Schurger and his colleagues proposed that the readiness potential may just reflect random fluctuations in brain activity that tip over a threshold, and not a decision. The experiment involves trivial choices, and it's far from clear that it tells us about the choices that matter.

# Signals of a dreaming mind
@src LaBerge, 1981

Can you know that you're dreaming while you dream? In the late 1970s Keith Hearne, in England, and Stephen LaBerge, in California, found a way to test it. During REM sleep, the body is paralyzed, but the eyes can still move. LaBerge asked people who'd had lucid dreams to move their eyes in a set pattern, such as left, right, left, right, when they realized in a dream that they were dreaming.

Recordings of the sleepers' eye muscles showed the patterns. The dreamers had been aware, and had sent a signal from inside a dream. Lucid dreaming is something that some people learn to do by practicing reality checks, such as asking themselves during the day whether they're dreaming. It's a striking demonstration that consciousness can continue in sleep.

# What the first brain waves showed
@src Berger, 1929

In 1924 the German psychiatrist Hans Berger recorded electrical activity from a human brain for the first time, using wires pressed against the scalp of a patient. He didn't publish until 1929, because he was cautious. He saw that the signal had a slow, regular rhythm, about ten waves a second, when a person was relaxed with closed eyes. It became known as the alpha wave.

When the person opened their eyes or did mental arithmetic, the rhythm disappeared and was replaced by a faster, smaller one. The electroencephalogram, or EEG, became the basis of sleep research, epilepsy diagnosis and much of cognitive neuroscience. It's still used, since it's cheap, safe and fast. A grid of wires on the head shows, to the millisecond, how the brain's activity changes.

# Is a bigger brain a smarter brain?
@src Pietschnig et al., Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews, 2015

Across many studies, people with larger brains do tend to score somewhat higher on intelligence tests. In 2015 Jakob Pietschnig and his colleagues combined data from over 8,000 people in 88 studies and estimated the correlation at about 0.24, which is modest. Brain size explains only a small fraction of the difference in test scores between people.

Size across species is an even poorer guide. In 2014 Suzana Herculano-Houzel counted the neurons in the brain of an African elephant, which is three times bigger than ours, and found about 257 billion, three times as many as a human's 86 billion. But most of them were in the cerebellum. In the cortex, humans have more. How a brain is wired, and how many neurons are in the thinking regions, seems to matter more than total size.

# Teaching the brain to juggle
@src Draganski et al., Nature, 2004

In 2004 Bogdan Draganski and his colleagues taught a group of young adults to juggle three balls, and scanned their brains before and after. Those who learned the skill, over about three months, had increased gray matter in areas that process visual motion. A group who didn't juggle showed no change. When the jugglers stopped practicing, the gray matter shrank back partly.

It was one of the first demonstrations that learning a new physical skill can change the structure of the adult brain, in only weeks. Later studies found similar effects for learning a language, and for taxi drivers learning a city. The brain responds to what you ask of it. The findings are a hopeful reminder that new learning, even in adulthood, can change the brain.

# Exercise and the memory center
@src Erickson et al., PNAS, 2011

In 2011 Kirk Erickson and his colleagues assigned 120 older adults to a year of either brisk walking three times a week or stretching and toning. Those in the walking group increased the size of their hippocampus, the brain's memory center, by about 2 percent, which was like reversing one to two years of age-related shrinkage. In the stretching group, the hippocampus shrank by about 1.4 percent, as is normal with age.

The walkers also improved in fitness and on a test of spatial memory. The study suggests that aerobic exercise can protect the brain, and even partly reverse some of the effects of aging. Exercise raises levels of a protein called BDNF, which supports the growth and survival of neurons. It's one of the best-supported ways of taking care of your brain.

# Does meditation change the brain?
@src Hölzel et al., 2010, 2011
@conf mixed

In 2011 Britta Hölzel and her colleagues scanned the brains of people who had just completed an eight-week program of mindfulness-based stress reduction, and compared them with people on a waiting list. The meditators had more gray matter in the hippocampus, and in areas associated with self-awareness and empathy. In a related 2010 study, the same team found less gray matter in the amygdala, in line with the participants' lower reported stress.

The study was small and the findings haven't always been repeated. Reviews of the field suggest that meditation does change the brain in measurable ways, but that the effects are modest, and many studies are poorly controlled. It's a promising area, and worth watching, but cautious claims are better than "meditation rewires your brain." What we can say is that sustained practice of anything seems to leave a mark.

# The sound that is not there
@src McGurk & MacDonald, Nature, 1976

In 1976 Harry McGurk and John MacDonald made a video of a woman saying "ga" over a recording of her voice saying "ba." When people watched the film, they didn't hear "ba," and they didn't hear "ga." They heard "da." When they closed their eyes, they heard "ba" correctly.

It's called the McGurk effect, and it's a vivid demonstration that hearing speech involves the eyes as well as the ears. The brain combines what it sees and hears into a single perception, and when they conflict, it makes a compromise. It works even when you know about it. It helps to explain why talking on the phone with a bad connection is harder than in person, and why it's easier to understand someone whose face we can see.

# The hand that wasn't yours
@src Botvinick & Cohen, Nature, 1998; Armel & Ramachandran, 2003

In 1998 Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen sat people at a table with one arm hidden behind a screen. A rubber hand was placed in front of them, where their own hand might be. The experimenter then stroked the hidden real hand and the visible rubber hand at the same time with a small paintbrush. After a minute or so, many people began to feel that the rubber hand was their own.

If the stroking was out of time, the illusion disappeared. In later experiments, when the rubber hand was threatened, with a knife or a hammer, people flinched, and showed a stress response in their skin. The experiment shows that our sense of owning a body is built by the brain, from the match between what we see and what we feel. It's a flexible construction that can be fooled, and it's now used to study conditions such as phantom limbs and chronic pain.

# Time seems to slow down when you fall
@src Stetson, Fiesta & Eagleman, PLoS ONE, 2007

People who have been in accidents often say that time seemed to slow down. In 2007 Chess Stetson, Matthew Fiesta and David Eagleman tested whether this reflects a real increase in the brain's ability to perceive. They had volunteers fall backward from a platform 150 feet high into a net, while wearing a device on their wrist that flashed numbers at a speed just too fast to be read. If time were slowed, the numbers might be readable.

They weren't. The volunteers couldn't read the numbers any better in the fall. But when they were asked afterward to estimate how long the fall had taken, they thought that it lasted about a third longer than other people estimated while watching. The researchers concluded that the slowing is an effect of memory. In a frightening event, the brain lays down a denser record of it, and a rich memory feels longer.

# The scan that found a pulse in a dead fish
@src Eklund, Nichols & Knutsson, PNAS, 2016
@conf mixed

Functional MRI has produced many of the best-known images in brain science, but its statistics are tricky. In 2016 Anders Eklund and his colleagues tested the standard software on data from hundreds of people at rest, who were doing no task at all. They then looked for a false alarm, a pattern that the software would report as brain activity. The software gave false alarms far more often than it should, with rates up to 70 percent in some settings, where 5 percent was expected.

The finding caused alarm because thousands of studies had used the software. Later analysis suggested that the problem was real but smaller than first feared, and affected certain methods most. The lesson isn't that brain scans are useless. It's that colorful images need to be read with caution, and that the way a result was calculated matters as much as the picture.

# Playing a risky game with the head
@src Mez et al., JAMA, 2017
@conf mixed

Repeated blows to the head can cause a disease called chronic traumatic encephalopathy, or CTE, which can be diagnosed only after death, by examining the brain. In 2017 Jesse Mez and his colleagues published a study of 202 brains of former football players, donated by their families. They found signs of CTE in 110 of the 111 brains of former NFL players, and in many from college and high school.

The number is striking, but the sample isn't representative, because families often donated brains when they suspected a problem. So it doesn't show what fraction of all players are affected. It does show that the disease is real, and linked to exposure to repeated head impacts. The findings have led to rule changes in many sports, and to more attention to protecting young players.

# The disease that has a name and a date
@src Alzheimer, 1906

In 1901 a German doctor, Alois Alzheimer, examined a 51-year-old woman named Auguste Deter, who had been brought to a hospital in Frankfurt with severe memory loss, confusion and paranoia. When she was asked her name, she could say it. When asked her husband's name, she said, "My husband." She said of her problem, "I have lost myself."

After she died in 1906, Alzheimer examined her brain under the microscope and found two things: clumps of protein between the cells, now called plaques, and tangled fibers inside them, called tangles. He reported them that year, and the condition was named after him. More than a century later, plaques and tangles are still central to how we understand the disease, and treatments that target them are only beginning to show effects.
