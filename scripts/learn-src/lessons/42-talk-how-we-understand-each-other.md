# We start to sound alike
@src Pickering & Garrod, Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 2004

When two people talk, they begin to line up with each other without trying. They pick the same words for things, the same way of building a sentence, and even the same pronunciation and speed. In 2004 Martin Pickering and Simon Garrod proposed that this alignment is the basis of conversation. Each thing one person says primes the other to use similar words and structures in reply.

On this view, we don't need to build a complicated model of what the other person thinks. As the alignment spreads across levels, from sounds to words to meaning, the two minds get to a shared picture. It helps to explain why talking to someone is easier than talking to a wall. It also explains why we pick up other people's phrases and accents, and why the people we talk to most come to sound like us.

# Names we invent together
@src Brennan & Clark, JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 1996

Imagine that you and a friend are looking at a set of shoes, and that one of them is a loafer. You call it the loafer. Later, you refer to it that way again. Susan Brennan and Herbert Clark studied this in 1996. Two people worked together to match pictures of objects, and they were free to describe them however they liked.

They found that partners settled on a way of referring to each object, and they kept it even when a different, more general term would have done. They called it a conceptual pact. If a new partner came along, the speaker went back to more general words. A pact belongs to the pair. It explains why close friends and couples have private words and nicknames, and why the same speaker talks differently to a stranger.

# Why we look away to think
@src Glenberg, Schroeder & Robertson, Memory & Cognition, 1998

When someone asks you a hard question, you probably look away. You may stare at the ceiling or close your eyes. In 1998 Arthur Glenberg and his colleagues showed that this is a way of thinking. People were more likely to avert their gaze as questions got harder, and the more difficult the question, the more often they looked away.

Looking away also helps. People who closed their eyes or looked away answered difficult questions better than people who kept looking at what was around them, including the face of the person asking. The idea is that the environment, and especially a face, pulls on your attention. Looking away frees your mind. So if someone seems to ignore you when you ask something hard, they may be doing exactly what they need to do.

# The shape of a story
@src Labov & Waletzky, 1967; Labov, Language in the Inner City, 1972

If someone says, "You'll never guess what happened to me yesterday," what do they say next? In the 1960s the linguist William Labov collected stories that ordinary people told about their own lives, such as fights and near-misses. He and Joshua Waletzky found that, although the stories were very different, they tended to share a shape.

They begin with a short summary, and then set the scene, saying who, when and where. Then comes the trouble, the event that moves the story. Along the way, the storyteller adds comments about why it matters. The story ends with the outcome, and often a last line that brings the listener back to the present. The comments are the key part, since they are what turns a list of events into a story. It's useful if you want to tell one well.

# Reading with a child, not to a child
@src Whitehurst et al., Developmental Psychology, 1988

Reading to a young child is a good habit. But how you do it matters. In 1988 Grover Whitehurst and his colleagues taught parents of two-year-olds a method called dialogic reading. In it, the parent stops to ask the child questions about the book, and doesn't just read the words straight through. The parent listens to the answer, adds a little to it, and asks something more.

After a few weeks, the children whose parents read this way were using more words than the children who were read to in the usual way. Their speech had developed faster. The important change is that the child becomes the storyteller, and the parent the audience. Pointing at a picture and asking, what's that, and what is it doing, is easy to do. It turns a quiet activity into a conversation.

# We shout without meaning to
@src Lombard, 1911

In a noisy restaurant, you find that you're talking more loudly, without deciding to. In 1911 the French doctor Étienne Lombard noticed that people raise their voices when there is noise around them. It isn't a choice. The effect, which carries his name, happens even when people are asked not to do it, and it is found in many animals, including birds and whales.

When we hear background noise, we automatically speak louder, and also higher, and with the vowels drawn out. This helps the listener, but it adds to the noise, so everybody in a crowded room gets louder. It's a reason why a room can become unbearably loud so quickly. It also shows that speaking isn't just a one-way act. We listen to ourselves, and we adjust to what we hear.

# I-words and low mood
@src Edwards & Holtzman, Journal of Research in Personality, 2017; Carey et al., 2019
@conf mixed

Researchers have noticed that people who are depressed tend to use more first-person singular pronouns, such as I, me and my. In 2017 Tom Edwards and Nicholas Holtzman combined the results of twenty-one studies, with nearly four thousand people. They found a small but reliable link between depression and the use of those words, when people write or speak about themselves.

The effect was small, and not large enough to use as a test of any one person. A later analysis suggested that it's not specific to depression. It seems to go with negative emotion in general, including anxiety and anger. A likely explanation is that when we feel bad, we focus on ourselves. So the words are a sign and not a cause. Counting your own pronouns won't tell you much, but the finding shows how language reflects the mind.

# The nuns and the autobiographies
@src Snowdon et al., JAMA, 1996

In the 1990s David Snowdon studied a large group of elderly nuns in the United States. Many of them had written a short autobiography when they joined the order, at about the age of twenty-two. The records had been kept for decades. The researchers scored the essays for how many ideas were packed into each sentence, and for how complex the grammar was.

Decades later, nuns whose early writing had been low in idea density were more likely to do poorly on thinking tests in old age, and more of them developed Alzheimer's disease. Those whose essays had been rich in ideas were much less likely to. The nuns had similar lives and education, which makes the comparison cleaner. It doesn't mean that writing causes protection, but it suggests that the roots of late-life thinking can be seen early.

# Couples share a memory
@src Wegner, Erber & Raymond, JPSP, 1991

In a long relationship, one person may remember where the keys go and the other the birthdays. In 1985 the psychologist Daniel Wegner called such a system transactive memory. Each person holds part of what they both need, and knows what the other knows. In 1991 Wegner and his colleagues tested it with couples and with pairs of strangers.

People were asked to learn lists of items and recall them together. Dating couples remembered more than pairs of strangers did, apparently because they already had a way of dividing the work. But when the couples were told how to split the task, they did worse, because it interfered with their natural system. The study suggests that part of a long relationship is a shared mind. It also helps to explain how hard it can be to lose one.

# Babies know words before they say them
@src Bergelson & Swingley, PNAS, 2012

A baby's first spoken word typically arrives at about a year. But understanding comes earlier. In 2012 Elika Bergelson and Daniel Swingley tested babies of six to nine months. Each baby sat on a parent's lap, in front of a screen that showed two pictures, such as an apple and a foot. A recorded voice said one of the words, and an eye tracker recorded where the baby looked.

The babies looked at the right picture more than chance would predict. They did it for common words, such as the names of foods and body parts, which they probably heard at home. So infants who can't speak yet are already learning what words mean. It's a reason to talk to babies, and to name what you see. They are listening, and building a vocabulary long before they say a word.

# Counting without numbers
@src Gordon, Science, 2004; Frank et al., Cognition, 2008
@conf mixed

The Pirahã, a small group of hunter-gatherers living along a river in the Amazon rainforest, have no words for exact numbers, only for roughly one, a few and many. In 2004 Peter Gordon tested whether that affects their thinking. He laid out a row of batteries and asked people to match it with a row of their own. With a small number, they did well. As the number grew, they made more mistakes.

It was taken as evidence that language shapes thought, since without number words, exact counting seemed to be out of reach. Later research found that the results depended on the task. In some tasks, the Pirahã performed accurately if they could match items one to one. The best summary may be that words for numbers are a tool. They make exact counting much easier, and without them people find other ways.

# Color words come in an order
@src Berlin & Kay, Basic Color Terms, 1969
@conf mixed

Languages differ in how many color words they have. In 1969 Brent Berlin and Paul Kay compared color terms in about a hundred languages. They noticed a pattern. A language with only two color words has terms for dark and light. If it has three, the third is red. Next come green or yellow, then blue, and so on.

This suggested that the names of colors develop in a common order, as if every language is following the same path. It was a famous finding, and the idea that there are universal tendencies in how we divide up color has held up in broad terms. But it has been criticized, for the way the data was gathered and because some languages don't fit well. It suggests there may be something shared in how humans see color, and a lot that is shaped by culture.

# Why digit span differs by language
@src Ellis & Hennelly, British Journal of Psychology, 1980; Stigler, Lee & Stevenson, Cognition, 1986

How many digits can you remember in a row? It depends partly on the language you speak. In the 1980s researchers found that Chinese speakers could hold more digits in mind than English speakers, and Welsh speakers held fewer than English speakers. It isn't that the people were smarter. The words for numbers differ in length.

Our short-term memory for spoken items seems to hold about as much as we can say in two seconds. Chinese number words are short, so more of them fit. Welsh number words take longer to say, so fewer fit. This idea comes from the work on the phonological loop in working memory. Memory depends on the time that the words take to say, as well as on the number of items. It's a reason to prefer a shorter way of saying a long number.

# The weight of a secret
@src Slepian, Chun & Mason, JPSP, 2017

Everyone keeps secrets. In 2017 Michael Slepian and his colleagues surveyed thousands of people about the secrets they were keeping. The average person was keeping a dozen or so at a time, and some of them were things they had never told anyone.

What made secrets heavy wasn't the effort of hiding them in conversations. That was surprisingly rare. It was how often the mind wandered back to the secret when the person was alone. People who thought about their secrets more often felt worse. It suggests that confiding in someone you trust, and not having to carry the secret alone in your head, can be a relief. It may be a reason to see a therapist or a friend.

# The look that lasts three seconds
@src Binetti et al., Royal Society Open Science, 2016

How long should you look someone in the eye? In 2016 Nicola Binetti and her colleagues asked people to watch video clips of faces that looked straight at them for different lengths of time, and say which duration felt most comfortable. They also measured the size of their pupils.

On average, people preferred looks of about three seconds. Shorter looks seemed cold, and longer ones felt intense. The preferred time varied from person to person, and it would depend on the situation and on how well you know the other person. It's a useful guide. If you find a conversation uncomfortable, it may help to know that most of us are comfortable with brief looks, and that no one is staring at you all the time.

# Laughs and screams are understood everywhere
@src Sauter, Eisner, Ekman & Scott, PNAS, 2010

Words differ from one language to another, but what about cries, laughs and sighs? In 2010 Disa Sauter and her colleagues tested whether emotions in nonverbal sounds are understood across cultures. They played recordings of English speakers making sounds such as screams, laughs and groans to listeners in Britain, and to the Himba, a remote people in Namibia with little contact with Western media.

The Himba listeners could tell, at better than chance levels, which sounds signaled anger, fear, disgust, sadness, amusement and relief. They did the same with the recordings made by their own people. The study suggests that some of our emotional sounds are shared across humanity. It fits the idea that before language, our ancestors communicated feelings by their voices.
