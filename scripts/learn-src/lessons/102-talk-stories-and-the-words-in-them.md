# One hundred fairy tales, thirty-one steps
@src Propp, Morphology of the Folktale, 1928

In the 1920s the Russian scholar Vladimir Propp read one hundred Russian fairy tales, from a famous collection made by Alexander Afanasyev. He did not ask what the tales meant. He asked how they were built. He looked at what the characters did, and ignored who they were, so that a prince killing a dragon and a peasant tricking a witch could count as the same kind of act.

He found that the tales were made from a set of just thirty-one actions, which he called functions, such as the hero leaving home, receiving a magic object, being tested, and marrying at the end. Not every tale had all of them, but when they appeared they came in the same order. He also found seven roles, including the villain, the helper and the false hero. His book, Morphology of the Folktale, was little noticed outside Russia until it was translated, in the 1950s, and then it shaped the study of stories.

# One story with a thousand faces
@conf mixed
@src Campbell, The Hero with a Thousand Faces, 1949

In 1949 the American scholar Joseph Campbell published a book arguing that myths from all over the world tell one story. He called it the monomyth. A hero leaves the ordinary world, often after a call that he at first refuses. He crosses a threshold, meets helpers and enemies, and passes through a great ordeal. He wins a prize, and returns to the ordinary world, changed, with something to give.

The pattern has been used by writers, and the filmmaker George Lucas has said that the book influenced Star Wars. Scholars have criticized it. They say that Campbell chose the stories that fit, that he flattened the differences between cultures, and that many stories do not follow the pattern at all. It is more useful as one lens on stories than as a law about them. It shows why so many tales of leaving home and coming back feel familiar.

# The oldest epic found in a library of clay
@series history
@src Smith, Transactions of the Society of Biblical Archaeology, 1873

In the 1850s, archaeologists digging in Nineveh, in what is now Iraq, found the library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal. It held tens of thousands of clay tablets, written in cuneiform. The city was destroyed in 612 BC, and the library was buried in the fire. The tablets went to the British Museum, where for years they waited to be read, and one of those who learned to read them was George Smith, a self-taught assistant.

In November 1872 Smith found a fragment of a tablet that told of a great flood, in which a man was told by a god to build a boat, and so survived. He read his translation to a meeting in London on December 3, 1872, and caused a sensation, because it resembled the story of Noah. It was the eleventh tablet of the Epic of Gilgamesh, a poem about a king of Uruk and his search for a way to escape death. Its oldest parts are Sumerian poems from about 2100 BC.

# The poem that nearly burned
@series history
@src Beowulf manuscript, about AD 1000; Tolkien, 1936

Beowulf is the longest surviving poem in Old English, of about 3,180 lines. It tells of a hero who kills a monster called Grendel, and Grendel's mother, and later a dragon. It survives in a single manuscript, written about AD 1000, in a bound volume that is now called Cotton Vitellius A.xv. Nobody knows when the poem itself was composed, and estimates range over several centuries.

In October 1731 a fire broke out at Ashburnham House in London, where the Cotton collection of manuscripts was kept. The manuscript was scorched, its edges were left brittle, and some of the text was lost in the years afterward as the pages crumbled with handling. It was saved, though, and in 1936 the scholar J. R. R. Tolkien gave a lecture, Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics, arguing that the poem should be read as a work of art, with the monsters at its center.

# The fox and the grapes
@src Aesop, about 600 BC; Elster, Sour Grapes, 1983

A fox sees a bunch of ripe grapes hanging high on a vine, and leaps again and again, but cannot reach them. At last he walks away, saying that they were probably sour. The fable is attributed to Aesop, a storyteller of ancient Greece, whom tradition says was a slave. We do not know for sure if he lived. The phrase sour grapes entered English from the fable, and means to scorn what you cannot have.

In 1983 the philosopher Jon Elster used the fable to name a pattern in human desire, in his book Sour Grapes. People tend to adjust what they want to what they think they can get, without noticing that they are doing it. He called it adaptive preference formation. It is not the same as choosing to want less. It happens without choice. It raises a question about our wants. Are they truly ours, or have they been trimmed to fit our circumstances?

# A scientific word that became an insult
@src Goddard, 1910; Pinker, The Blank Slate, 2002

In 1910 the American psychologist Henry Goddard proposed a scheme for sorting people with intellectual disabilities, at a meeting of the American Association for the Study of the Feeble-Minded. Earlier categories were idiot and imbecile, which were used for those with the most severe disabilities. He invented a new word for the mildest group, moron, from a Greek word that means foolish. They were people with a mental age of between eight and twelve.

All three words were technical terms, used by doctors and in laws. Over time they passed into everyday speech as insults, and by the later twentieth century experts dropped them, and chose new words, such as mentally retarded and later intellectual disability. The linguist Steven Pinker calls this pattern the euphemism treadmill. A polite word takes on the stigma of the thing it names, so people look for another, and the new one in turn can become an insult.

# Baker and baker
@src McWeeny et al., British Journal of Psychology, 1987; Cohen, 1990

Why do we forget people's names so easily? In 1990 the psychologist Gillian Cohen did an experiment that isolated one reason. She showed people photographs of strangers, and told them either that the man's last name was Baker, or that he was a baker. A few days later they were asked to recall what they had been told about each person. They remembered the occupation more often than the name.

The word was the same in both cases. It was the use that differed. An occupation brings associations, such as bread and ovens, and an early-morning start, which link to what you already know. A surname is just a label, and has little to hold on to. An earlier study by McWeeny and colleagues, in 1987, found the same thing. It suggests a way to remember names. Attach it to something meaningful, such as a picture, a rhyme or a story.

# The language that came first
@src Tolkien, Letter 165, 1955; A Secret Vice, 1931

J. R. R. Tolkien was a professor of Old English at Oxford. He was also a person who made up languages for pleasure, from his youth. In 1931 he gave a lecture, called A Secret Vice, in which he described how much he enjoyed the relation between sound and meaning in a language that he had made. The two languages that he built most fully were Quenya, which is partly modeled on Finnish and Latin, and Sindarin, which is modeled on Welsh.

In a letter of 1955 he said that people had the order wrong. He had not invented the languages to suit the stories. The languages came first, he said, and the stories were made to give a world to them. The Lord of the Rings grew from there. A language needs a history, a people and places, and so he invented them. It is a sign of how closely words and the worlds that they describe belong to each other.
