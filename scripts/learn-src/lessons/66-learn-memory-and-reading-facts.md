# Sleep protects what you learned
@src Jenkins & Dallenbach, American Journal of Psychology, 1924

In 1924 John Jenkins and Karl Dallenbach, two psychologists at Cornell University, ran a small but famous experiment. Two students learned lists of nonsense syllables, such as ZOF and LUB. Then, after intervals of one, two, four or eight hours, they tried to recall them. Sometimes they had gone to sleep after learning, and sometimes they had stayed awake and gone about their normal day.

After sleeping, they remembered much more than after the same time awake. The difference was clearest after a few hours. During waking, new experiences seemed to interfere with the old ones. During sleep, the memories were left alone, and perhaps strengthened. The study was small, but it started a long line of research that now shows how sleep helps the brain to consolidate learning. If you've just learned something, a nap or a night's sleep is a good way of keeping it.

# The item that stands out
@src von Restorff, Psychologische Forschung, 1933

Look at this list, and then try to recall it: table, chair, bed, lamp, banana, sofa, desk. Most people remember banana. In 1933 the German psychologist Hedwig von Restorff showed that an item that is different from the others in a list is remembered better. It can be different in color, size, meaning or shape. This is called the isolation effect, or the von Restorff effect.

The reason is that the unusual item gets extra attention. The brain notices what is different, and spends more effort on it. The effect is used by designers and advertisers, who make something stand out on a page. For studying, it means that if you want to remember a key point, highlight it sparingly. If everything is marked, nothing stands out. It also suggests that attaching a strange image to a fact can help you to remember it.

# Remembering ten thousand pictures
@src Standing, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1973

How good is human memory? In 1973 Lionel Standing at Bishop's University in Canada ran an experiment with an extreme number of items. He showed people up to ten thousand photographs, one at a time, for a few seconds each. Afterward he showed them pairs of pictures, one old and one new, and asked which they had seen.

They picked the right one about eighty-three percent of the time, even when they had seen ten thousand. It was much better than their performance with words, which was lower. Pictures seem to be remembered in a different, richer way, which is called the picture superiority effect. The result is one reason why teachers and speakers use images, and why turning an idea into a mental picture may help you to remember it.

# Letting a gadget hold your memory
@src Risko & Gilbert, Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 2016

When you set a reminder on your phone, or write a note on your hand, you are using the world to carry some of the load of your thinking. In 2016 Evan Risko and Sam Gilbert called this cognitive offloading. It's a sensible strategy. We have limited mental capacity, and tools can extend it.

Offloading has benefits and costs. Writing down a task frees your mind, and may help you to attend to something else. But in experiments, people who offloaded information remembered less of it later, and when the tool was taken away, they did worse. When people trust a device to remember, they tend to put less effort into encoding. A good rule is to offload what you only need to keep for a while, such as appointments, and to work to remember what you want to know and use.

# Does a GPS weaken your sense of direction?
@src Dahmani & Bohbot, Scientific Reports, 2020
@conf mixed

Drivers who use a GPS may worry that they are losing their own sense of direction. In 2020 Louisa Dahmani and Véronique Bohbot, at McGill University, studied this. They asked fifty people about their use of GPS, and then tested their ability to find their way and to form a mental map of places they knew. In a second part, they followed participants for three years.

People who relied more on GPS did worse at forming mental maps, and the decline continued over the three years in those who used it most. The study was not a controlled experiment, so it's possible that people with a poor sense of direction use GPS more. But the authors suggest that when we follow turn by turn directions, we use habit-based parts of the brain, and not the hippocampus, which makes maps. Using a map, or trying to navigate yourself sometimes, may keep that skill in practice.

# Training a memory athlete
@src Dresler et al., Neuron, 2017

Are memory champions born, or made? In 2017 Martin Dresler and his colleagues in the Netherlands and Germany tested whether ordinary people can learn the methods of memory athletes. They took people with average memory, and trained a group for six weeks, for thirty minutes a day, in the method of loci. This is the technique of placing the items to be remembered along a familiar route, in imagination.

After training, the group's recall of a list of words more than doubled. Brain scans showed that their pattern of connections had changed, and had become more like that of the champions. Four months later, much of the improvement was still there. The people had not become smarter in general. They had learned a trick. It shows that the skills of a memory athlete can be learned by anyone, with practice.

# Number games for four-year-olds
@src Siegler & Ramani, Journal of Educational Psychology, 2008

Some children start school knowing a lot about numbers, and some know little. In 2008 Robert Siegler and Geetha Ramani gave preschoolers from low-income families a simple game, played for about one hour in total over two weeks. It was a board game with squares in a row, numbered from one to ten. The children took turns moving a token, and said the numbers aloud as they moved.

After playing, the children were better at telling which of two numbers is bigger, at estimating the position of a number on a line, and at counting. The benefits lasted for nine weeks. Children in a control group, who played a similar game with colors, did not improve. The game gives children a sense of numbers as positions along a line. It's a cheap and effective way of preparing children for school.

# The reading wars
@src National Reading Panel, 2000

For decades, teachers argued about how to teach children to read. One side favored phonics, teaching the connections between letters and sounds. The other favored whole language, in which children learn to read by exposure to meaningful text and guessing words from the context. In 1997 the United States Congress asked a panel of experts, the National Reading Panel, to review the evidence.

In 2000 it reported that systematic phonics instruction was more effective than instruction without phonics, particularly for young children and for those who were at risk of reading failure. It also said that other skills matter, including the ability to hear sounds in words, fluency, vocabulary and comprehension. The findings were debated, but later research has supported the importance of explicit teaching of the sound system. The best programs combine phonics with rich language and reading for meaning.

# How fast do we read?
@src Brysbaert, Journal of Memory and Language, 2019

How many words per minute can a person read? Teachers and speed-reading courses often give very high figures. In 2019 Marc Brysbaert reviewed almost two hundred studies of silent reading by adults, covering many languages. He worked out an average, taking account of the kind of text.

For non-fiction, the average adult reads about two hundred and thirty-eight words a minute, with a comprehension of about seventy-five percent. For fiction, it's about two hundred and sixty. Reading aloud is slower, at about one hundred and fifty. Skilled readers can reach more than three hundred words a minute, and a few reach four hundred, with a small loss in understanding. The claims of speed readers of a thousand words or more are not borne out by tests.

# Do hard-to-read fonts help you learn?
@src Diemand-Yauman, Oppenheimer & Vaughan, Cognition, 2011; Xie, Zhou & Liu, 2018
@conf contested

In 2011 Connor Diemand-Yauman, Daniel Oppenheimer and Erikka Vaughan reported a surprising result. Students who studied material printed in a hard-to-read font, such as Monotype Corsiva or Comic Sans Italicized, remembered it better than those who studied it in a plain font. The idea was that the difficulty makes people think more deeply. They also tested it in a high school, where students whose classroom materials were in the odd fonts did slightly better.

It was widely reported, and some teachers began to change the fonts of their worksheets. But other labs tried to repeat the finding, and mostly failed. A review of many studies by Xie and colleagues in 2018 found no benefit overall. It seems that making text harder to read doesn't help, unless the difficulty is the right kind. Desirable difficulties, such as spacing and self-testing, work. Ugly fonts don't.
