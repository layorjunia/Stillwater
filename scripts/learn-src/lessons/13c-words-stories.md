# Disaster: an ill-starred word

The word "disaster" means "ill star." It comes from the Italian disastro, built from dis-, meaning bad, and astro, meaning star. It's from astrology. People used to believe that a calamity was caused by an unfavorable position of the stars and planets, and so the word began as a way of saying that the heavens were against you.

Today we use the word for any sudden catastrophe, with no thought of the sky. It's a nice example of how a word can keep a picture from an old belief long after the belief is gone. Other words, such as "consider," which may come from a Latin phrase meaning "with the stars," and "influence," which once meant the flowing of power from the stars, have a similar background. The sky used to be part of everyday explanations.

# Enthusiasm: the god within
@src Oxford English Dictionary

Today "enthusiasm" just means eagerness, but the word began as something religious. It comes from the Greek en theos, "having the god within." In ancient Greece, it described a prophet or priestess who was possessed by a god and speaking his words.

By the 1700s the word had broadened to mean any passionate fervor, and then, gradually, a cheerful interest. At first it was often used as an insult, since sober people in Europe found religious excitement alarming. Only later did it become a compliment. When you say you're enthusiastic about a plan, you are using a word that once meant being filled with a god. It's a lovely thing to be.

# Mortgage and bankrupt: words from money
@src Oxford English Dictionary

Two common money words have vivid pictures hidden in them. "Mortgage" means "dead pledge." It comes from Old French mort, meaning dead, and gage, meaning pledge. The idea is that the pledge of property "dies" in one of two ways: either when the loan is paid off, so the pledge is no longer needed, or when the borrower fails to pay and the property is lost.

"Bankrupt" means "broken bench." It comes from the Italian banca rotta. In Renaissance Italy, money changers did business at benches in the marketplace, and, according to the traditional story, if a banker failed and couldn't pay what he owed, his bench was broken, publicly, so that everyone would know. That's the origin of the word "bank," too, since a bank was a bench. Whether or not the story is exactly true, the image has stuck.

# Quarantine and curfew: words from old fears
@src Oxford English Dictionary

"Quarantine" means "forty days." It comes from the Italian quaranta giorni. In the era of the Black Death, port cities like Venice and Ragusa, now Dubrovnik, made ships wait offshore for a period, first 30 days and then 40, to see whether plague appeared among the crew before they were allowed to land. The number 40 had a long history of use in the Bible and in medicine.

"Curfew" comes from the French couvre-feu, which means "cover the fire." In medieval towns, most buildings were wooden and had open fires, so a bell was rung each evening to tell people to cover their fires and go to bed, to prevent fires from burning down the whole town. The word later came to mean any rule about when you have to be home. Both words began as practical rules to protect a community.

# Muscle: a little mouse
@src Oxford English Dictionary

The word "muscle" means "little mouse." It comes from the Latin musculus, a diminutive of mus, "mouse." The Romans thought that a flexed muscle, like a bicep, moving under the skin, looked like a mouse running under a cloth.

So the name stuck, and has been passed down into English through French. It's a fine example of how people name things by what they look like. The Greeks did something similar, and their word for muscle, mys, also means mouse. Next time you flex your arm and watch the bump move, you can imagine what the ancient Romans saw. It's a charming picture, and it has lasted for two thousand years.

# Window and daisy: eyes in old words
@src Oxford English Dictionary

Two ordinary English words contain the word "eye." "Window" means "wind eye." The word comes from Old Norse vindauga, from vindr, "wind," and auga, "eye." Before glass was common, a window was just a hole in the wall that let in air and light, and that you could look through. English took the word from the Vikings, replacing the older "eyethirl," or "eye-hole."

"Daisy" means "day's eye." It comes from the Old English dægesege, which is "day" plus "eye." The name fits the flower, which opens its petals in the morning, when the Sun comes up, and closes them at night, like an eye opening and closing. It's a rare word that has changed little in a thousand years. Both words show how people described the world in simple pictures.

# Nice: a word that changed sides
@src Oxford English Dictionary

When it first entered English, in the 1300s, "nice" meant "foolish" or "ignorant." It comes from the Latin nescius, "not knowing," through Old French. So the word started out as an insult.

Over the next 600 years, the meaning drifted: to "fussy," then "precise" or "delicate," as in "a nice distinction," and finally, in the 1700s, to "agreeable" and "kind." Traces of the old senses remain. When we say that someone has a "nice sense of balance," or when we talk of "the nicety of the argument," we're using the middle meaning. It's a good reminder that words don't stay still, and that a word's origin does not tell you what it means now.

# Decimate and trivial: words that drifted
@src Oxford English Dictionary

In ancient Rome, if an army unit had mutinied, its commander could order that every tenth soldier, chosen by lot, be killed as punishment. That's decimation, from the Latin decem, ten. The modern sense, to destroy a large part of something, came later, and some people still insist that the word should be used only for one in ten. Language, however, has moved on, and most dictionaries now accept the wider meaning.

"Trivial" comes from the Latin trivialis, "of the crossroads," from tri, "three," and via, "road." The idea is of a place where three roads meet, where people exchanged common talk and gossip. What was said there was ordinary, so the word came to mean unimportant. The same root gave medieval schools their "trivium," the three basic subjects. Both words started with vivid, precise pictures, and drifted away from them.

# Latin pictures: companion, candidate and calculate
@src Oxford English Dictionary

Three English words started out as little pictures in Latin. "Companion" means "one with whom you share bread." It comes from the Latin com, "with," and panis, "bread." The same root gives us "pantry," the room where bread is kept, and the French word "copain," for pal. A companion, in the old sense, was someone you ate with, which says a lot about friendship.

"Candidate" means "clothed in white." It comes from the Latin candidatus, from candidus, "bright white." In ancient Rome, people who ran for public office wore specially whitened togas, to look pure and honest, and to stand out in a crowd. "Calculate" means "to count with pebbles." It comes from the Latin calculus, a small stone used for counting on a board. The same word gives us "calculus," and in medicine a calculus is a small stone, such as a kidney stone.

# Three Greek words: sarcasm, ostracize and panic
@src Oxford English Dictionary

"Sarcasm" comes from the Greek sarkazein, which means "to tear flesh," as dogs do, and later "to speak bitterly." It entered English in the 1500s. The image is of a remark meant to cut, to wound with biting irony. It's a fitting name for a type of humor that says the opposite of what it means, in order to sting.

"Ostracize" comes from a piece of pottery. In ancient Athens, citizens could vote to send away a person who was seen as a threat to the city, for ten years, by scratching his name on an ostrakon, a broken piece of pottery, used like a ballot. Archaeologists have found thousands of these shards. "Panic" comes from the Greek god Pan, the goat-legged god of shepherds and wild places. The Greeks believed that sudden, unexplained terror in the hills was caused by Pan, who could cry out and frighten flocks.

# Greek myth in words: tantalize, mentor, narcissism, clue
@src Homer, Odyssey; Ovid, Metamorphoses

In Greek myth, King Tantalus offended the gods, and as his punishment, he stood in the underworld in water up to his chin, beneath branches of fruit. Whenever he bent to drink, the water drained away, and whenever he reached for the fruit, the wind blew it out of reach. To tantalize is to tease someone with something that they can see but not have. In Homer's Odyssey, Mentor was an old friend of Odysseus, who was left in charge of Odysseus's household and who advised his young son, Telemachus. His name has become a word for any wise and trusted adviser.

Narcissus was a beautiful young man who fell in love with his own reflection in a pool. In the Roman poet Ovid's version, he couldn't tear himself away, and wasted away, and was turned into a flower. Sigmund Freud and later psychologists used the name for an excessive love of oneself. And "clue" began as a ball of thread, spelled "clew." The hero Theseus used one to find his way out of the Minotaur's labyrinth: he unrolled it behind him on the way in and followed it back.

# Invented words: nostalgia, robot, serendipity
@src Hofer, 1688; Čapek, R.U.R. (1920); Walpole, 1754; Milton, 1667

Most words grow slowly, but a few were made up by named people. "Nostalgia" was coined in 1688 by a Swiss medical student, Johannes Hofer. He put together the Greek nostos, "return home," and algos, "pain," to describe the severe homesickness that he saw among Swiss soldiers fighting abroad. For about 200 years, doctors treated nostalgia as a real disease, with symptoms like fever and loss of appetite. "Robot" comes from the Czech robota, which means forced labor or drudgery. It was first used in the 1920 play R.U.R. by the Czech writer Karel Čapek, in which artificial workers rebel against humans. Čapek said that he got the word from his brother, the painter Josef.

The writer Horace Walpole made up "serendipity" in a letter in 1754, from a Persian fairy tale about three princes of Serendip, who made happy discoveries of things they weren't looking for. And the poet John Milton coined "pandemonium" for Paradise Lost, in 1667, as the capital of Hell, from the Greek pan, "all," and daimon, "demon." It later came to mean any wild, noisy confusion.

# Boycott: a man's name
@src Oxford English Dictionary

Unlike most words, this one comes from a real man's name. Charles Boycott was a land agent in County Mayo, Ireland, in 1880, who refused tenants' demands for lower rents. In response, tenants, shopkeepers and even the postman refused to deal with him. His workers left, and neighbors would not serve him at shops or deliver his mail. The fields had to be harvested by soldiers.

The newspapers described it as "boycotting," and within a year the word was used across Europe and America. The word spread so fast because it named something that people were ready to use. Captain Boycott himself did not enjoy the fame. He moved away and later returned to England, and he found that his name had become a verb.

# Arabic gifts: alcohol, zero and cipher
@src Oxford English Dictionary

English has borrowed a good many words from Arabic, especially in science and trade. "Alcohol" is one of them. It comes from al-kuḥl, which originally meant a very fine powder of antimony, used as eye makeup. Later the Arabic term was applied to anything refined to its essence by a process like distillation, and when it passed into Latin and European languages, it came to mean the spirit that is distilled from wine. The prefix "al-" is the Arabic word for "the," and it appears in many other English words, such as algebra and algorithm.

Two more words came from the same Arabic one. Arabic sifr means "empty," and it was a translation of the Sanskrit shunya, the Indian word for zero. "Cipher" came into English first, in the 1300s, and it came to mean both zero and, later, secret code. "Zero" arrived through Italian in the 1600s. So when you say "zero," you are using a word that has traveled from India to the Arab world to Italy and then to us.
