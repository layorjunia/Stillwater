# How long does a habit take?
@src Lally et al., European Journal of Social Psychology, 2010

You may have heard that it takes 21 days to form a habit. The number has no solid source. In 2010 Phillippa Lally and her colleagues at University College London followed 96 volunteers, each of whom chose a new habit to build, such as drinking a glass of water at breakfast or doing a short run after work. Every day, they reported how automatic it felt.

On average, it took 66 days for a behavior to reach its plateau of automaticity, and the range was wide, from 18 days to 254 days. Simple habits formed faster than complex ones. One missed day didn't make much difference to the overall progress. So there's no magic number. Be patient, and start with something small, since the task of building a habit is mostly a matter of repeating it in the same situation until it becomes automatic.

# Habits belong to places
@src Wood, Tam & Witt, JPSP, 2005

Habits are tied to settings. In 2005 Wendy Wood and her colleagues surveyed students who were transferring to a new university. They asked about behaviors such as exercising, reading the newspaper and watching TV, and they asked how the habits had changed. Students whose environment changed the most were the ones whose habits changed most.

Those who'd had a strong habit before, and whose new setting didn't have the old cues, were able to act on their intentions. If the setting stayed the same, the old habit tended to continue, whatever they intended. It's the reason that new habits are easier to start on vacation or after a move, and old ones are difficult to break at home. To change a habit, change the context. Move the TV, change the route, or rearrange the room.

# Nearly half of what we do
@src Wood, Quinn & Kashy, JPSP, 2002

How much of our daily life is habit? In 2002 Wendy Wood, Jeffrey Quinn and Deborah Kashy asked students to keep a diary at several moments in the day and note what they were doing, where, and whether they'd been thinking about it. About 43 percent of the time, students were doing something that they did almost daily, in the same place, and many were thinking about something else while they did it.

Habits free up the mind for other things, and that's their value. They also mean that a large portion of our behavior doesn't come from fresh decisions. It's a reason to be gentle with yourself about willpower. If you want to change a pattern, it's easier to alter the cues around you than to fight your habits by force of will every time.

# Treat yourself while you work
@src Milkman, Minson & Volpp, Management Science, 2014

In 2014 Katherine Milkman and her colleagues tested a way of making exercise more attractive. They loaded gripping audiobooks, such as The Hunger Games, onto iPods, and gave them to gym-goers in a study. Some participants were allowed to keep the iPod and listen whenever they wanted. Others could listen only at the gym, and the iPod was kept locked there between visits.

Those who could enjoy the audiobooks only while exercising went to the gym more often, at least at first. The researchers called the idea temptation bundling: pairing something you should do with something you want to do. It's an easy habit to try. Save a favorite podcast for the treadmill, or a favorite show for when you fold laundry, and let the want pull the should.

# A fresh start
@src Dai, Milkman & Riis, Management Science, 2014

Why do so many people start diets on Monday, or make resolutions on January 1? In 2014 Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis looked at gym visits and Google searches for the word "diet," and found that they rise at the start of a week, a month, a year and after a birthday or a holiday. People are more likely to take up a goal at moments that mark a new chapter.

They call it the fresh start effect. Such dates create a mental break from our past selves, so that we think of earlier failures as belonging to someone else. It's not magical, but it's useful. If you want to begin something, don't wait for the perfect plan. Pick the next natural beginning, such as the first of the month, and let it do some of the work.

# Tying your own hands
@src Ashraf, Karlan & Yin, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2006

In Greek myth, Ulysses had himself tied to the mast of his ship so he could hear the Sirens' song without steering onto the rocks. Economists call it a commitment device: a choice made now that limits what you can do later. In 2006 Nava Ashraf, Dean Karlan and Wesley Yin offered a savings account in the Philippines in which people couldn't withdraw their money until they'd reached a goal that they set.

Many took it up, and those who were offered the account saved considerably more after a year than those who weren't. People who knew themselves to be impulsive were especially likely to choose it. It's a sign of self-awareness to use such a tool. If you know that you'll be tempted, arrange things so that the temptation is hard to act on: automatic transfers, a friend who expects you, or a gym class that you've paid for.

# The goal gets closer
@src Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, Journal of Marketing Research, 2006

In the 1930s the psychologist Clark Hull noticed that rats ran faster as they got closer to the food at the end of a maze. In 2006 Ran Kivetz and his colleagues found the same pattern in people. They studied customers at a café who used a loyalty card that gave a free coffee after ten purchases. As customers got closer to the tenth purchase, they bought more frequently.

After they'd collected their reward, they slowed down again. The effect is called the goal gradient. Motivation rises as the finish line seems near. It means that large goals feel more manageable when they're broken into stages, each with its own visible milestone, and that a progress bar can keep us going.

# A head start
@src Nunes & Drèze, Journal of Consumer Research, 2006

In 2006 Joseph Nunes and Xavier Drèze tested the effect of a head start at a car wash. Customers were given a loyalty card. One card needed eight stamps for a free wash and was blank. The other needed ten stamps, but already had two stamps on it, so it too needed eight more. Either way, you'd need eight more washes.

The people who'd been given the card with two stamps already on it were considerably more likely to complete it, and sooner. The researchers called it endowed progress. We feel more committed to a task if we think we've already begun it. In practice, it's helpful to start any big task by doing something small, even trivially, so that you can see that you've already begun.

# The power of small wins
@src Amabile & Kramer, The Progress Principle, 2011

In a study that took years, Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer asked 238 employees in several companies to keep a daily diary about their work, and collected nearly 12,000 entries. They analyzed what made the best days. It wasn't recognition, money or even teamwork. It was making progress in meaningful work, even a small step.

They called it the progress principle. On days when people felt that they'd moved forward, they were happier, more motivated and more creative, and on days of setbacks, the reverse. It suggests that the key to motivation is to organize work so that you can see progress, by breaking projects into steps and keeping a record of what you've done. A list of finished tasks may do more for your morale than a list of what is left.

# What kind of praise helps
@src Mueller & Dweck, JPSP, 1998

In 1998 Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck gave fifth graders a set of puzzles, and told them that they'd done well. Some children were praised for ability: "You must be smart at this." Others were praised for effort: "You must have worked hard." Then the children were offered a choice of a harder or easier next task.

Most of those praised for intelligence picked the easy one, to protect their image. Those praised for effort chose the harder one. After a failure, the intelligence group enjoyed the task less and performed worse, and when they were asked to report their scores to other children, they were more likely to inflate them. The study suggests that praise for talent can make children fear failure. Praise for effort and strategy helps them to keep trying.

# Goals that work
@src Locke & Latham, American Psychologist, 2002

For more than thirty years, Edwin Locke and Gary Latham studied what kinds of goals lead to better performance, in hundreds of experiments in labs and workplaces. Their finding was simple. Specific and difficult goals lead to better performance than easy goals or vague ones such as "do your best."

The reasons are that a clear goal directs attention, increases effort and persistence, and makes you think of strategies. There are conditions. People need to accept the goal, and receive feedback on their progress, and have the ability and resources to reach it. Overly hard goals set for a complex new task may backfire, because people focus on the outcome and don't learn. A good goal is specific, challenging and within reach.

# What children's self-control predicts
@src Moffitt et al., PNAS, 2011

In the early 1970s, researchers in Dunedin, New Zealand, began to follow more than 1,000 children born in a single year. In 2011 Terrie Moffitt and her colleagues reported on what had happened to them by age 32. They'd measured the children's self-control in childhood using tests and reports from parents, teachers and the children themselves.

Children with lower self-control were more likely, as adults, to have poor health, to have money problems and to have a criminal record. The link held after the researchers took account of intelligence and family background. And among siblings in the same family, the one with better self-control did better. Self-control is a skill that can be taught and strengthened. It's one of the most useful things a child can learn.

# The willpower battery that did not charge
@src Baumeister et al., JPSP, 1998; Hagger et al., 2016
@conf contested

In 1998 Roy Baumeister and his colleagues brought hungry students into a room that smelled of fresh-baked cookies. Some were allowed to eat the cookies, and others had to eat radishes instead. Then they were given a puzzle that was impossible to solve. The radish eaters gave up sooner. The researchers proposed that self-control is like a muscle that tires, and called it ego depletion.

The idea became hugely influential, with hundreds of studies. But when 23 laboratories ran the same experiment in 2016, led by Martin Hagger, with more than 2,000 participants, the overall effect was close to zero. The theory is now widely doubted, at least in its simple form. Feeling tired of effort is real, but it may be more about motivation and attention than about a store of willpower that runs out.

# The task that nags
@src Masicampo & Baumeister, JPSP, 2011

In the 1920s Bluma Zeigarnik, a student in Berlin, noticed that waiters could remember complicated orders while they were unpaid, and forgot them after paying. She proposed that unfinished tasks stay active in memory. Her results have been mixed in later studies, but the experience of unfinished business nagging at us is familiar.

In 2011 E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister had people think about a goal that they hadn't finished, such as an important exam. Their minds kept returning to it during another task, and it hurt their performance. But when they made a specific plan, writing down what they'd do and when, the nagging stopped, though the task was still unfinished. It suggests that the mind needs a plan more than completion. Write down the next step, and you can put the task out of your head.

# Why we put things off
@src Steel, Psychological Bulletin, 2007

In 2007 Piers Steel combined the results of hundreds of studies of procrastination. He found that people put things off when the task is unpleasant, when they're impulsive and easily distracted, when they doubt their ability, and when the reward is far away. He summed it up in an equation: motivation rises with confidence and the value of the reward, and falls with delay and with how easily you are distracted.

Later work, including by Timothy Pychyl and Fuschia Sirois, suggests that procrastination is really about managing mood. We avoid the task because it brings up unpleasant feelings, and delay offers short-term relief. This suggests making the task less aversive by starting with the easiest part, setting a short timer, and forgiving yourself when you slip, since self-blame tends to make it worse.

# Deadlines you set yourself
@src Ariely & Wertenbroch, Psychological Science, 2002

In 2002 Dan Ariely and Klaus Wertenbroch asked three classes of students at MIT to turn in three papers. One class could choose their own deadlines, and the second had deadlines set for them at evenly spaced dates. The third could turn the work in at any time before the end of the term.

Students who set deadlines for themselves spread them out, which showed that they knew they'd put things off. They did better than the class that had only a final deadline. But the class with deadlines set by the teacher did best of all, with the highest grades. The study shows that precommitment helps, and it also shows that we're not very good at setting deadlines strict enough for ourselves. An outside deadline is a gift.

# Choosing for now and for later
@src Read, Loewenstein & Kalyanaraman, 1999

In 1999 Daniel Read, George Loewenstein and Shobana Kalyanaraman asked people to choose movies to rent. Some chose three movies to watch over the next few days, one at a time, and others picked all three at once, for three different times. When the choices were made all together, people chose more highbrow films, such as foreign films and historical dramas. When they chose one at a time for tonight, they went for light comedies and action films.

The result shows our tendency to make plans for our future selves that our present selves don't follow. We choose healthy and cultured for next week, and easy and fun for tonight. A good approach is to plan ahead, when you're thinking about the long term, and make it easy to follow through. Prepare meals on Sunday so that on Wednesday night, the right choice is the convenient one.

# Hungry judges
@src Danziger, Levav & Avnaim-Pesso, PNAS, 2011
@conf mixed

In 2011 Shai Danziger and his colleagues analyzed more than 1,000 decisions by Israeli parole judges. They found that the proportion of rulings in favor of prisoners started high, at about 65 percent, at the start of each session, and fell to nearly zero just before a break. After the break for food, it jumped back up. The paper became a famous example of how tired and hungry decision makers become harsher.

The study has been criticized. Other researchers pointed out that the order of cases wasn't random, since prisoners without lawyers were scheduled at the end of each session, and that these cases are less likely to succeed. Later analyses suggest that the effect is much smaller than reported, if it exists. The finding is a good reminder that vivid results need to be checked. It's still a sound idea to schedule hard decisions when you're fresh and fed.

# Creativity on a walk
@src Oppezzo & Schwartz, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 2014

In 2014 Marily Oppezzo and Daniel Schwartz at Stanford tested whether walking affects creative thinking. People did a test in which they had to think of as many unusual uses as possible for common objects, such as a button. They did it while sitting, and while walking on a treadmill, or while being pushed in a wheelchair around campus, and indoors and outside.

Walking, either on the treadmill or outside, boosted creative output compared with sitting, by an average of about 60 percent in the test. The effect was on the flow of new ideas, and it lingered a little after the walk. It was less helpful for tasks needing a single correct answer. Many writers and thinkers have said that they do their best thinking while walking, and now there's some evidence for it.

# The benefit of letting it rest
@src Sio & Ormerod, Psychological Bulletin, 2009

When you're stuck on a problem, is it better to keep working or take a break? In 2009 Ut Na Sio and Thomas Ormerod combined the results of 117 experiments on what's called incubation, in which people put a problem aside for a time. They found a clear benefit: those who took a break did better, mostly on creative, open-ended problems.

The effect was larger when people had spent longer working on the problem before the break, and when the mind was occupied with an undemanding activity during it, such as a simple task, and smaller when people did nothing or did something hard. The explanation may be that the mind goes on working in the background, or that a break lets you drop a wrong approach. Taking a walk, or washing dishes, may be a good way to solve a stubborn problem.

# Bored into creativity
@src Mann & Cadman, Creativity Research Journal, 2014

In 2014 Sandi Mann and Rebekah Cadman at the University of Central Lancashire asked people to do something very dull: copy numbers from a phone book. Others skipped the dull task. Then everyone did a creative test, in which they had to come up with as many uses as possible for a pair of plastic cups.

The people who'd done the boring task came up with more, and more creative, ideas. The researchers suggested that boredom makes the mind wander and search for stimulation, which leads to daydreaming and new associations. It's a small study, but it fits other work. It suggests that constant entertainment from a phone, filling every gap, may crowd out the quiet that creative thoughts come from.

# The cost of an interruption
@src Mark, Gudith & Klocke, 2008; Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert, 2015

In studies of office workers, Gloria Mark found that people are interrupted or switch tasks about every three minutes. In one analysis, it took on average about 23 minutes to return to the original task after an interruption, though people often did other work in between. The figure is quoted widely, though it comes from observation of specific workplaces.

Even a small interruption has a cost. In 2015 Cary Stothart and his colleagues found that when people's phones simply buzzed with a notification during a task, without being picked up, they made more mistakes than people who weren't notified. Hearing the alert was enough to scatter attention. If you need to concentrate, silence your phone, and let it wait.
