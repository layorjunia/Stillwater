# Why your fingers prune in water
@src Kareklas, Nettle & Smulders, Biology Letters, 2013

After a long bath, the skin on your fingers and toes wrinkles. It used to be explained as water soaking into the skin, but that turned out to be wrong. The wrinkling is an active response. The nervous system narrows the blood vessels under the skin, which makes the surface pucker. People whose nerves to the fingers are damaged don't get wrinkles.

In 2013 Kyriacos Kareklas and his colleagues tested what the wrinkles are for. People picked up wet objects, such as marbles, and moved them from one container to another. They were quicker with wrinkled fingers than with smooth ones. With dry objects, there was no difference. The wrinkles seem to work like the tread on a tire, channeling water away, which gives a better grip on wet things.

# The baby with three hundred bones
@src Standard human anatomy

A newborn baby has about three hundred bones. An adult has two hundred and six. The difference isn't that bones are lost. Many of the baby's bones are made partly of cartilage, and they fuse as the child grows. The skull is the clearest example. A newborn's skull is made of several plates, with gaps between them called fontanelles, the soft spots on a baby's head.

The gaps have a purpose. They let the skull squeeze a little during birth, and they give the brain room to grow quickly in the first years. They close over time, most within the first one or two years. Bone is also living tissue. It is constantly broken down and rebuilt, so that most of the adult skeleton is replaced over about ten years.

# A new stomach lining every few days
@src Standard gastroenterology

The stomach produces acid strong enough to dissolve metal, yet the stomach doesn't digest itself. It's protected by a thick layer of mucus and by a lining of cells that is constantly renewed. The cells on the surface of the lining live only a few days, and they are replaced by new ones from stem cells below.

If the protection fails, the acid can damage the lining, and cause an ulcer. For many years, doctors thought that ulcers were caused by stress and spicy food. In the early 1980s Barry Marshall and Robin Warren showed that most are caused by a bacterium, Helicobacter pylori. Marshall famously drank a culture of it to prove his point. They shared the Nobel Prize in 2005. Ulcers can now be cured with antibiotics.

# The most common kind of color blindness
@src Standard vision science

Color blindness is often misunderstood. It rarely means seeing in black and white. Most people with it have trouble telling red from green, because one of the three kinds of color-sensitive cone cells in the eye is missing or works differently. About one in twelve men of European descent has this, and about one in two hundred women.

The reason for the difference is that the genes for the red and green cones are on the X chromosome. A man has one X, so a single faulty copy means color blindness. A woman has two, and would need both to be faulty. Many people don't know they have it until they're tested. Designers can help by not depending on color alone, using patterns, labels or shapes as well.

# Do some women see more colors?
@src Jordan & Mollon, Vision Research, 1993; Jameson et al., 2001
@conf mixed

Most people have three kinds of cones in their eyes. Some women may have four. The genes for two of the cone types lie on the X chromosome. A woman has two X chromosomes, and if the two copies differ slightly, she may carry two different versions of a cone, in addition to the other two. In theory, this could give her four types of color receptor, and the ability to tell apart colors that look the same to others.

In 1993 Gabriele Jordan and John Mollon found a woman who seemed to have four cone types. Later work, especially by Kimberly Jameson, found that some carriers can do slightly better on special color tests. But most carriers, perhaps nearly all, don't show an obvious benefit. The idea of a superhuman color sense is intriguing, but the evidence for it is thin.

# How the fastest heart beats
@src Standard comparative physiology

A hummingbird's heart can beat more than a thousand times a minute when the bird is flying. The bird's wings beat dozens of times a second, and it hovers in the air, which is very costly in energy. It needs to eat a large part of its body weight in nectar every day, and it can drop into a deep rest called torpor at night, when its heart rate falls to a small fraction of its daytime rate.

By contrast, a blue whale's heart can beat only a few times a minute when the whale dives. In general, smaller animals have faster hearts and shorter lives. Across many kinds of mammals, the number of heartbeats in a lifetime comes out close to the same, with a few exceptions, humans among them. It's a rough rule, and it shows how closely body size and the pace of life are linked.

# The longest migration
@src Egevang et al., PNAS, 2010

The Arctic tern breeds in the far north in summer and spends the other half of the year around Antarctica. In 2010 Carsten Egevang and his colleagues attached tiny light recorders to terns in Greenland. The devices kept track of the birds' positions as they flew south and north again.

The terns didn't fly in a straight line. They followed a winding route down the Atlantic and back, riding the winds. The round trip was about seventy thousand kilometers a year, the longest known migration of any animal. Because of the long days at both ends of the journey, the terns are thought to see more daylight than any other creature on the planet. A tern lives for about thirty years, so it may fly a distance equal to two or three round trips to the Moon.

# Eleven thousand kilometers without landing
@src Gill et al., Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 2009

The bar-tailed godwit is a plump shorebird that breeds in Alaska. In the fall, it flies to New Zealand and Australia. In 2007 researchers tracked godwits with small satellite tags, and followed one female for a flight of more than eleven thousand kilometers, across the Pacific Ocean, without stopping to eat, drink or rest. It took her about eight days.

Before the flight, godwits greatly increase their weight by eating, and they shrink some of their digestive organs to make room for fat. During the flight, they burn that fat. It's the longest known nonstop flight of any bird. It shows how migrants can push the limits of physiology.

# How salmon find home
@src Hasler & Scholz, Olfactory Imprinting and Homing in Salmon, 1983

A salmon is born in a stream, swims to the ocean, and years later returns to spawn in the same stream where it hatched. How does it find the way? In the 1950s the biologist Arthur Hasler suspected that it was smell. Fish that were taken from a stream and had their noses plugged could not find their way home.

In experiments in the 1960s and 1970s, Hasler and his student Allan Scholz treated young salmon with a chemical, morpholine. Years later, when the adult fish swam back, they were drawn to streams into which the researchers had put the same chemical. Salmon imprint on the smell of their home stream when they are young, and follow it back as adults. They use other cues, such as the Earth's magnetic field, on the open sea, but the final steps are guided by scent.

# The butterfly that takes four generations
@src Standard entomology; Reppert & de Roode, 2018

Each fall, millions of monarch butterflies in eastern North America fly up to four thousand kilometers south to overwinter in the mountains of central Mexico. They have never been there. The butterflies that make the journey are not the ones that left the previous spring. The migration takes about four generations. Three generations live for a few weeks in the north, and the fourth is a special generation that lives for months.

The final generation flies south, spends the winter in the forest, and in spring flies north to lay eggs, and then dies. The butterflies navigate using the position of the Sun, and a clock in their antennae that compensates for its movement across the sky. They may also sense the Earth's magnetic field. How a butterfly knows where to go without having been there is still not fully understood.

# Ants that farm
@src Schultz & Brady, PNAS, 2008

Humans have farmed for about ten thousand years. Leafcutter ants have been farming for about fifty million. They carry pieces of leaves back to their underground nests, but they don't eat the leaves. They chew them into a paste on which they grow a fungus, and the fungus is their food. The ants tend the garden, weeding out other molds and feeding the fungus fresh leaf material.

In 2008 Ted Schultz and Seán Brady studied the family tree of fungus-growing ants and their fungi. They concluded that agriculture arose once, about fifty million years ago, and has since spread into hundreds of species. The ants even use bacteria on their bodies that make antibiotics to protect the garden from disease. A single nest can have millions of ants, with a division of labor between those who forage, those who farm and those who defend.

# The ant that counts its steps
@src Wittlinger, Wehner & Wolf, Science, 2006

A desert ant in the Sahara goes out looking for food, moving in a winding path, sometimes for hundreds of meters. When it finds a dead insect, it runs straight home, in a nearly straight line. There are no landmarks on the sand. How does it do it? Part of the answer is a kind of dead reckoning. The ant keeps track of the direction it has gone, using the Sun, and of the distance.

In 2006 Matthias Wittlinger and his colleagues tested how the ant measures distance. They attached tiny stilts to some ants, and clipped the legs of others. The ants with stilts overshot their nest on the way home, and the ants with shortened legs stopped short. The ants seem to count their steps, in effect, like a pedometer. It's a remarkable feat for a brain the size of a grain of sand.

# The shrimp that punches like a bullet
@src Patek et al., Nature, 2004; Thoen et al., Science, 2014

The mantis shrimp is a small, colorful animal that lives on the seafloor, and it has one of the fastest strikes in the animal kingdom. Some species have clubs that accelerate as fast as a bullet leaving a small gun. The strike is so fast that it creates a bubble in the water, which collapses with a snap and a flash of light, adding a second blow. It can break the shell of a crab or crack the glass of an aquarium.

The mantis shrimp's eyes are also extraordinary. They have up to sixteen kinds of photoreceptors, compared with our three. It was thought that this gave them an amazing range of color vision. In 2014 Hanne Thoen and her colleagues showed that they are actually poor at telling similar colors apart. It seems they identify colors quickly by recognizing them, without comparing, which may speed up decisions.

# A mammal that lays eggs
@src Standard zoology; Warren et al., Nature, 2008

The platypus looks like a collection of parts from different animals, with a duck's bill, a beaver's tail and an otter's feet. It's a mammal, since it has fur and feeds its young milk, but it lays eggs. It is one of only five living species of egg-laying mammal, called monotremes. The others are the echidnas. When the first specimen was sent to Britain in 1799, scientists suspected a hoax.

The platypus has other odd features. Its bill can sense the electrical fields produced by the muscles of its prey, so it can hunt with its eyes shut. The males have a venomous spur on their hind legs. In 2008 researchers sequenced its genome and found a mix of mammal, reptile and bird genes. It sits on an ancient branch of the mammal family tree.

# An animal that regrows its limbs
@src Standard developmental biology

The axolotl is a salamander that lives in a lake near Mexico City. It has a remarkable ability. If it loses a leg, it grows a new one that is perfect, with bones, muscles and nerves. It can regrow its tail, parts of its heart and spinal cord, and even parts of its brain, without scars.

When a limb is lost, cells at the wound form a clump called a blastema, which acts like a set of instructions for building a new limb. Scientists are working to understand how this happens, in the hope that human tissue could one day be coaxed to do the same. Humans can regrow only a few things, such as the liver and the tip of a child's finger. Wild axolotls are critically endangered, though many are kept in laboratories and as pets.

# Bumblebees that learn from each other
@src Loukola, Perry, Coscos & Chittka, Science, 2017; Alem et al., PLoS Biology, 2016

In 2017 Olli Loukola and his colleagues taught bumblebees to do something they would never do in nature. A ball was placed at a distance from a platform with a reward, and the bees had to pull a string to move the ball to the reward. This is hard for a bee, and most untrained bees never solved it by themselves.

The researchers trained some of the bees, and then let other bees watch. The observers learned to do the task, and some even improved on the method. In later work, a skill taught to one bee spread through a colony as others watched. Insects were once seen as creatures of instinct. This result shows that a bee has a brain smaller than a sesame seed, and can still learn by watching, and can learn something new.

# Alex the parrot
@src Pepperberg, The Alex Studies, 1999

Irene Pepperberg bought an African gray parrot from a pet shop in 1977, and named him Alex. Over three decades, she trained him and tested what he understood. He didn't just repeat words. He could name dozens of objects, as well as colors and shapes, and answer questions about them.

Shown a tray of objects, Alex could be asked, "What color is the square one?" or "How many blue?" and give the right answer. He seemed to understand same and different, and could count small collections up to about six. He also asked for things, and once asked what color he was. It is debated how much of this reflects language as we know it. But the work showed that a bird's brain, which looks very different from ours, can do some of the same things.

# Dolphins have names
@src Janik, Sayigh & Wells, PNAS, 2006

Each bottlenose dolphin develops a signature whistle in its first year, a distinctive pattern that is like a name. Dolphins use it to identify themselves to others, and mothers and calves use it to find one another. In 2006 Vincent Janik and his colleagues tested what dolphins do when they hear another dolphin's whistle.

They played recordings of whistles to wild dolphins in Florida. A dolphin responded much more to the recording of the signature whistle of a close relative, or a long-term companion, than to that of a stranger. The recordings were made so that voice features were removed, which meant that the dolphins responded to the whistle pattern itself. Dolphins seem to use learned labels for individuals, which makes them one of the few animals other than humans known to do so.

# The whale that outlives empires
@src George et al., Canadian Journal of Zoology, 1999

The bowhead whale lives in the cold waters of the Arctic. It is a huge animal with a head that makes up about a third of its body, and it can break through ice that is several inches thick. In 1999 John George and his colleagues estimated the ages of bowheads by looking at chemical changes in the lens of the eye, which accumulate over a lifetime.

Some animals seemed to be more than a hundred years old, and a few over two hundred. Old stone harpoon points, of a kind that hunters stopped using long ago, have been found embedded in harvested whales. Bowheads seem to get cancer rarely, in spite of their size and their long lives, and scientists are studying why. Their genes suggest that the whales may be especially good at repairing damage to DNA.
