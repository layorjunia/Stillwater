# The feedback sandwich
@src Parkes, Abercrombie & McCarty, 2013; Henley & DiGennaro Reed, 2015
@conf mixed

The feedback sandwich is a popular way of giving criticism. You start with something positive, then give the criticism, and end on another positive note. The idea is that praise softens the blow. It has been taught in management courses for decades, and is common in schools and medical training.

The evidence for it is thin. In 2013 Jenny Parkes and her colleagues tested it with students. The sandwich changed how the feedback felt, but not how well the students performed. People may learn to expect a criticism after praise, so the praise loses its value. The main message can also be lost in the padding. Studies suggest that feedback works best when it is specific, timely, focused on the work and not on the person, and when it's clear what to do next. A sandwich is fine, but clarity matters more.

# Transformational leaders
@src Judge & Piccolo, Journal of Applied Psychology, 2004

Some leaders inspire their followers, and others only manage them. In the 1980s the political scientist James MacGregor Burns and the psychologist Bernard Bass described transformational leadership. These leaders articulate a vision, set an example, encourage new ways of thinking, and pay attention to the needs of each individual. Transactional leaders, in contrast, rely on rewards and punishments.

In 2004 Timothy Judge and Ronald Piccolo combined the results of dozens of studies. They found that transformational leadership was linked to better follower satisfaction, motivation and performance, more than transactional methods. The link was found across many types of organization. But most studies were surveys, in which the same people rated both the leader and the outcome, so the results may be inflated. It's still some of the best evidence that how a leader communicates matters.

# Humble leaders
@src Owens & Hekman, 2012; Owens, Johnson & Mitchell, Organization Science, 2013

We often picture a leader as someone confident, bold and sure of themselves. But in 2012 Bradley Owens and David Hekman interviewed leaders and followers in many types of organization, and asked what humility looked like. Leaders said that it meant admitting mistakes, spotlighting the strengths of others, and being open to feedback and new ideas.

A follow-up study in 2013 found that when leaders behaved in this way, followers were more engaged, and more likely to stay in their jobs. They also learned more, and teams performed better. A humble leader sets an example that makes it safe to say I don't know, or I was wrong. The finding goes against the stereotype of the heroic leader. Admitting a limit seems to make people trust you more, not less.

# A premium for height
@src Judge & Cable, Journal of Applied Psychology, 2004

Do tall people earn more? In 2004 Timothy Judge and Daniel Cable analyzed data from four large studies that had followed thousands of people through their working lives. They looked at the relationship between height and income, while taking account of age, sex and weight.

Each additional inch of height was linked to about eight hundred dollars more in annual earnings. For someone six feet tall, compared with someone five feet five, that amounted to thousands of dollars a year. The effect held for men and women. The researchers suggested that taller people may be seen as more leaderlike, and gain confidence. It's a striking example of bias in the workplace, working in ways that most of us don't notice.

# The beauty premium
@src Hamermesh & Biddle, American Economic Review, 1994

In 1994 the economists Daniel Hamermesh and Jeff Biddle asked a simple question. Do better-looking people earn more? They analyzed surveys in which interviewers had rated the physical attractiveness of workers on a scale. They then compared pay, while accounting for education, experience and other factors.

People who were rated as below average in looks earned several percent less than those of average looks. Those who were above average earned a few percent more. The penalty for plainness was bigger than the premium for good looks. The effect was found for both men and women, though it was a little stronger for men. It occurs in occupations in which looks should not matter. The gap was found in many later studies, and it's a form of discrimination that is rarely discussed.

# Open offices and fewer conversations
@src Bernstein & Turban, Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 2018

Open-plan offices are supposed to encourage collaboration, by removing walls. In 2018 Ethan Bernstein and Stephen Turban studied two companies that moved from traditional offices to open plans. The employees wore sensors that tracked who talked with whom, and the researchers also collected email records.

The result was the opposite of what was expected. Face-to-face interaction fell by about seventy percent. Email use increased. People seemed to withdraw, wearing headphones and avoiding the discomfort of being seen and heard. The researchers suggested that the lack of privacy made people less willing to talk. Open offices may bring benefits, such as cost savings. But if the aim is to get people to talk, the evidence suggests that taking down the walls may not work.

# Working from home
@src Bloom, Liang, Roberts & Ying, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2015

In 2010 Nicholas Bloom and his colleagues ran an experiment at Ctrip, a large travel agency in China, with a call center of thousands of employees. Employees who volunteered were randomly assigned to work from home for nine months, or to stay in the office. Their performance was tracked by the number of calls they handled.

The people who worked at home were thirteen percent more productive. About nine percent of that came from working more minutes per shift, with fewer breaks and sick days, and the rest from taking more calls per minute, in a quieter environment. They were also happier, and the rate of quitting fell by half. Some of them later chose to return to the office, because of loneliness. Home working was good for routine tasks, but promotions fell for those who stayed away.

# A little thanks
@src Grant & Gino, JPSP, 2010

Does saying thank you make people work harder? In 2010 Adam Grant and Francesca Gino ran an experiment with university fundraisers who called alumni to ask for donations. Their work was often met with rejection. One day, the director of fundraising visited some of them, and said, I am very grateful for your hard work. We sincerely appreciate your contributions. Others were not visited.

In the week after, the callers who had been thanked made about fifty percent more calls than those who had not. They felt more valued. In follow-up experiments, people helped a stranger more often after being thanked. The thanks wasn't a reward in money. It made people feel that their efforts mattered, and so they wanted to do more. It's a cheap and effective way of encouraging people, and few of us do it as often as we could.

# Meeting the person you're helping
@src Grant, Campbell, Chen, Cottone, Lapedis & Lee, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 2007

At a call center at a university, workers phoned alumni to ask for money for scholarships. It was a difficult job, and many found it boring. In a study in 2007, Adam Grant and his colleagues arranged for a student who had benefited from a scholarship to visit the call center. He spent five minutes telling the callers how the scholarships had changed his life.

A month later, the callers who had met the student were spending more time on the phone, and the amount of money that they raised was far higher, more than doubled, than that of the others. The callers themselves said that they had not noticed any change. Knowing the effect of what you do on a real person seems to make work more meaningful. It's a lesson for managers, and for anyone who wants to stay motivated.
