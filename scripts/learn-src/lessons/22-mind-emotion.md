# Two ways to manage a feeling
@src Gross, JPSP, 1998; Gross & John, JPSP, 2003

In 1998 James Gross showed people a disgusting film, a graphic medical procedure, and gave them different instructions. Some were told to watch naturally. Some were told to hold back any sign of emotion, which is suppression. Others were asked to think about the film in a detached, clinical way, which is reappraisal. The suppressors looked calm, but their bodies were more aroused than those of people who simply watched. The reappraisers felt less disgust to start with.

Later work found that suppressing also uses up mental effort, and that people who suppress during a conversation remember less of it. Gross and Oliver John also found that people who habitually reappraise tend to have better moods, closer relationships and higher well-being than those who habitually suppress. Reappraisal means changing how you read a situation, such as "this is a chance to learn" or "he's probably tired," before the emotion has fully formed. Suppression is clamping down after it has. The first changes the feeling, while the second just hides it.

# Naming it to tame it
@src Lieberman et al., Psychological Science, 2007

In 2007 Matthew Lieberman and his colleagues scanned people's brains while they looked at photographs of faces showing strong emotions, such as anger and fear. Seeing such a face normally causes activity in the amygdala, the brain's alarm system. But when people were asked to put a label on the emotion, by choosing a word like "angry" or "scared," activity in the amygdala dropped, and a region in the right front of the brain, involved in regulation, became more active.

The effect was small but consistent, and it matches what many therapists observe, that putting words to a feeling makes it less overwhelming. A good practice for a bad moment is to say what you feel, silently or aloud, as precisely as you can: not just "bad," but "disappointed" or "ashamed" or "overloaded." Naming the feeling gives your thinking brain something to hold onto.

# Are there six basic emotions?
@src Ekman & Friesen, 1971; Barrett et al., Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2019
@conf mixed

In the 1960s and 70s Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen showed photographs of faces to people in several countries, including the Fore people of Papua New Guinea who had little contact with the outside world, and found that people recognized expressions of happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust and surprise. They argued that these basic emotions and their faces are universal and built into us.

The idea shaped decades of research, and it's still much debated. In 2019 a team led by Lisa Feldman Barrett reviewed the evidence and concluded that facial expressions are less reliable than the theory says. People smile when they're happy, but they also smile when they're polite or nervous, and how well people judge a face depends on the context and on the culture. The face is a clue, but not a window.

# Do blind people smile?
@src Matsumoto & Willingham, JPSP, 2009

How much of our emotional expression is learned? In 2009 David Matsumoto and Bob Willingham looked at photographs of judo athletes at the Olympic and Paralympic Games, taken at the moment when they had won or lost a match. They compared sighted athletes with athletes who'd been blind from birth, and who'd never seen a face.

The blind athletes showed the same expressions. They showed joy when they won and sadness when they lost, in much the same pattern as sighted ones. It's strong evidence that some expressions of emotion come naturally and aren't imitated. Culture then shapes how and when we show them, but the basic gestures seem to be part of us.

# The genuine smile
@src Harker & Keltner, JPSP, 2001

A real smile involves the muscles around the eyes, and not only the mouth. It's called a Duchenne smile, after the French physician who described it in the 1800s. In 2001 LeeAnne Harker and Dacher Keltner studied the yearbook photographs of women who graduated from Mills College in California in the early 1960s, and measured how much positive emotion each woman showed.

The women who showed more genuine smiles in their photographs at about age 21 were more likely to be married by 27, and reported greater marital satisfaction and well-being at 52, decades later. It was a correlation, and a smile in a photo can't cause a happy marriage. But it suggests that some positive qualities are quite stable, and that they show in a face at an early age.

# How feelings spread
@src Hatfield, Cacioppo & Rapson, 1994; Kramer, Guillory & Hancock, PNAS, 2014

Emotions are contagious. In 1994 Elaine Hatfield, John Cacioppo and Richard Rapson described how we catch each other's feelings through small, automatic mimicry of faces, voices and posture. If someone near you is yawning, laughing or crying, you tend to feel a trace of it too.

It also spreads through screens. In 2014 researchers at Facebook, with Cornell collaborators, changed the posts that appeared in the feeds of about 689,000 users for a week. People who saw fewer positive posts wrote slightly fewer positive posts of their own, and slightly more negative ones. The effect was tiny, and the experiment raised ethical objections because users weren't asked. But it fits with the finding that moods travel from person to person.

# Is life better on a sunny day?
@src Schwarz & Clore, JPSP, 1983

In 1983 Norbert Schwarz and Gerald Clore phoned people on sunny days and on gloomy days and asked how satisfied they were with their lives. People were less satisfied on the cloudy days. Then they tried something different: before asking about life, they casually asked, "How's the weather down there?" When people were reminded of the weather, the difference disappeared.

The people had used their present mood as information about their lives, without noticing why they felt that way. When they were prompted to see that the weather was a likely cause, they stopped counting it. The result is a good illustration of how moods can bias our judgments. When you're making a big decision, it helps to ask whether the way you feel right now has to do with the matter, or with something else, like the rain.

# Why anger makes us bold
@src Lerner & Keltner, 2001

Different emotions with the same intensity can make us think in very different ways. In 2001 Jennifer Lerner and Dacher Keltner found that fear and anger, although both are negative, had opposite effects on risk. Frightened people saw the world as more uncertain, and made pessimistic and cautious choices. Angry people felt more certain and in control, and made optimistic and risky ones.

After the September 11 attacks, Lerner also found that Americans who were made to feel angry about the attacks later thought that terrorist attacks were less likely than those who were made to feel afraid. It means that decisions made in anger tend to be bolder, and less careful about the downsides. It's wise to wait until you're calm before you decide something big, and to ask what a fearful version of you would be worried about.

# The sprayed room
@src Schnall, Haidt, Clore & Jordan, 2008
@conf mixed

In 2008 Simone Schnall and her colleagues ran an experiment that got a lot of attention. People were asked to judge the morality of acts such as lying on a résumé or eating a dead pet. Some did it in a room that had been sprayed with a foul-smelling fart spray, and others in a clean room. Those in the smelly room made harsher moral judgments.

It was taken as evidence that physical disgust can feed into moral disgust. But later attempts to repeat the effect, with larger groups, have found it to be small or absent, and the topic became one of the examples that are used when people discuss the replication problem in psychology. The general idea, that our bodies influence our judgments in ways we don't notice, remains plausible. Any single experiment that seems too neat deserves a second look.

# The unexpected thing we hate
@src Wilson et al., Science, 2014

Could you sit in an empty room for 15 minutes with nothing to do but think? In 2014 Timothy Wilson and his colleagues asked college students to do it, with no phone, no book and no writing. Most found it unpleasant. In one version they were given a button that delivered a mild but unpleasant electric shock, which they'd earlier said they'd pay to avoid. Twelve of 18 men, 67 percent, and 6 of 24 women, 25 percent, gave themselves at least one shock.

The researchers concluded that people find it hard to be alone with their thoughts, and would rather do something, even something unpleasant, than nothing. The students were young, and one man shocked himself many times. But the broader result, from 11 experiments, was that people don't enjoy unstructured time with only their own thoughts. It's worth being kind to yourself about boredom. It's a mild and normal discomfort that the mind seeks to escape.

# Looking back fondly
@src Sedikides & Wildschut, 2016

Nostalgia was once treated as a disease, a kind of homesickness. Constantine Sedikides, Tim Wildschut and their colleagues have studied it for years, and found that it's mostly a pleasant, bittersweet feeling that arises when we think about happy times. It's often triggered by a song, a smell or a conversation, or by loneliness, and it usually involves other people.

In their studies, thinking about a nostalgic memory increased people's sense of meaning in life, strengthened their feeling of being connected to others, and boosted their self-esteem. It even made some people more generous and more willing to help. Nostalgia seems to be a resource that we use when we feel low. Letting yourself look through old photographs or play an old song can be a way of reminding yourself that your life has had good things in it.

# Why hope helps
@src Snyder et al., 1991

In the 1990s the psychologist Charles Snyder proposed that hope isn't just a vague feeling that things will get better. He argued that it has two parts: agency, the belief that you can act to reach a goal, and pathways, the ability to think of ways to do it. A hopeful person has a goal, a plan and a belief that they can carry it out, and when one route is blocked, can find another.

Students who scored high on his hope scale tended to get better grades, and people with high hope often coped better with illness and injury. Hope can be learned, by breaking big goals into steps, thinking of backup plans, and noticing evidence of progress. It's a more practical idea than wishing. Hope is something you do, as well as something you feel.

# Being kind to yourself
@src Neff, 2003; Breines & Chen, 2012

Kristin Neff proposed in 2003 that self-compassion has three parts: kindness toward yourself when you fail, instead of harsh criticism; seeing your struggles as part of being human, instead of feeling alone; and holding painful feelings in balance, without exaggerating them. She developed a scale to measure it.

Many people fear that being kind to themselves will make them lazy. In 2012 Juliana Breines and Serena Chen tested this. They asked students to think about a weakness or failure, and then to respond to it with self-compassion, or with self-esteem boosting, or neutrally. The self-compassion group spent more time studying for a test after failing and were more motivated to improve. It suggests that the harsh inner critic isn't a necessary driver. Treating yourself as you would a good friend seems to help you try again.

# Guilt and shame are different
@src Tangney & Dearing, Shame and Guilt, 2002

People often use "guilt" and "shame" as if they meant the same, but June Tangney and her colleagues have shown that they're quite different. Guilt is about what you did: "I did something bad." Shame is about who you are: "I am bad." Guilt tends to make people want to apologize and repair the damage. Shame makes them want to hide, and sometimes to lash out.

In many studies, people who were prone to shame were more likely to have problems with depression, anxiety and anger, while those prone to guilt generally did better and had better relationships. This suggests a useful habit. When you've done wrong, focus on the act, and ask what you can do to make it right, and not on what it says about you.

# The blush that wins you friends
@src Feinberg, Willer & Keltner, JPSP, 2012

Embarrassment feels awful, but Matthew Feinberg, Robb Willer and Dacher Keltner argued in 2012 that it has a social purpose. After someone breaks a norm, such as tripping or spilling a drink, showing embarrassment signals that they care about what others think and want to put things right. In their experiments, people who showed embarrassment after a mistake were trusted and liked more than those who didn't.

Observers also judged those who showed no embarrassment, or acted smug, more harshly. The researchers suggest that the blush evolved as a way of appeasing others and keeping social bonds. So next time you feel mortified, you might remember that the flush on your face is telling people something good about you.

# Does a good cry help?
@src Bylsma, Vingerhoets & Rottenberg, 2008; Gračanin et al., 2014
@conf mixed

Most people believe that crying makes you feel better. The research is more complicated. In laboratory studies, people who watched a sad film and cried often felt worse straight afterward and returned to their earlier mood after about 20 minutes, and in some studies felt better after an hour or two. Whether it helps seems to depend on the situation.

Lauren Bylsma and her colleagues found in 2008 that crying helped more when the person was in the presence of someone who was supportive, when it led to a resolution of the problem, and when the person had a sense of relief. It helped less when they felt embarrassed or were alone. Crying has a clear social function, which is to call for help, and it seems to work best when somebody answers.

# We get used to good things
@src Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman, JPSP, 1978
@conf mixed

In 1978 Philip Brickman and his colleagues compared lottery winners, people who'd become paralyzed in accidents, and a control group. The lottery winners weren't much happier than the others, and took less pleasure in everyday things. The accident victims were less happy than the controls, but not as unhappy as expected, and they expected a happier future.

The study is the origin of the idea of the hedonic treadmill: we adapt to changes, good and bad, and return to a baseline. It had a small sample and later work has complicated it. Ed Diener and others have found that people don't adapt completely to some events, such as the death of a spouse, long-term unemployment or severe disability. The picture is that we adapt more than we expect, but not entirely.

# Does more money mean more happiness?
@src Kahneman & Deaton, PNAS, 2010; Killingsworth, PNAS, 2021; Killingsworth, Kahneman & Mellers, PNAS, 2023

In 2010 Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton reported that emotional well-being rises with income up to about $75,000 a year in the US and then levels off. The finding was widely quoted as the price of happiness. In 2021 Matthew Killingsworth used a phone app that asked people how they felt at random moments, and gathered data from more than 30,000 workers. He found that happiness kept rising with income, with no plateau.

The two researchers, who disagreed, then worked together, with a third scientist as referee. In 2023 they reported that both were partly right. For most people, happiness continues to rise with income. But for the unhappiest group, it rises until about $100,000 and then stops, perhaps because their unhappiness has other causes that money doesn't fix. Money helps, and it's not the whole story.

# Buying time
@src Whillans et al., PNAS, 2017

In 2017 Ashley Whillans and her colleagues surveyed more than 6,000 people in four countries and found that those who spent money to save time, such as paying for a cleaner, a delivery or a shorter commute, were more satisfied with life. The effect held for rich and poor alike. Yet few people said they did it.

In a follow-up, working adults were given $40 one weekend to spend on a purchase that would save them time, and $40 another weekend to spend on something material. They reported feeling happier after the time-saving purchase, since it reduced the stress of not having enough hours. The finding suggests that, if you've the spare money, getting rid of a tedious task might do more for you than buying another thing.

# Emotional intelligence in perspective
@src Mayer & Salovey, 1990; Joseph & Newman, 2010
@conf mixed

The term emotional intelligence was introduced in 1990 by John Mayer and Peter Salovey, who defined it as the ability to perceive, understand and manage emotions. It was popularized in 1995 by Daniel Goleman's best-selling book, which claimed that it matters more than IQ for success in life, a claim that wasn't supported by the evidence.

Research since then has been mixed. Tests that measure real ability, like identifying the emotion in a face or story, show modest links to job performance and social skills. Questionnaires that ask people to rate themselves mostly overlap with personality traits such as agreeableness. So emotional skill is real and valuable, but it's less of a distinct superpower than the early claims suggested, and it doesn't replace other abilities.

# Feeling with, or feeling for
@src Singer & Klimecki, Current Biology, 2014

Empathy and compassion sound the same, but Tania Singer and Olga Klimecki have shown that they are different, in the brain as well as in experience. Empathy means sharing the other person's feelings, such as feeling pain when you see someone in pain. Compassion means feeling warmth and concern for the person, and wanting to help.

In their studies, empathy training alone sometimes led to distress and burnout, with more negative feelings. Compassion training increased positive feelings, and activated brain areas involved in reward and affiliation. Their work suggests that for helpers such as nurses and caregivers, the way to avoid exhaustion isn't less caring. It's turning shared suffering into compassion, which refreshes and doesn't drain.

# Pride has a posture
@src Tracy & Robins, 2008; Tracy & Matsumoto, PNAS, 2008

Pride is a puzzle for emotion researchers because it isn't one of the classic basic emotions, and yet it seems to be universal. In 2008 Jessica Tracy and Richard Robins showed that people can recognize pride from the body: the head tilted back slightly, the chest out and the arms raised or on the hips. People in the US and in a remote community in Burkina Faso, who'd had little contact with Western media, identified the display.

It was also found that people read pride quickly and automatically, and linked it to high status. Another study found that athletes from many cultures, including blind ones, showed the same pose after winning. Pride seems to signal achievement, and so to establish a place in the group. It can be a healthy reward for effort, though there's also a fragile, arrogant kind that tends to be hostile.
