# New Coke
@src Coca-Cola Company, 1985; Gladwell, 2005

In the early 1980s Coca-Cola was losing ground to Pepsi. In blind sip tests, many people preferred Pepsi, which is sweeter. So in 1985 the company changed its formula and launched New Coke. It had tested the new taste on about two hundred thousand people, and in the tests, the new formula beat both old Coke and Pepsi.

The launch was a disaster. Customers protested, and the company's phone lines filled with angry calls. Within about three months, the old formula was back on the shelves, as Coca-Cola Classic. The company had tested a sip, but people drink a whole can, and they also drink a brand with a history. The lesson for anyone doing research is that what people prefer in a test may not be what they value in life.

# Slow music, slow shoppers
@src Milliman, Journal of Marketing, 1982

In 1982 the researcher Ronald Milliman tested the effect of background music in a supermarket. For several weeks, he alternated between slow music and fast music, and measured how long shoppers took to walk through the store, and how much they spent.

When the music was slow, shoppers moved more slowly. And the store's sales were higher, by about a third, than on the days with fast music. The shoppers didn't notice the music's effect on them. Many later studies have shown that tempo can alter how we move and how much time we spend. It's one reason why a store's music is chosen carefully. If you're shopping, and you find you've walked slowly, the speakers may be part of the reason.

# Hearing it too often
@src Cacioppo & Petty, JPSP, 1979

How many times should a message be repeated? In 1979 John Cacioppo and Richard Petty had students hear a persuasive talk once, three times or five times. They measured how much the students agreed with it afterward, and what they thought of it.

For a good argument, a few repetitions made it more convincing. With more repetitions, people began to tire of it. They agreed less and thought of reasons against it, and the argument lost some of its force. It's called the wear-out effect. The best number of repetitions is not the maximum. That's true in advertising, and it's true when we are making a point to someone. If it hasn't worked after a few tries, saying it again may do harm.

# Why online reviews are mostly five stars or one
@src Hu, Zhang & Pavlou, Communications of the ACM, 2009

If you look at the reviews of a product online, you often see a lot of five-star ratings and a lot of one-star ratings, and few in the middle. The shape of the graph is a J. In 2009 Nan Hu, Jie Zhang and Paul Pavlou studied it and gave an explanation based on who writes reviews.

People who are happy with a purchase tend to write, and people who are upset also write, but the many people with a middling experience tend not to. The reviews are not a random sample of customers. The average star rating can therefore be a poor guide to how most buyers felt. It helps to read a few of the middle ratings, and to look at the number of reviews as well as the average.

# One early vote
@src Muchnik, Aral & Taylor, Science, 2013

In 2013 Lev Muchnik, Sinan Aral and Sean Taylor ran an experiment on a website where people post comments and others can vote them up or down. They randomly gave some new comments a first vote of approval, without the users knowing, and left others alone. Then they watched what happened to the comments over five months.

A comment that got a single early up-vote was about thirty percent more likely to receive another up-vote. And the effect piled up, so the final ratings of those comments were about a quarter higher on average. It was a case of herding. We are influenced by what others seem to like, and a small random start can grow. It's a reason to be careful about taking ratings and counts as signs of quality alone.

# Hits and luck
@src Salganik, Dodds & Watts, Science, 2006

Why do some songs become hits? Is it quality? In 2006 Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds and Duncan Watts built a website where people could listen to unknown songs and download the ones they liked. Visitors were split into groups. In one group, people made choices alone. In other groups, they could see how many times each song had been downloaded.

In the groups that could see the counts, the winners were different from group to group. A song that was a hit in one world could be a flop in another. And the more people were influenced by the counts, the more unequal and unpredictable the results became. Quality did matter. The very best songs rarely did badly, and the worst rarely did very well. But in between, luck and early momentum counted for a lot.

# The health halo
@src Chandon & Wansink, Journal of Consumer Research, 2007

If a restaurant says that its food is healthy, do people eat more or less? In 2007 Pierre Chandon and Brian Wansink asked people to estimate the calories in meals from fast food chains. Some of the chains advertised their food as healthy. People guessed that the meals from the healthy-sounding chains had fewer calories, though they did not. They also underestimated the calories more.

And people who thought a restaurant was healthy were more likely to order a side dish, such as fries or a soda, to go with their meal. The health claim worked as a halo. It made them feel they could afford extra. It's a good example of a halo effect. One good quality makes us assume other good qualities, and we may even be less careful because of it.

# The overfilled cup
@src Hsee, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 1998

In 1998 Christopher Hsee asked people how much they would pay for ice cream. One group saw a seven-ounce serving in a small cup that was overflowing. Another group saw an eight-ounce serving in a much larger cup, which looked only partly full. Each group saw only one of the two.

People were willing to pay more for the smaller serving in the overflowing cup. It looked generous and full, while the bigger serving looked skimpy. When people saw both servings at once, they said that the larger one was worth more. Hsee called it the less-is-better effect. We judge things by what's easy to evaluate, and not by what really matters. A good deal can look poor in a cup that's too big.

# Anchored by the asking price
@src Northcraft & Neale, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 1987

In 1987 Gregory Northcraft and Margaret Neale asked real estate agents and students to value a house. They gave each person a tour of the house and a packet of information that included the asking price. The researchers varied the asking price in the packets, so that some people saw a high figure and others a low one.

Both the students and the agents were swayed. Those who saw the high price valued the house at much more than those who saw the low price. The agents, who were experienced, said that they had ignored the listing price. The students, who were not experts, admitted that they had looked at it. Both gave themselves away in their numbers. Experts are not immune to anchoring, and they may be less aware of it.

# Judges and dice
@src Englich, Mussweiler & Strack, PSPB, 2006

In 2006 Birte Englich, Thomas Mussweiler and Fritz Strack asked experienced German judges to look at a case of shoplifting. Each judge was given the case file, and a prosecutor's recommendation for the sentence. Then they were told to throw a pair of dice to represent the recommendation, though the dice had been rigged to land on either a high number or a low one.

The judges who rolled a high number gave harsher sentences than those who rolled a low number. They knew that the numbers were random, and they still were influenced. Experience didn't protect them. The experiment is not the same as real life, since prosecutors don't pick their demands by dice. But it shows how an irrelevant number can sway even experts. It's a good reason to keep the first number you see from setting your own.

# Your neighbor's paycheck
@src Card, Mas, Moretti & Saez, American Economic Review, 2012

How much we earn isn't the only thing that decides whether we're happy with it. In 2012 David Card and his colleagues ran a field experiment with the employees of the University of California. Some employees were sent an email telling them about a website that listed everyone's pay. The others weren't. Afterward, everyone answered a survey about their job.

Workers who were paid less than the median for people in their unit and job reported lower satisfaction after learning where they stood. They were more likely to say that they would look for a new job. Those who were paid above the median were not happier. So the pain of being below was greater than the pleasure of being above. The study shows how much our feelings about pay depend on comparison.
