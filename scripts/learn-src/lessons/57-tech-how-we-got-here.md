# ENIAC and the women who programmed it
@src Light, Technology and Culture, 1999

ENIAC, finished in 1945 at the University of Pennsylvania, was one of the first large electronic computers. It filled a big room, and used about eighteen thousand vacuum tubes. It was built to calculate the paths of artillery shells. It could do in seconds what took people hours.

There was no software. To change the program, the operators had to rewire the machine by plugging cables, and setting thousands of switches. This job was given to six women, among them Kay McNulty, Betty Jean Jennings and Frances Bilas, who had been hired as human computers. They worked out how to program it from the wiring diagrams, with no manual. For many years, their role was not recognized in public. They are now regarded as among the first programmers.

# The first actual bug
@src Harvard Mark II logbook, 1947

In September 1947 a team working on the Harvard Mark II computer found that a relay was failing. When they looked, they found a moth trapped between its contacts. They removed it, and taped it into the logbook with the note: first actual case of bug being found. The page is now in the Smithsonian.

The word bug was used for faults in machines long before, and Thomas Edison used it in the 1870s. The joke in the logbook was that this time, the bug was a real one. Grace Hopper, who worked on the Mark II, often told the story, and helped to make it famous. The term debugging has since become the normal name for finding and fixing errors in code.

# UNIX and C
@src Ritchie & Thompson, Communications of the ACM, 1974

In 1969 at Bell Labs in New Jersey, Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie began to write a small operating system on an old minicomputer that nobody was using. They called it Unix. Its ideas were simple. Programs should do one thing well, and could be linked together, so that the output of one became the input of the next.

Ritchie then created a programming language, C, to rewrite Unix so that it could be moved to other computers. That was unusual at the time, since operating systems were written for specific machines. C and Unix spread through universities. Their descendants include Linux, macOS, Android and iOS. The language C is the basis of many others, including C++ and Java. Two people in a lab built the foundation of much of modern computing.

# The day the internet switched
@src Postel, RFC 801, 1981; Cerf & Kahn, IEEE Transactions on Communications, 1974

In the 1970s, several networks existed, but they couldn't talk to one another, since each used its own rules. Vint Cerf and Robert Kahn designed a common set of rules for sending data between networks, which broke information into small packets, labeled them, and reassembled them at the other end. It was called TCP/IP.

The ARPANET, the American network that had begun in 1969, switched over on January 1, 1983. It was called flag day, and everyone had to change at once. Computers that didn't were cut off. Many people regard that date as the birth of the internet, since it was the start of a single network of networks. The design didn't need a central controller, and any network that followed the rules could join. It's a reason why the internet grew so fast.

# PageRank
@src Brin & Page, Computer Networks, 1998

In 1996 two graduate students at Stanford, Larry Page and Sergey Brin, began to work on a better way to rank pages in web search. At that time, search engines mostly counted the words on a page. The results were often poor, and easy to manipulate by repeating a word many times.

Page and Brin had an idea from academic papers. A paper is important if many other important papers cite it. In the same way, a web page is important if many important pages link to it. They called the method PageRank, partly after Page's name. A page's score depends on the scores of the pages that point to it. In 1998 they founded Google. The core idea was to treat the links that people make as votes.

# The mother of all demos
@src Engelbart, 1968; Hiltzik, Dealers of Lightning, 1999

On December 9, 1968, in San Francisco, Douglas Engelbart gave a ninety-minute presentation to about a thousand computer scientists. Sitting at a console on a stage, with his colleagues connected by video, he showed things that no one had seen. He used a mouse to move a pointer on the screen, and showed windows, hyperlinks, text editing, and video conferencing.

It later became known as the mother of all demos. Engelbart's aim was to use computers to extend human intelligence, and not just to calculate. Some of his team later moved to Xerox PARC, in Palo Alto, where in 1973 they built the Alto, a computer with a graphical interface. Steve Jobs saw it on a visit in 1979, and the ideas went into the Apple Macintosh. It was a long way from the idea to the product.

# A disk the size of two refrigerators
@src IBM, 350 Disk Storage Unit documents, 1956

In 1956 IBM released the 305 RAMAC, the first computer with a hard disk drive. The disk unit held fifty metal platters, each two feet across, spinning, and could store about five million characters, or about five megabytes. It was as large as two refrigerators, and was leased to businesses for thousands of dollars a month.

A modern smartphone can store millions of times more, in a chip the size of a fingernail. The idea of storing data on spinning magnetic disks lasted for decades, and is still used for cheap storage in data centers. The growth in capacity has been one of the most remarkable trends in technology, and helped to make possible everything from photographs to streaming music.

# The first digital camera
@src Sasson, 1975; Eastman Kodak records

In 1975 Steven Sasson, a young engineer at Kodak, built the first digital camera. It was a clumsy box the size of a toaster, and weighed about eight pounds. It used a new kind of chip, a charge-coupled device, which converted light into electrical signals, and recorded the images on a cassette tape. The image was black and white, and was one hundredth of a megapixel.

It took about twenty-three seconds to record a picture, and a similar time to display it on a television. Kodak's management was unimpressed, and told Sasson that no one would want to look at photographs on a screen. The company made money from film, and was slow to move. Kodak went bankrupt in 2012. It's a famous case of a company that invented a new technology, and still failed to adapt.

# The first call on a cell phone
@src Cooper, Cutting the Cord, 2014

On April 3, 1973, Martin Cooper, an engineer at Motorola, stood on a street in New York and made the first call on a handheld mobile phone. He called a rival at Bell Labs to tell him that he was calling from a portable cell phone. The prototype was nicknamed the brick. It weighed over two pounds, and had a battery that lasted about twenty minutes.

It took another decade before the phone was sold. The Motorola DynaTAC 8000X was released in 1983, and cost almost four thousand dollars. Mobile networks, with many small cells, were needed to make it work. By the 2000s, billions of people had mobile phones, and in many countries, people got one before they had a landline. Smartphones, from 2007, turned them into pocket computers.

# CAPTCHA and the book scanners
@src von Ahn, Maurer, McMillen, Abraham & Blum, Science, 2008

A CAPTCHA is a puzzle, such as twisted letters, that humans can solve and software can't, used to prove that a user is not a robot. It was invented in the early 2000s by Luis von Ahn and his colleagues. Websites used it to stop programs from signing up for thousands of accounts.

Von Ahn realized that millions of people were spending a few seconds each solving them, and that the effort was wasted. In 2007 he created reCAPTCHA, which showed words that scanning software couldn't read, taken from old books and newspapers. When enough people agreed on a word, it was added to a digital copy. Over time, people typing in the words helped to digitize millions of books. Google bought the company in 2009.

# Linux
@src Torvalds & Diamond, Just for Fun, 2001

In August 1991 a Finnish student, Linus Torvalds, posted a message to an online discussion group. He wrote that he was doing a free operating system, just a hobby, that wouldn't be big and professional like the others. He had written the core of a system, called a kernel, for the processors in personal computers.

He released the code under a license that let anyone use it and change it, as long as they shared their changes. Programmers around the world began to contribute. Linux now runs most of the world's web servers, all of the top supercomputers, and the Android phones that billions of people use. It is one of the greatest examples of collaborative building, and of the way that a small idea can grow when it is shared.

# Pong
@src Kent, The Ultimate History of Video Games, 2001

In 1972 an engineer named Allan Alcorn, working for a new company called Atari, built a simple video game as a training exercise. It was a game of table tennis, with two paddles and a ball that bounced between them. They called it Pong. Atari put a prototype in a bar in California. A few days later, the owner called to say that it had stopped working.

When the engineers opened the machine, they found that the coin box was jammed with quarters. Pong became the first big commercial success in video games. Home versions came in 1975. Soon, dozens of companies made games. Pong was based on a game made for another system, the Magnavox Odyssey, and the makers of that system sued. It was a simple idea that opened an industry.

# Tetris and traumatic memories
@src Holmes, James, Coode-Bate & Deeprose, PLoS ONE, 2009
@series mind
@conf mixed

Tetris was created in 1984 by a Russian software engineer, Alexey Pajitnov. Players rotate falling blocks to fit them together. It's a simple game, and it became one of the most popular of all time. It's also associated with the Tetris effect, in which people who play for a long time see the shapes in their mind's eye, or in dreams.

In 2009 Emily Holmes and her colleagues in Oxford tested an unusual use. People watched a disturbing film, and then, after a delay of half an hour, some played Tetris for ten minutes, while others did nothing. In the following week, the Tetris players had fewer intrusive memories of the film. The idea was that the game uses the same visual-spatial resources as the memory. It's a small, early study, and it has been followed by trials. It's not a treatment.

# Password rules were wrong
@src NIST Special Publication 800-63B, 2017

For many years, the advice was that passwords should be changed every few months, and should contain a mix of upper case letters, numbers and symbols. The man who wrote the original guidance for the US government in 2003, Bill Burr, later said that he regretted much of it. In 2017 the National Institute of Standards and Technology published new guidelines.

The new advice was that length matters more than complexity, that passwords should be checked against lists of those already known to be breached, and that people should not be forced to change them regularly unless there is a sign of a compromise. The old rules led people to choose predictable changes, such as adding a number at the end. A long phrase that you can remember is better than a short, complicated one. A password manager is better still.

# Enigma and Bletchley Park
@src Kahn, Seizing the Enigma, 1991; Sebag-Montefiore, Enigma: The Battle for the Code, 2000

The Enigma was a machine that the German forces used in the Second World War to encrypt messages. It looked like a typewriter, with rotors that scrambled each letter, and the settings changed every day. The number of possible settings was astronomical, and the Germans believed it was unbreakable.

It was first broken not by the British, but by three Polish mathematicians, Marian Rejewski, Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski, in the 1930s. They gave their methods and a copy of the machine to the British and the French in 1939, just before the war began. At Bletchley Park in England, thousands of people, including Alan Turing, built on this work. They designed machines called bombes that searched for the settings. Historians have said that reading the messages shortened the war.

# Six degrees
@src Milgram, Psychology Today, 1967; Dodds, Muhamad & Watts, Science, 2003

In 1967 Stanley Milgram posted packets to random people in Nebraska and Kansas, and asked them to get them to a stockbroker in Boston by passing them to someone whom they knew personally. Of the packets that arrived, the average chain had about six links. From that, the phrase six degrees of separation arose.

Milgram's study had problems, since most of the packets never arrived. In 2003 Peter Dodds and his colleagues repeated it using email, with sixty thousand people in a hundred and sixty-six countries trying to reach one of eighteen targets. The chains that were completed had a median length of about six to seven. The world is a small one, because even though most of our friends know each other, a few long-range links connect distant groups.

# Dijkstra's twenty minutes
@src Dijkstra, Numerische Mathematik, 1959; Frana & Misa, Communications of the ACM, 2010

In 1956 Edsger Dijkstra, a young Dutch computer scientist, was shopping in Amsterdam with his fiancée. They stopped at a café, and he thought about a problem. How can you find the shortest route between two cities on a map? In about twenty minutes, he said later, he came up with an algorithm, without a pen or paper.

Dijkstra's algorithm works from the starting point, always extending the shortest path found so far, until it reaches the goal. He published it in 1959, in a paper of three pages. It is used in map software, in computer networks that route data, and in many other settings. He said that the lack of pencil and paper had pushed him toward a simple, elegant solution. He received the Turing Award in 1972.

# The perceptron and the first AI winter
@src Rosenblatt, Psychological Review, 1958; Minsky & Papert, Perceptrons, 1969

In 1958 Frank Rosenblatt, a psychologist at Cornell, unveiled the perceptron, a machine that could learn to recognize patterns by adjusting the weights of its connections. It was a simple model of a neuron. The New York Times reported that the Navy expected it to be able to walk, talk and be conscious of its existence.

In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published a book that analyzed the limits of the simplest perceptrons. They showed that a single layer couldn't solve certain problems, such as the exclusive or. It was widely taken to mean that neural networks were a dead end, though they had said that more layers might help. Funding dried up. It was one of the periods called AI winters. The ideas came back in the 1980s, and in the 2010s led to deep learning.

# The ImageNet moment
@src Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton, NeurIPS, 2012

Every year from 2010, researchers competed in a contest called ImageNet. Their programs had to name the objects in a million photographs, drawn from a thousand categories, such as cats, cars and mushrooms. The best programs made mistakes on about a quarter of the images.

In 2012 a team from the University of Toronto, Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton, entered a deep neural network called AlexNet. It was trained on graphics processors, which were designed for video games. It cut the error rate to about fifteen percent, far better than the next entry. It was a turning point. Within a few years, nearly everyone in the field was using deep networks, and the technique spread to speech, translation and many other tasks.

# The robot cars in the desert
@src DARPA Grand Challenge, 2004 and 2005; Thrun et al., Journal of Field Robotics, 2006

In 2004 the US Defense Department's research agency offered a million dollars to the team whose driverless vehicle could finish a course of about a hundred and fifty miles across the Mojave Desert. Fifteen vehicles started. None finished. The best went about seven miles, and then got stuck.

A year later, in 2005, the contest was held again. This time, five vehicles finished the course. The winner was a modified Volkswagen named Stanley, built by a team from Stanford led by Sebastian Thrun, which finished in just under seven hours. In the year between, the teams had made huge progress in sensors and software. Many of the people involved went on to lead the self-driving car projects of the following decade.

# VisiCalc
@src Bricklin, Bricklin on Technology, 2009

In 1979 Dan Bricklin, a student at Harvard Business School, was tired of the way that financial models were done on paper. If you changed one number, you had to recalculate everything by hand. He imagined a grid on a screen, where each cell could hold a number or a formula, and where a change would flow through to all the others.

With a programmer, Bob Frankston, he created VisiCalc, the first spreadsheet program, for the Apple II computer. It sold for about a hundred dollars. Businesses bought Apple IIs just to run it, and it's often called the first killer app. It helped to turn the personal computer from a hobbyist's toy into a tool for business. Bricklin never patented it, and later said that he did not regret it.

# The Antikythera mechanism
@src Freeth et al., Nature, 2006

In 1901 sponge divers found a shipwreck near the Greek island of Antikythera. Among the treasures was a corroded lump of bronze, which turned out to contain dozens of gears. It was dated to about the second or first century BC. For decades, no one knew what it was.

X-ray scans in the 2000s revealed the details. The device had at least thirty meshing gears, and dials. Turning a handle showed the positions of the Sun and Moon, the phases of the Moon, and when eclipses were likely. It also seems to have shown the dates of the ancient Olympic Games. It is the most complex machine known from the ancient world. Nothing of similar sophistication appears again for over a thousand years, when astronomical clocks were built in medieval Europe.

# Harrison's clocks and the longitude
@src Sobel, Longitude, 1995

For centuries, sailors could find their latitude by the Sun or stars, but not their longitude. To know it, they needed to know the time at a reference point, such as their home port, and compare it with local time. A pendulum clock wouldn't work on a heaving ship. Ships were lost on rocks because of errors, and in 1707, four warships were wrecked off the Isles of Scilly, with the loss of about two thousand men.

In 1714 the British government offered a large prize for a method that worked. John Harrison, a self-taught clockmaker from Yorkshire, spent decades building a series of marine timekeepers. His fourth, H4, looked like a large pocket watch, and in 1761 it lost only a few seconds on a voyage to Jamaica. The board was slow to award the prize, and he was not fully paid until 1773. His clocks made the sea much safer.
