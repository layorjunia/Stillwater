# Hiding a feeling
@src Gross & Levenson, JPSP, 1993; Gross & John, JPSP, 2003

When we are upset, many of us try to hide it. In 1993 James Gross and Robert Levenson showed people a disturbing film while instructing some of them to keep their faces still, so that no one watching would know how they felt. The people who suppressed their expressions felt just as disgusted as those who didn't. But their bodies reacted more, with greater activity in the sympathetic nervous system.

In 2003 Gross and Oliver John looked at what happens in everyday life. People who often suppress their emotions tend to feel less satisfied with life, to have less close relationships, and to be less well liked. Suppression takes effort, and it doesn't make the feeling go away. It also keeps other people from knowing how you feel, which makes it harder for them to support you.

# Looking at it differently
@src Gross, JPSP, 1998; Ochsner et al., Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience, 2002

There's another way of handling an emotion, which is to change the way that you think about the situation. This is called reappraisal. A person facing a stressful event might tell themselves that it's a chance to learn, or that a medical scene in a film is a doctor doing her job. In 1998 James Gross tested this with a disgusting film. People who were asked to watch with the detachment of a medical professional felt less disgust, and showed a smaller bodily reaction.

In 2002 Kevin Ochsner and his colleagues scanned the brains of people doing the same task. When they reappraised a sad picture, activity rose in the prefrontal cortex and fell in the amygdala. Thinking differently about something changes the response of the emotional brain. Reappraisal is one of the skills at the heart of cognitive therapy, and the evidence suggests that, unlike suppression, it works.

# Why a sad mood remembers sad things
@src Bower, American Psychologist, 1981

When we feel down, memories of sad times come to mind, and when we feel cheerful, happy ones do. In the 1980s Gordon Bower tested this in the lab. He put people in a happy or sad mood with hypnosis, and then asked them to recall events, or to read a story about two characters, one happy and one sad.

People in a sad mood recalled more sad events from their lives, and identified with the sad character in the story. People in a happy mood did the opposite. It's called mood-congruent memory. It helps to explain why depression can feel so bleak. A low mood makes bad memories more available, and they make the mood worse. It also suggests a way out. Doing something that lifts your mood a little can make other memories easier to reach.

# Anger and the heart
@src Chida & Steptoe, Journal of the American College of Cardiology, 2009

Is a hot temper bad for the heart? In 2009 Yoichi Chida and Andrew Steptoe combined the results of dozens of studies that followed people for years. They looked at anger and hostility, and at heart disease. In people who were healthy at the start, those who were angry or hostile were more likely to develop coronary heart disease later.

Among people who already had heart disease, anger and hostility were linked to worse outcomes. The link was stronger in men than in women. The effect is modest, and it is not easy to separate from other factors, such as smoking and diet, which are more common in angry people. But the connection has been seen repeatedly. A possible reason is that repeated surges of stress hormones put a strain on the cardiovascular system.

# Stress and the common cold
@src Cohen, Tyrrell & Smith, New England Journal of Medicine, 1991

In the early 1990s Sheldon Cohen and his colleagues carried out an unusual experiment in Sheffield, England. They gave about four hundred volunteers a questionnaire about the stress in their lives. Then they dropped a cold virus into each person's nose. The volunteers stayed in quarantine for several days, and doctors checked who got ill.

The more stress a person had reported, the more likely they were to develop a cold, and the effect rose steadily with the level of stress. It was seen even after the researchers accounted for age, smoking and other factors. Stress didn't change whether people were infected, but it changed whether they got sick. The study was among the first to show, under controlled conditions, that stress can affect the immune system.

# Friends and colds
@src Cohen, Doyle, Skoner, Rabin & Gwaltney, JAMA, 1997

Do social connections protect against illness? In 1997 Sheldon Cohen and his colleagues asked volunteers about their social ties, including whether they were married, had friends and family, belonged to groups and attended religious services. Then they exposed them to a cold virus, and watched who got sick.

The people with the most varied social ties were much less likely to come down with a cold than those who had few. The difference was large. It was not explained by health habits, or by the amount of virus in the body. The researchers thought that social support might protect by helping people cope with stress. The result fits a body of work that links relationships with good health. It's one more reason to nurture friendships.

# Sleep and colds
@src Cohen, Doyle, Alper, Janicki-Deverts & Turner, Archives of Internal Medicine, 2009

If you sleep badly, do you catch more colds? In 2009 Sheldon Cohen and his colleagues followed one hundred and fifty-three healthy adults. For two weeks, they kept track of how much people slept. Then the volunteers were given drops of a cold virus in the nose, and were watched for a week.

People who had slept less than seven hours a night were nearly three times as likely to develop a cold as those who slept eight hours or more. The link remained after the researchers accounted for age, stress and other factors. Sleep seems to help the immune system to do its work. It's one more reason to protect your sleep when you've been exposed to an illness.

# Does a hug protect you?
@src Cohen, Janicki-Deverts, Turner & Doyle, Psychological Science, 2015

Hugs are a form of social support, which is known to protect against stress. In 2015 Sheldon Cohen and his colleagues asked whether hugging could help to protect against illness. Four hundred volunteers were interviewed by telephone on fourteen evenings, and asked about their conflicts and hugs. Then they were given a cold virus.

Those who had more social support were less likely to get ill. The people who had been hugged more often, among those who had had arguments with others, had less severe symptoms. The researchers suggested that hugging works as a sign of support, which in turn eases the effects of stress. It was an observational study, and it can't prove cause. But it's an easy and pleasant thing to try.

# Believing you slept well
@src Draganich & Erdal, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 2014
@conf mixed

In 2014 Christina Draganich and Kristi Erdal ran a clever experiment on what people believe about their sleep. Students slept in the lab, and in the morning they were attached to a machine, and were told that it had measured their sleep. Some were told that they had had above-average sleep, and others below average. The information was made up.

The students told that they had slept well did better on tests of attention and thinking than those told that they had slept poorly. Their beliefs about their sleep changed their performance. The effect was found only in a small study, and later results have been mixed. But it may help to explain why worry about sleep can make you perform worse. It's a reminder not to catastrophize about a bad night.

# Fears that we're ready for
@src Seligman, Behavior Therapy, 1971; Öhman & Mineka, Psychological Review, 2001

Why do so many people fear snakes and spiders, and so few fear cars, guns or electrical outlets, which are much more dangerous today? In 1971 Martin Seligman proposed that humans are prepared to learn some fears easily. These are the ones that threatened our ancestors.

In lab experiments, Arne Öhman and his colleagues paired pictures with a mild shock. People learned to fear pictures of snakes and spiders faster, and the fear was harder to extinguish, than when the pictures were of flowers or mushrooms, or even of guns. In 2001 Öhman and Susan Mineka proposed a fear module in the brain, which acts quickly and is built around the amygdala. It's a reason why some fears are easy to acquire and hard to lose.

# Learning to fear a snake by watching
@src Mineka, Davidson, Cook & Keir, Journal of Abnormal Psychology, 1984

In the 1980s Susan Mineka studied monkeys raised in a lab, which showed no fear of snakes. She had them watch a video of wild-reared monkeys that showed a strong fear when they were shown a snake. After a short time, the lab-raised monkeys also acted afraid of snakes, and the fear lasted for months.

The researchers then edited the video so that the monkeys seemed to be afraid of a flower instead. The watching monkeys did not develop a fear of flowers. So the monkeys had learned from others, but only when the object was one that they were ready to fear. The study shows how fears can spread socially. It's a reason why children can pick up a phobia from a parent, and why a calm model can help them to lose it.

# A single afternoon for a phobia
@src Öst, 1989; Zlomke & Davis, 2008

Phobias are among the most treatable of mental health problems. In the 1980s the Swedish psychologist Lars-Göran Öst developed a method that works in a single long session. A person with a spider phobia, for example, is guided in stages to face the feared thing, with a therapist who models the behavior, from looking at a spider in a jar to touching it with a hand.

In his studies, most patients were greatly improved after about three hours, and the benefits lasted for years. A review in 2008 by Kristin Zlomke and Thompson Davis confirmed that one-session treatments work well for many kinds of specific phobias, and for children and adults. The key is that the person stays with the fear until it falls, and finds that nothing bad happens. It's a fast and effective treatment.

# Why we like what hurts
@src Rozin, Guillot, Fincher, Rozin & Tsukayama, Emotion, 2013

Why do people eat hot chili peppers, take cold plunges or watch films that make them cry? In 2013 Paul Rozin and his colleagues studied what they called benign masochism. In a series of surveys, people listed activities where they enjoy something that the body sees as a threat, including very hot baths, spicy food, scary rides and sad music.

The researchers suggested that the pleasure comes from enjoying a negative feeling in a safe setting. The body reacts as if there is danger, while the mind knows that there is none. This lets us feel a thrill, and a sense of mastery over our own reactions. It's a special human trait, since animals don't seem to take pleasure in chili. It reminds us that emotions can be mixed, and that it's possible to enjoy being a little afraid.
