# Why the same words feel different as a loss
@series think
@conf mixed
@src Tversky & Kahneman, Science, 1981

In 1981 Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman put a problem to people. An unusual disease is expected to kill 600 people. In one version, the choice is between a program that will save 200 for certain, and one that has a one third chance of saving all 600 and a two thirds chance of saving none. Of 152 people who were asked, 72 percent chose the sure thing.

In the second version, the choice is the same in fact but is worded in lives lost. One program means that 400 people will die. The other has a one third chance that nobody dies and a two thirds chance that 600 die. Of 155 other people, only 22 percent chose the sure program, and most chose the gamble.

The outcomes are identical, and the only change is the wording, saved or lost. People are cautious when the choice is about gains, and take risks when it is about losses. The finding has been used to explain how the framing of a message can change a decision. Later studies show that the effect is not as general as first thought. It depends on the context, and a pandemic-era attempt to repeat it did not find the original result.

# The urn with a hidden mix
@series think
@src Ellsberg, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 1961

In 1961 a young economist, Daniel Ellsberg, described a puzzle. An urn holds 30 balls. Ten are red, and the other twenty are black or white in an unknown mix. You win a prize if you draw a ball of a color you choose. Most people would rather bet on red, where the odds are known to be one in three, than on black, where the odds could be anything.

Then the choice is changed. You can bet on red or white, or on black or white. Now the odds for black or white are known to be exactly two in three, while red or white could be anywhere. Most people choose black or white. The two choices together do not make sense. Choosing red in the first means that you think red is more likely than black, and so red or white should be more likely than black or white.

The effect is called ambiguity aversion. People will give up a better bet to avoid one whose odds they cannot see. Ellsberg later became better known for something else. In 1971 he gave the Pentagon Papers, a secret government study of the Vietnam War, to newspapers.

# The pattern that was only chance
@series think
@src Clarke, Journal of the Institute of Actuaries, 1946

During the Second World War, German flying bombs fell on London. Londoners noticed that some districts seemed to be hit again and again, and others never. Some suspected that the bombs were aimed, or that certain places were spared on purpose. Many people had their own theories about it.

After the war a statistician, R. D. Clarke, who worked for an insurance company, tested the idea. He divided an area of south London into 576 squares, each a quarter of a square kilometer, and counted the bombs in each. There were 537 bombs in all. He compared the counts to what a random scatter would give. About 229 squares had none, 211 had one, 93 had two, and a few had more.

The fit to a random pattern was close. His test gave a probability of 0.88, so there was no support for the idea that the bombs clustered. The belief in a pattern is called the clustering illusion. In small samples chance produces streaks and clumps, and we are not good at expecting them. Kahneman and Tversky explained it by the representativeness heuristic, the habit of expecting random things to look evenly spread.

# The shapes that seemed to have motives
@series brain
@src Heider & Simmel, American Journal of Psychology, 1944

In 1944 two psychologists at Smith College, Fritz Heider and Marianne Simmel, made a short film. It showed a large triangle, a small triangle, a circle, and a box with a door, all moving around a screen. Nothing in it was alive. They asked 34 people to say what they saw.

Nearly everyone told a story. The big triangle was a bully, and the small shapes were being chased and fighting. The circle was frightened and hid in the box. Only one person described the film in terms of geometry, as moving shapes. Almost all of them said that the shapes wanted things, and that they were being kind or cruel.

The study is a classic on how automatically people see intention and character. From very little, such as the speed and path of a movement, we infer goals and feelings. It helps to explain why people give names to cars and talk to computers, and why a short cartoon can move us. The urge to find minds is built into how we see.

# The bridge that made her seem attractive
@series mind
@conf mixed
@src Dutton & Aron, J. Personality and Social Psychology, 1974

In 1974 Donald Dutton and Arthur Aron did a field experiment near Vancouver, Canada. A woman interviewer approached young men who had just crossed one of two bridges over the Capilano River. One was a narrow footbridge of wooden boards on wire cables, high above a gorge, and it swayed. The other was a low, solid bridge nearby.

She asked them to write a short story for her survey, and gave each man her phone number in case he wanted to know more. The men from the high bridge wrote stories with significantly more sexual imagery. By one account, nine of eighteen men who took her number from the high bridge later called, while only two of sixteen did from the low one.

The authors' explanation was misattribution of arousal. The men's pounding hearts from fear were taken, in part, as attraction to her. The finding has been repeated in various forms, but the exact numbers differ among accounts, and the study was small. It remains a striking case of how the body's signals can be misread.

# Does a small gift of luck make us kind?
@series mind
@conf mixed
@src Isen & Levin, J. Personality and Social Psychology, 1972

In the early 1970s the psychologist Alice Isen asked whether a good mood makes people helpful. She and Paul Levin arranged for some people to find a dime in the coin return of a pay phone, and others not to. Then a stranger walked past and dropped a folder full of papers.

According to a later summary, 88 percent of those who found the dime stopped to help, while only 4 percent of those who had not found one did. It was a small study, and the figures come from a secondhand description. Isen also gave people cookies, or good news about their performance, and found similar effects on helping.

Her work helped found the study of how mood affects thinking. She also showed that people in a good mood tended to be better at solving some creative problems. Not every finding has held up in later tries. But the idea that a small piece of luck can change what we do for a stranger has been influential, and has been repeated in many forms.

# When a favor makes a friend
@src Jecker & Landy, 1969; Franklin, Autobiography

Most of us think that we do favors for people we like. Benjamin Franklin noticed that it may work the other way. In his autobiography he tells of a rival in the Pennsylvania Assembly, who had never been friendly to him. Franklin heard that the man owned a rare book, and wrote asking to borrow it for a few days.

The man sent it at once. Franklin returned it a week later with a note of thanks. The next time they met in the Assembly, the man spoke to him civilly, for the first time, and became a friend for life. Franklin quoted an old maxim: he that has once done you a kindness will be more ready to do you another than he whom you yourself have obliged.

In 1969 Jon Jecker and David Landy tested it. Students who had won money in a contest were asked to give it back, some by the researcher himself, some by a secretary, and some were not asked. Those the researcher had asked personally liked him the most, and those the secretary had asked liked him the least. The usual explanation is cognitive dissonance. Having helped, we decide that he must be worth helping.

# Why pretty tools seem easier to use
@series tech
@src Kurosu & Kashimura, 1995; Tractinsky, 1997

Suppose you are shown two designs for a machine, and one looks better than the other. Does that change how easy you think it will be to use? In 1995 Masaaki Kurosu and Kaori Kashimura, researchers at the company Hitachi in Tokyo, tested it with different layouts for the screen of a cash machine.

They found that how usable people thought a layout was had a stronger link to how beautiful it looked than to how well it actually worked. People seemed to be influenced by looks even when they were asked to judge function. Later work by Noam Tractinsky and others found similar results, with other ATM layouts and with a home control system.

The result is now called the aesthetic-usability effect. People tend to see a good-looking design as easier to use, and to forgive its faults. For designers it suggests that appearance is not decoration. But it has a risk, which is that a pretty design can hide real problems that a plain one would have shown. Careful testing must look at what people do, and not only at what they say.

# Why friends look better in a group photo
@series mind
@conf mixed
@src Walker & Vul, Psychological Science, 2014; How I Met Your Mother, 2008

In a television comedy in November 2008, a character called Barney points out a group of women who seem attractive, but who look plain when he considers them one at a time. He named it the cheerleader effect. It sounded like a joke, but psychologists took it up.

In a paper that appeared in 2014, Drew Walker and Edward Vul tested it. They found that faces were rated as about 1.5 to 2 percent more attractive when seen in a group than when seen alone. The effect did not depend on how long the faces were shown, how many people were in the group, or how they were arranged.

The explanation they offered is that the brain sums up a group, forming an average, and our judgment of each face is pulled toward that average. Averages of faces tend to be smooth and symmetrical, and attractive. The effect is small, and not all studies agree on its causes. It is a reminder that we do not judge each thing on its own, but in the setting around it.

# Why small coins go faster
@series sell
@src Raghubir & Srivastava, Journal of Consumer Research, 2009

If you have a twenty dollar bill, and you break it, the money seems to go faster. In 2009 Priya Raghubir and Joydeep Srivastava tested this idea in three studies, and called it the denomination effect.

In the first, 89 undergraduate students were thanked for taking part, and each was given either four quarters or a one dollar bill. They were told that they could keep the money or spend it on candy. About 63 percent of the students with four quarters bought candy. Only about 26 percent of those with the dollar bill did.

The authors suggested that a large note feels less exchangeable than the same value in small coins, so it is harder to part with. This also means that people who want to control their spending may like to carry large bills. The researchers noted that during the Great Recession, a businessman saw employees using coins more than banknotes at an office vending machine. It is a small effect, but it shows that the form of money changes how we spend it.

# Did hunger change the judges' decisions?
@conf contested
@src Danziger, Levav & Avnaim-Pesso, PNAS, 2011

In 2011 Shai Danziger, Jonathan Levav and Liora Avnaim-Pesso reported a study of Israeli parole boards. They looked at about a thousand rulings by judges during their day, which had two meal breaks. The share of requests granted was about 65 percent at the start of a session, but fell nearly to zero before a break. After the break it jumped back up.

The authors suggested that mental fatigue pushed the judges toward the safe choice, which was to refuse. Rest and food restored their willingness to take chances. The paper was cited some 2,500 times, and it became a favorite example of how small things can bias serious decisions.

But the findings have been questioned. A few months later a letter in the same journal argued that the order of cases is not random. Prisoners without lawyers tend to go last, for one, and lawyers may arrange the order for their clients. A later analysis suggested that part of the effect could come from scheduling, since cases that were likely to be granted took more time. The psychologist Daniël Lakens argued that the effect was far too large to be plausible. The lesson is to be careful with a striking result.

# The feeling of being a fraud
@series mind
@conf mixed
@src Clance & Imes, Psychotherapy: Theory, Research and Practice, 1978

In 1978 two psychologists at Georgia State University, Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes, wrote about an experience they had met in their work with clients. Some highly successful women felt that they were not really intelligent, and that their success was luck or a mistake that would soon be found out. The authors called it the impostor phenomenon.

Their paper drew on their therapy work with more than 150 high-achieving women, and was a clinical description and not a test. They linked it to family messages and to stereotypes about women. They took care not to call it a syndrome or a disorder. It is not a diagnosis in the standard manuals of psychiatry.

Later research found that the experience is common among men as well as women, and across ages. It has become one of the best known ideas in popular psychology, and appears in discussions of work and school. Studies suggest that people who feel like impostors are not less able. Their doubt does not follow their performance, and talking about it can help.

# Why you suddenly see a word everywhere
@series words
@src Mullen, St. Paul Pioneer Press, 1994; Zwicky, 2005

Many people have learned a new word, and then seen it three times in a week. In 1994 a reader in St. Paul, Minnesota, Terry Mullen, wrote to his newspaper, the Pioneer Press, about it. He had heard the name of the German militant group Baader-Meinhof once, and then kept coming across it.

Other readers wrote in with their own stories, and the effect became known as the Baader-Meinhof phenomenon, though it has nothing to do with the group. In 2005 a Stanford linguist, Arnold Zwicky, wrote about it on his blog and gave it a better name, the frequency illusion.

Zwicky explained it as the work of two processes. One is selective attention. Once something has caught our notice, we are on the lookout for it. The other is confirmation bias, in which we register each new sighting as proof that it is everywhere. The word was always as common. What changed was that you began to notice it, and your mind counted the hits and ignored the misses.

# Why a calm person cannot imagine being angry
@series mind
@src Loewenstein, hot-cold empathy gap research

When you are angry, it is hard to remember what it felt like to be calm. When you are calm, it is hard to understand how you could have been so angry. Psychologists call this a hot-cold empathy gap. A hot state is one driven by emotion or by bodily urges such as hunger, pain or desire. A cold state is the absence of these.

The point is that what we understand is tied to the state we are in. People in a cold state underestimate how much a hot one will drive them, and people in a hot state have trouble believing they will feel differently later. The economist George Loewenstein has been prominent in studying it. It helps to explain why people make plans they do not keep, and why they give in to temptation.

The idea has practical uses. A doctor who has never had severe pain may underestimate a patient's suffering. A person who makes a rule for a time of temptation, when calm, may find the rule easy to make and hard to follow. A sensible response is to set up the situation ahead of time, so that the hot self has fewer chances to take over.

# Why you cannot tell how long you will feel bad
@series mind
@src Gilbert, Pinel, Wilson, Blumberg & Wheatley, J. Personality and Social Psychology, 1998

In 1759 the economist and philosopher Adam Smith observed that people who chase wealth think it will bring them lasting peace, and often find that it does not. Two centuries later the idea became a field of research. The term affective forecasting, meaning the prediction of one's own future feelings, was coined by the psychologists Timothy Wilson and Daniel Gilbert.

They found that people are poor at it. People imagine that winning a lottery would make them happy for a long time, and they overlook the many other things in their lives that will go on affecting their mood. In 1998 Gilbert and his colleagues argued that people overestimate how long bad feelings will last, because they are unaware of what they called the psychological immune system, the mental processes that soften pain.

The practical result is that we often choose badly, working hard for things that give less lasting pleasure than we predicted, and dreading things that will bother us less than we fear. One way to improve a forecast is to ask someone who has been through the same experience. It is a better guide than our own imagination.

# Faces that look alike
@series brain
@src Meissner & Brigham, Psychology, Public Policy and Law, 2001

Many people say that members of another race look alike to them. The first study of the effect was published in 1914. In 2001 Christian Meissner and John Brigham reviewed thirty years of research in a meta-analysis called Thirty Years of Investigating the Own-Race Bias in Memory for Faces.

They found a mirror effect. When people were shown faces and later asked to pick them out, they were more often right about faces of their own race, and also made fewer false alarms, which are wrong claims to have seen a face. With faces of another race they did worse on both. The effect has been found in many groups, and it matters in court, where witnesses are asked to identify strangers.

There are several explanations, including a lack of experience with the faces and the way attention is directed. Contact with people of the other race helps a little, but no one theory explains all the findings. Brain scans show that parts of the brain that handle faces respond differently to faces of one's own group. The effect is not a sign of prejudice, though it can lead to serious mistakes.

# The Rorschach test and the problem of validity
@conf contested
@src Rorschach, Psychodiagnostik, 1921

In 1921 a Swiss psychiatrist, Hermann Rorschach, published a book describing a test with ten inkblots. He showed them to patients and asked what they saw. He was interested not so much in what the person saw as in how, such as whether he used the whole blot, the details, or the white spaces, and whether he noticed color or movement. He died the next year, in 1922, at the age of 37.

The test became one of the best known in the world, and its blots are a symbol of psychology in popular culture. But its scientific standing has been disputed for decades. Critics argued that psychologists using it did not agree on how to score responses, and that it did not predict behavior well. Later scoring systems were developed to address this.

Today many psychologists regard it as a weak tool for diagnosing most conditions, though some find it useful as a way of starting a conversation and of seeing how a person organizes experience. It is a good example of a test that is popular, and that people trust, without having been shown to measure what it is supposed to.
