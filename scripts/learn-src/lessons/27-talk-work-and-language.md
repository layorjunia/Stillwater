# When feedback makes things worse
@src Kluger & DeNisi, Psychological Bulletin, 1996

Feedback is supposed to help people improve, but does it? In 1996 Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi combined the results of 607 experiments with feedback, covering tens of thousands of people. On average, feedback did help. But in more than one third of the cases, it made performance worse.

The pattern depended on what the feedback focused on. When it pointed to the person, as in "you're not good at this," it often backfired, because attention moved from the task to the self. When it focused on the task, and on how to do it better, it was far more likely to help. The researchers also found that feedback worked best when it was specific and gave a clear way forward. The lesson is to talk about the work and not the worker.

# Why the best teams reported more mistakes
@src Edmondson, Administrative Science Quarterly, 1999

In the 1990s Amy Edmondson studied hospital teams to find out which ones made the fewest medication errors. She expected the best teams to report the fewest. It turned out to be the opposite: the teams that were rated best by their staff had reported more errors. At first she was baffled.

Her explanation was that they didn't make more mistakes. They were more willing to talk about them. In those teams, people felt safe to speak up, which she called psychological safety. In others, people hid mistakes for fear of blame, so they stayed unreported and uncorrected. Google's own research, called Project Aristotle, found years later that psychological safety was the most important factor in effective teams. A group learns when people can say "I made a mistake" without being punished.

# What gestures do for the speaker
@src Broaders, Cook, Mitchell & Goldin-Meadow, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2007

We gesture when we talk, even on the phone, where nobody can see. Susan Goldin-Meadow has spent decades studying why. In 2007 she and her colleagues taught children in a math lesson, and asked some of them to move their hands as they explained how to solve the problems, and others not to gesture. The children who gestured went on to learn more from the lesson.

In some cases, children's gestures showed ideas that they couldn't yet put into words, a sign that they were ready to learn. It seems that gesture is part of thinking, and not just a decoration for speech. It helps us find words and organize ideas. If you're trying to understand something, try explaining it with your hands. It may help you think.

# Hearing an accent changes what we believe
@src Lev-Ari & Keysar, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 2010

In 2010 Shiri Lev-Ari and Boaz Keysar had people listen to trivia statements, such as "A giraffe can go without water longer than a camel can," read by native English speakers and by non-native speakers with a mild accent. The listeners were told that the speakers were only reading statements that had been written for them. Even so, they rated statements spoken with an accent as less true.

The accent was easy to understand, so it wasn't a problem of comprehension. The researchers suggested that it's about processing fluency. Speech that's a bit harder to process feels less trustworthy, and we confuse the difficulty with the content. When the listeners were told about the effect beforehand, the bias shrank. It's worth knowing, since it means that we may discount good ideas because of the way they're pronounced.

# Do some voices sound less competent?
@src Anderson, Klofstad, Mayew & Venkatachalam, PLoS ONE, 2014
@conf mixed

Vocal fry is a low, creaky quality that's sometimes heard at the end of sentences, and it's often associated with young American women. In 2014 Rindy Anderson and her colleagues played recordings of the same speakers saying the same words, with and without vocal fry, and asked listeners to rate them. The listeners judged the voices with vocal fry as less competent, less trustworthy and less hirable, and the effect was larger for women than for men.

It was a study of young adults in the US, and later work has found that the effect varies. It raises the question of whether the bias is about the voice or about expectations of the speaker. Many experts, including linguists, consider it a normal feature of speech, found in all kinds of voices. It's a reminder that we sometimes judge how people sound without noticing.

# The words that show who is on top
@src Kacewicz et al., Journal of Language and Social Psychology, 2014

In his research on language, James Pennebaker noticed something odd about small words such as pronouns. In 2014 Ewa Kacewicz and her colleagues analyzed the language that people used in emails, and in tasks in which people of different status worked together. They found that people with lower status used the word "I" more. People of higher status used it less, and used more "we."

You might think that the powerful would talk about themselves more. But the pattern was consistent. Using "I" shows that attention is turned inward, a bit like being self-conscious, while higher-status people tend to look outward at others and at the task. The words are used without thought, and it suggests a different way of reading a conversation. The small words can reveal more than the big ones.

# A language invented by children
@src Senghas & Coppola, Psychological Science, 2001

Until the late 1970s, deaf children in Nicaragua had no schools, and most lived at home with their families, using improvised gestures. When schools for the deaf opened in Managua in the late 1970s, hundreds of children were brought together for the first time. They didn't have a shared language, and they began to invent one on the playground.

Over the next few years, the signs became more systematic, and each new cohort of children took them further. Ann Senghas and her colleagues, studying the language as it developed, found that younger children added grammar, such as ways of showing the direction of movement, that the first group hadn't used. Nicaraguan Sign Language is one of the few cases of a new language being born in front of scientists. It suggests that children are built to create language.

# The window for learning a language
@src Johnson & Newport, Cognitive Psychology, 1989

Is it easier to learn a language when you're young? In 1989 Jacqueline Johnson and Elissa Newport tested 46 native speakers of Chinese and Korean who'd come to the US at various ages, and who'd been living there for at least five years. They gave them a test of English grammar. People who arrived before about age 7 did as well as native speakers. For those who'd arrived later, the older they were when they arrived, the worse they did.

The decline continued steadily through the teenage years. It was one of the best-known tests of a critical period for language. Later research suggests that the picture is more complicated, since adults learn more quickly at first, and the age effects are gradual. But it's clear that, for mastering accent and grammar, there's an advantage in starting early, and still it's possible to learn at any age.

# Babies are statisticians
@src Saffran, Aslin & Newport, Science, 1996

Spoken language doesn't have gaps between words. When you listen to a language you don't know, it sounds like a continuous stream. So how do babies find the words? In 1996 Jenny Saffran, Richard Aslin and Elissa Newport played eight-month-old infants a stream of nonsense syllables for just two minutes. Certain syllables always went together, such as "bi-da-ku," and others didn't.

Afterward, the babies paid more attention to new combinations that broke the pattern than to those that kept it, which showed that they'd learned which syllables belonged together. They did it after only two minutes, with nothing to look at. It's called statistical learning, and it shows that even babies detect patterns in sounds. It's part of how an infant, in the first year, begins to carve speech into words.

# Learning a word in one try
@src Carey & Bartlett, 1978

How quickly can a child learn a word? In 1978 Susan Carey and Elsa Bartlett did a simple experiment with three-year-olds at a nursery school. They said to a child, "Bring me the chromium tray, not the blue one, the chromium one." The children had never heard the word chromium, and the tray was olive green. Many of them brought the olive-colored tray.

A week later, many of the children still remembered that chromium has something to do with the color olive, after just one exposure. It was called fast mapping, and it shows that children make a quick hypothesis about a new word from context, and keep it. Children learn about ten words a day through preschool years, and this speed is part of the way they do it.

# Pictures with spoken words
@src Mayer, Multimedia Learning, 2009

Richard Mayer has run dozens of experiments on how people learn from words and pictures, and he's drawn some practical rules. People learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone. They learn better when the words are spoken than when they're written on the same screen as the animation, because the eyes have too much to do while the ears are free. And they learn worse when a narrator reads exactly what's on the slide.

It's called the redundancy principle. If you're giving a presentation, use images and a few words, and say the rest. Don't fill your slides with text and read it aloud, since your audience will read faster than you speak, and will stop listening. A slide should support what you say, and not repeat it.

# You can ask more people than you think
@src Flynn & Lake, JPSP, 2008; Bohns, 2016

Most people dislike asking others for help, partly because they think that others will say no. In 2008 Francis Flynn and Vanessa Lake asked people to approach strangers and ask for favors, such as borrowing a cell phone or filling out a questionnaire. Before they went, they'd asked the participants to guess how many strangers they'd have to approach to get a set number of yeses. They underestimated how many people would agree, and by a lot.

The reason is that the person who is asking thinks about the risk of rejection, and the person being asked feels social pressure to say yes, since saying no is awkward. Vanessa Bohns later called it underestimating compliance. It's a good thing to remember when you hesitate to ask. People are generally more willing to help than we expect, and saying no feels harder to them than it looks to us.

# Copying the other person at the table
@src Maddux, Mullen & Galinsky, JESP, 2008

In 2008 William Maddux, Elizabeth Mullen and Adam Galinsky tested whether mimicking the other person helps in a negotiation. In one experiment, business students were asked to negotiate a sale. Some were told to subtly copy the other person's posture and gestures, about 10 to 15 seconds after the other person did, and the others were told not to. The people who mimicked were much more likely to reach a deal.

The mimickers also got more for themselves, and the other person was not aware of the mimicry. The researchers suggest that mimicry builds trust and rapport, so that the other person is more open to finding mutually good solutions. It works best when it's subtle and natural. If it's obvious, it looks mocking, so it's more a matter of staying attuned to the other person than of copying them.

# What great listeners do
@src Itzchakov, Kluger & Castro, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2017

In 2017 Guy Itzchakov, Avraham Kluger and Dotan Castro asked people to talk about a topic on which they had a strong opinion, such as an issue about which they felt passionate, while a listener either paid good attention or acted distracted. The people who were listened to well afterward had a less extreme view, and were more aware of the ambiguity in their own opinions.

Being listened to made them feel safe enough to look at their own thinking. It's the opposite of what many of us expect, that if you let people say their piece without arguing, they'll dig in. In fact, a good listener, who seems interested and doesn't interrupt, can help a person to think more clearly. The most persuasive thing you can do with someone who disagrees may be to listen.

# Why video calls tire us out
@src Bailenson, Technology, Mind, and Behavior, 2021
@conf mixed

During the pandemic, many people reported that video calls were exhausting. In 2021 Jeremy Bailenson at Stanford wrote an influential analysis suggesting four reasons. First, there is excessive close-up eye contact, since in a grid of faces everyone seems to be staring at you, and faces appear larger than in life. Second, seeing yourself constantly is tiring, like having a mirror in front of you all day.

Third, video calls reduce movement, since you have to stay in view of the camera. And fourth, they raise the mental load, as we have to work harder to send and receive the nonverbal cues that come naturally in person. Some of his claims have been tested and some haven't, but studies of users find that the experience is real. Turning off your own view, shrinking the window and taking audio-only calls can help.

# Why speaking up is hard
@src Milliken, Morrison & Hewlin, Journal of Management Studies, 2003

In 2003 Frances Milliken, Elizabeth Morrison and Patricia Hewlin interviewed 40 full-time employees in a range of organizations about whether there'd been a time when they hadn't spoken up about something important at work. Remarkably, 85 percent said they had. The top reasons were fear of being seen as negative, of damaging a relationship, and a sense that it wouldn't make a difference.

The employees had concerns that they thought would be useful, about pay, a boss's behavior or a safety issue, and still stayed silent. They called it organizational silence. It costs teams a great deal, since the information never reaches those who could act on it. A leader can help by inviting dissent, by thanking people who bring bad news, and by acting on what they hear.

# The tone of the voice in a quarrel
@src Gottman et al., Journal of Marriage and the Family, 1998
@conf mixed

John Gottman has said that he can often tell, after listening to just a few minutes of a couple talking, whether they're likely to stay together. In studies, the pattern of the first few minutes of a hard conversation did predict the later outcome, though the accuracy that has been claimed for these predictions has been criticized.

The most important signals were in the tone. Contempt, with a sneer or a sarcastic voice, was the worst. Warmth, humor and appreciation were good signs. The words may be the same, "You forgot to call," but the tone decides whether it's a complaint or an attack. It suggests that, in a close relationship, it matters how you say things as much as what you say. A kind tone can carry a hard message.

# Why a call beats a text
@src Schroeder & Epley, 2015; Kumar & Epley, 2021

The research on voice and text suggests that, for personal messages, a voice often does better than writing. In 2021 Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley asked people to reconnect with an old friend with whom they'd lost touch, some by phone or video, and some by writing. Before they started, people predicted that a call would be more awkward than a text. In fact, those who made the call felt more connected afterward, and found it no more awkward than expected.

A voice carries tone, warmth and emotion in a way that text can't. In the earlier study by Juliana Schroeder and Epley, people who heard a job pitch spoken, instead of reading the same words, judged the speaker as more thoughtful and intelligent. We often choose to write because it feels safer, and we underrate how much a voice conveys. The next time that you want to reach out to someone, consider calling.

# The first thing people notice in a story
@src Labov & Waletzky, 1967
@conf mixed

Linguists William Labov and Joshua Waletzky analyzed the structure of personal stories told in everyday conversations in 1967, and found a common pattern: an abstract that says what the story is about, an orientation of who, when and where, a complicating action, an evaluation, a resolution and a coda. The evaluation, they noted, is the part that tells us why the story is worth hearing.

Storytellers who left out the point were boring even if the events were interesting. It's a lesson for any talk or conversation. Tell people why you're telling it, and make it clear what's at stake. Claims that stories work by releasing oxytocin have been popular but are not well supported by the research. It's enough to know that a story with a clear point, and a person at its center, is how people have always passed on what matters.
