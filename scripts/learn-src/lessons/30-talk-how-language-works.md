# The rules we follow without knowing
@src Grice, Logic and Conversation, 1975

The philosopher Paul Grice noticed that conversation runs on an unspoken agreement to cooperate. In his 1975 lecture, he described four habits that speakers assume of each other. Say as much as is needed and no more. Say what you believe is true. Stay on topic. Be clear and orderly. We rarely notice these habits, but we rely on them all the time.

Because we assume them, we can get meaning from what is left out. If someone says that some of the students passed, you hear that not all of them did, since otherwise they would have said all. Grice called such unstated messages implicatures. A great deal of what people mean sits there, and many misunderstandings happen when two people assume different rules.

# Words that do things
@src Austin, How to Do Things with Words, 1962

Most sentences describe the world, but some do something. When a judge says "I sentence you," or a person says "I promise" or "I do," saying the words is the act. The philosopher J. L. Austin, in lectures that were published after his death in 1962, called these performative utterances, and he argued that they can't be called true or false. They can only succeed or fail.

For one to work, the circumstances have to be right. A promise needs someone who means to keep it, and a wedding vow needs a person who is allowed to marry. Austin's insight was that speaking is a kind of action. It's a good thing to remember when you say sorry, thank someone or make a promise. You aren't reporting a feeling, you are doing something that changes the relationship.

# Why we hint
@src Pinker, Nowak & Lee, PNAS, 2008

Why do we say "Would you like to come up and see my etchings?" instead of just saying what we want? In 2008 Steven Pinker, Martin Nowak and James Lee argued that indirect speech gives the speaker plausible deniability. If the listener is willing, both of them get what they want. If not, the speaker can say that no offer was made, and nobody has to lose face.

The authors used a driver trying to bribe a police officer as an example. "Is there some way we could take care of the ticket here?" can't easily be used as proof of bribery, though both people understand it. The same logic applies in social situations with no legal risk, and in relationships where being too direct might change what two people are to each other. Hints let us test the waters.

# Hearing your name across the room
@src Cherry, 1953; Moray, 1959

In a crowded room, you can follow one voice and screen out the others. In 1953 Colin Cherry named this the cocktail party problem and studied it by playing different messages to each ear and asking people to repeat one. People could follow the chosen message well and noticed little about the other, beyond whether it was a man's or a woman's voice.

But not everything gets filtered out. In 1959 Neville Moray found that about a third of people noticed their own name when it was played in the ear they were supposed to be ignoring. Your name, and other highly meaningful words, can break through. Attention seems to be a filter that is leaky for what matters to you most. It's why you can suddenly tune in to a conversation across the room when someone mentions you.

# Hearing lips
@src McGurk & MacDonald, Nature, 1976

In 1976 Harry McGurk and John MacDonald made a discovery by accident while working with infants. They showed adults a video of a person saying "ga" while the soundtrack played "ba." Most people heard neither. They heard "da," a sound in between, as though the brain were blending what they saw with what they heard.

The effect is strong, and it works even when you know about it. If you close your eyes, you hear "ba" again, and when you open them, it shifts back. It shows that speech perception isn't just hearing. Your brain combines sound with the movements of the speaker's lips, which is one reason why it's easier to understand someone in a noisy room when you can see their face.

# Why we sing to babies
@src Fernald, 1985; Hilton et al., 2022

Adults all over the world change their voices when they talk to babies. They go higher, slower and more musical, with an exaggerated rise and fall. In 1985 Anne Fernald found that young infants prefer to listen to this kind of speech over normal adult speech. It seems to hold their attention and to signal that the message is for them.

In 2022 Courtney Hilton and his colleagues looked at recordings from dozens of societies in different parts of the world. Speech and song directed at infants shared acoustic features that set them apart from adult-directed speech and song. And thousands of listeners from many countries could tell, most of the time, whether a recording was meant for a baby. It suggests a shared human way of soothing and engaging the very young.

# Gossip that keeps people honest
@src Feinberg, Willer & Schultz, Psychological Science, 2014

Gossip has a poor reputation, but in 2014 Matthew Feinberg, Robb Willer and Michael Schultz found that it can help a group cooperate. They had people play a game in which each player could contribute to a shared pot or keep money for themselves. When players could pass notes about each other's behavior, and could exclude those who didn't contribute, cooperation went up.

Selfish players changed their behavior when they knew that their reputations would travel. The study doesn't show that all gossip is good, since gossip can be cruel and false. But it helps to explain why people talk about others' conduct so much. In small groups with no police, a reputation is a way of enforcing fairness. Our wish to talk about who did what may have grown out of that need.

# Talking about yourself feels good
@src Tamir & Mitchell, PNAS, 2012

If it seems that people love talking about themselves, there may be a reason. In 2012 Diana Tamir and Jason Mitchell scanned people's brains while they were answering questions about their own opinions, or about someone else's. Thinking and talking about themselves lit up areas of the brain's reward system more than thinking about another person did.

In another experiment, people were offered a choice. They could answer a question about themselves, or about others, and get paid slightly more for the second option. Many took less money in order to talk about themselves. It suggests that self-disclosure is rewarding in itself. The practical lesson for conversation is a kind one. Asking someone about themselves gives them something they enjoy, and you can offer it for free.

# Most of us lie rarely
@src DePaulo et al., JPSP, 1996; Serota, Levine & Boster, 2010
@conf mixed

In 1996 Bella DePaulo and her colleagues asked people to keep a diary of their lies for a week. On average, people reported telling about one or two lies a day. Most were small, told to smooth things over, to avoid hurting someone, or to make themselves look a little better. Few were serious, and most people felt little regret.

But an average can mislead. In 2010 Kim Serota, Timothy Levine and Franklin Boster surveyed a large sample of Americans. Most people reported telling no lies in the past day, and a small group of prolific liars accounted for a large share of all the lies. Later studies in other countries found a similar skew. The picture is that most people are honest most of the time, and a few people tell a great many lies.

# Why a name is harder than a job
@src McWeeny, Young, Hay & Ellis, British Journal of Psychology, 1987

You meet someone at a party, and a week later you remember that they are a baker, but not that their name is Baker. In 1987 Kate McWeeny and her colleagues tested this. People learned faces paired with surnames and occupations. Some labels were both, like Baker, Cook and Potter. Those labels were remembered better when they were presented as an occupation than when they were presented as a name.

The researchers concluded that names are hard because they usually don't mean anything. A job is attached to a rich set of ideas, such as bread, ovens and early mornings, while a surname often has no meaning to hook onto. This is why a trick to remember names is to give them a meaning, such as a picture. It also explains why forgetting names is normal, and not a sign of a poor memory.

# Easy to read, easy to believe
@src Reber & Schwarz, Consciousness and Cognition, 1999

In 1999 Rolf Reber and Norbert Schwarz showed people statements such as "Osorno is in Chile," printed in colors on a white background. Some colors were easy to read and some were faint. People were more likely to judge a statement true when it was easy to read than when it was hard to read, though the statements were the same.

The authors proposed that people use the feeling of ease, which they called processing fluency, as a clue to truth. If something is easy to take in, it feels more familiar, and familiar feels right. The effect is modest, but it appears in many settings. It's a reason to make your own writing easy to read, and a reason to be wary when something seems right because it was presented well.

# Long words make you sound less smart
@src Oppenheimer, Applied Cognitive Psychology, 2006

Many people think that using long words makes them look intelligent. In 2006 Daniel Oppenheimer tested this by giving people texts and asking them to rate the intelligence of the writer. In one version, he took a plain essay and replaced words with longer synonyms from a thesaurus. In others, he made texts harder to read in other ways, with a fancier font or poorer printing.

The result was the opposite of what people expect. Texts that were harder to read, or that used complicated words that weren't needed, led readers to rate the author as less intelligent. The reason may be the same as for the last lesson. Hard writing feels like effort, and readers blame the writer. The title of his paper has a joke in it, and it's good advice. Use the simplest word that says what you mean.

# A virus or a beast?
@src Thibodeau & Boroditsky, PLOS ONE, 2011
@conf mixed

In 2011 Paul Thibodeau and Lera Boroditsky gave people a short report on rising crime in a city. In one version, crime was described as a virus infecting the city, and in the other as a beast preying on it. The facts were the same. Then they asked what the city should do about it.

People who read about a virus were more likely to suggest reforms such as fixing the causes, and people who read about a beast were more likely to suggest punishment and enforcement. Few said that the metaphor had influenced them. Some later studies found smaller or less consistent effects, so the size of the effect is uncertain. But the idea matters. The pictures we use to describe a problem can quietly shape which solutions seem natural.

# Describing a face can make you forget it
@src Schooler & Engstler-Schooler, Cognitive Psychology, 1990
@conf mixed

In 1990 Jonathan Schooler and Tonya Engstler-Schooler had people watch a short film of a bank robbery. Some of them then wrote a description of the robber's face, and others did something unrelated. Later, everyone tried to pick the robber out of a lineup. The ones who had described the face were less accurate.

The researchers called this verbal overshadowing. Putting a visual memory into words seems to replace some of it with a description, and the description is a poorer guide than the picture. A large multi-lab replication in 2014 found the effect again, though it was smaller than the original. It suggests that for things like faces, tastes and wines, you may recognize things better than you can describe them, and trying to describe them may get in the way.

# Confident talkers get followed
@src Anderson & Kilduff, JPSP, 2009

In 2009 Cameron Anderson and Gavin Kilduff studied groups of students who had never met before, and had them solve problems together. They measured who in each group was dominant, meaning who liked to take charge, and then asked group members, observers and the researchers to rate everyone's competence.

The dominant people were rated as more competent, even after the researchers accounted for how good they actually were at the task. The way they behaved gave the impression, because they spoke up first, spoke more often, and sounded sure. Being right wasn't what earned the status. The finding is a warning in groups. A confident voice is not the same as a correct one, so it's worth asking for the reasoning.

# A period that sounds cold
@src Gunraj et al., Computers in Human Behavior, 2016
@conf mixed

Written conversation has lost the tone of voice, so punctuation takes on a job. In 2016 Danielle Gunraj and her colleagues showed people text messages that either ended with a period or didn't. For example, a friend replies "Sure" or "Sure." when invited to dinner. The messages that ended with a period were rated as less sincere than those that didn't.

The same effect did not show up on handwritten notes, so it seems to be a feature of texting, where people usually drop the period. Norms like this keep changing, and they differ with age and from group to group, so the effect may be different where you are. But the lesson is useful. In text, small signals carry more meaning than you may intend, and a short reply can read as cold.

# Strangers can feel emotion in a touch
@src Hertenstein et al., Emotion, 2006

In 2006 Matthew Hertenstein and his colleagues ran an experiment in which a person was asked to communicate an emotion to a stranger using touch alone. They sat on opposite sides of a screen, so neither could see or speak to the other. The sender could touch the receiver's forearm in any way they liked.

The receivers, who were guessing from a list of emotions, were able to identify several of them at better than chance levels. They included anger, fear, disgust, love, gratitude and sympathy. Touch can carry a message without any words. It's one reason why a hand on the shoulder can comfort someone in a way that a sentence can't, and why being touched in the wrong way can feel so wrong.

# Listening lowers people's guard
@src Itzchakov, Kluger & Castro, PSPB, 2017
@conf mixed

In a series of lab experiments, Guy Itzchakov and his colleagues asked people to talk about their views on a topic to a listener who behaved in different ways. A high-quality listener paid close attention, didn't interrupt, and showed that they understood. A poor listener seemed distracted and impatient.

Speakers who were well listened to felt less anxious, thought more carefully about their own views, and became more aware of the contradictions in them. Later studies by the same group suggested that good listening can reduce how extreme a person's attitudes are during a disagreement. Most of this evidence comes from one research group and from lab settings, so it needs more testing. But it suggests that listening is more than a courtesy. It can change the way the other person thinks.

# The story that changed in the retelling
@src Bartlett, Remembering, 1932
@conf mixed

In the 1920s Frederic Bartlett gave students at Cambridge a Native American folktale called The War of the Ghosts, and later asked them to retell it. Then he asked them to tell it again, and again, over weeks and months. The stories grew shorter, and details that didn't fit English expectations dropped out or changed.

Bartlett argued that memory isn't a recording. It's a reconstruction in which we fit what we heard to what we already know. Later researchers who tried to repeat his work found that his results were less dramatic than he reported, and that people sometimes do very well on retelling when they are asked to be accurate. Even so, the point stands that stories change as they pass from person to person.

# The thirty million word gap
@src Hart & Risley, 1995; Sperry, Sperry & Miller, Child Development, 2019
@conf contested

In 1995 Betty Hart and Todd Risley published a study that followed forty-two American families and recorded how much parents talked to their young children. They found that children in professional families heard far more words than those in families receiving welfare. Extended over the early years, the gap was said to be thirty million words.

The idea changed policy and led to campaigns encouraging parents to talk more to their children. But the original sample was small and not typical. In 2019 Douglas Sperry, Linda Sperry and Peggy Miller studied families in several communities and found much more variety, and did not find the large differences. Talking with a child is surely good for them, though recent work suggests that the back and forth of real conversation matters more than a count of words.

# A phone on the table
@src Przybylski & Weinstein, Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, 2013
@conf mixed

In 2013 Andrew Przybylski and Netta Weinstein asked pairs of strangers to have a ten minute conversation. In some pairs, a mobile phone sat on the table, and in others a notebook did. The pairs with the phone nearby later reported less closeness and trust, and less sense that the other person understood them, especially when they were talking about something meaningful.

The idea is that a phone is a reminder of everything else that could be happening, so people hold back. Later studies have had mixed results, and some did not find the same effect. The study is a reminder that attention is a gift in conversation, and that a phone in view may affect it. If someone is telling you something that matters, it costs little to put it away.

# Why speeches go in threes
@src Atkinson, 1984; Heritage & Greatbatch, American Journal of Sociology, 1986

Why do so many speeches use lists of three? "Government of the people, by the people, for the people." "Education, education, education." In the 1980s the sociologist Max Atkinson, and then John Heritage and David Greatbatch, studied recordings of political speeches and the moments when audiences applauded. They wanted to know what triggered it.

They found that a small number of rhetorical formats were behind a large share of the applause. Two stood out. One was the three-part list, and the other was a contrast, such as "not this, but that." These patterns let the audience know in advance when the point is about to be completed, so they could start clapping together. A pattern helps listeners follow a speech, and makes it easy for them to join in.

# Lady Mondegreen
@src Wright, Harper's Magazine, 1954

As a child, the writer Sylvia Wright loved a ballad that her mother read aloud. She heard the line "They hae slain the Earl o' Moray, and laid him on the green" as "and Lady Mondegreen." She imagined the lady dying beside him. In 1954 she wrote about it in Harper's Magazine, and gave the name mondegreen to the mishearing of words that creates a new meaning.

The classic examples are lyrics, such as hearing "excuse me while I kiss this guy." They happen because we don't hear speech word by word. The sounds run together, and the brain fills in gaps using what it expects. When the expectation is wrong, we hear something else, and it can feel completely real. It's a small demonstration that what we hear is partly built by the mind.

# The horse that fell
@src Bever, 1970

Read this sentence and see what happens: "The horse raced past the barn fell." Most people stumble at the last word and feel that it is a mistake. But it is a correct English sentence. It means that the horse that was raced past the barn is the one that fell. Thomas Bever described such sentences in 1970 and called them garden path sentences.

They are called garden path sentences because they lead you down one path and then leave you stranded. When we read, we don't wait for the end of a sentence to decide what it means. We guess as we go, and commit to the most likely reading. When the guess is wrong, we have to go back. It shows how quickly we build meaning, and how often that quick guess is the right one.

# Colorless green ideas
@src Chomsky, Syntactic Structures, 1957

In 1957 the linguist Noam Chomsky offered a sentence that is nonsense but still sounds like English: "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously." He compared it with "Furiously sleep ideas green colorless," which sounds wrong. His point was that grammar and meaning are separate. We can feel that one string is a sentence even when it makes no sense.

The sentence was meant to show that a language isn't just a matter of what is likely to be said. We seem to hold a system of rules in our heads that tells us what is well formed, and that system works on its own. Later researchers have argued about how much of language is rules and how much is experience. But the example is still a good way to feel that you know a structure that no one ever taught you.

# Bouba and kiki
@src Köhler, 1929; Ramachandran & Hubbard, 2001; Bremner et al., Cognition, 2013

Look at two shapes. One is rounded and soft, and the other is jagged and spiky. Which is called bouba, and which is kiki? Nearly everyone says that bouba is the round one and kiki is the spiky one. The psychologist Wolfgang Köhler found this pattern in the 1920s with other made-up words, and Vilayanur Ramachandran and Edward Hubbard tested it again in 2001.

It isn't just a matter of English speakers. In 2013 Gavin Bremner and his colleagues tested the Himba, a people in Namibia who have no written language, and they matched the words and shapes in the same way. The sounds of a word seem to have a link to its shape. Most words are arbitrary, but this shows a small, shared pull in how people connect sounds and meanings.
