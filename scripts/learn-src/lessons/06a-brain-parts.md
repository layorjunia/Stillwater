# How many neurons?
@src Azevedo et al., 2009

For a long time, textbooks said that the human brain has 100 billion neurons. It was a round number that nobody had really counted. In 2009 the Brazilian scientist Suzana Herculano-Houzel and her colleagues found a different way to do it. They dissolved brain tissue into a uniform soup of cell nuclei and counted samples of it under the microscope.

They arrived at about 86 billion neurons, and roughly the same number of other cells. It's a smaller number than the legend, and it's still more than enough to be astonishing. If each neuron were a grain of sand, you'd have enough to fill several bathtubs. What matters more than the count is the wiring. Each neuron can connect to thousands of others, and it's the connections that make a mind.

# The synapse
@src Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science

Neurons don't touch. At the junction between two of them, called a synapse, there's a tiny gap. When a signal arrives, the first cell releases chemical messengers that cross the gap and tell the next cell whether to fire.

A single neuron can receive thousands of these connections. Learning works partly by strengthening some synapses and letting others fade, so what you do with your brain changes its physical wiring. That's how a skill you practice turns into something your body just knows. It also means that the brain is never finished. Each time you repeat something, you are making a few connections a little stronger, and each time you leave something alone, you are letting some of them go quiet.

# The action potential
@src Hodgkin & Huxley, 1952

A neuron's signal is a spike of electricity that either happens fully or doesn't happen at all. In 1952 Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley worked out how the spike travels along a nerve fiber. They used the huge nerve fiber of a squid, which was big enough to put electrodes inside.

A stronger stimulus doesn't make a bigger spike. It makes more spikes per second. So the brain's code for "louder" or "brighter" is "faster," like a Geiger counter clicking more rapidly when it gets closer to something radioactive. The two men later shared a Nobel Prize for the work. It's a nice example of how a basic question, answered with the right animal, can explain something about every nervous system, including your own.

# Myelin
@src Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science

Many of your nerve fibers are wrapped in a fatty coating called myelin, in the way that electrical wire has insulation. It makes signals travel dramatically faster, over 100 meters per second in the fastest fibers, compared with about a meter per second in bare ones.

The coating is added in gaps, with small bare spots between, and the signal jumps from one spot to the next. In multiple sclerosis, the immune system attacks the myelin, and messages slow down or stop getting through. It's why the disease can cause weakness, numbness and vision trouble. Myelination continues for years in childhood and adolescence, which is part of why young children's reactions are slower than adults'.

# Glial cells
@src Azevedo et al., 2009

Neurons get the attention, but the brain has about as many other cells, called glia, and they do vital work. Astrocytes feed neurons and tidy up their chemical surroundings. Oligodendrocytes make myelin. Microglia act as the brain's cleaners and defenders.

"Glia" comes from the Greek word for glue, since early anatomists thought that was all they were, a kind of packing material. It's now known that they help shape how circuits work, and they're a very active area of research. Einstein's brain, famously, was found to have more glia per neuron in one region than average, though whether that means anything is unclear. The lesson is that the brain is a team of different cells, and the neurons are only some of the players.

# Brain energy
@src Raichle & Gusnard, 2002

Your brain is only about 2 percent of your body's weight, but it uses roughly 20 percent of its energy, whether you're thinking hard or lying on the couch. Much of that goes to keeping neurons ready to fire, like an engine idling.

Hard concentration adds only a small amount to the constant bill. So "thinking burns lots of calories" is mostly a myth, since your brain is expensive just for being on. A chess match won't do much for your waistline, though the long sitting and the snacks might not help. It also means that a tired brain isn't necessarily one that has run out of fuel. Fatigue has more to do with how the brain's chemistry and state shift than with an empty tank.

# Glutamate and GABA
@src Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science

Most of the brain's signaling comes down to a push and a pull. Glutamate is the main excitatory messenger, telling neurons to fire. GABA is the main inhibitory one, telling them to calm down.

The balance between them matters a great deal. Too much push, and the brain can tip into seizures. Too little, and thinking and movement slow. A lot of medicines work by shifting that balance. Alcohol and many anti-anxiety drugs, for instance, partly work by boosting the effect of GABA, which is why they feel calming. It also explains why mixing such drugs can be dangerous, since two things that turn down the brain can turn it down too far.

# Serotonin
@src Moncrieff et al., 2022
@conf mixed

Serotonin is often called the mood chemical, but most of it, about 90 percent, is actually in your gut, where it helps regulate digestion. It doesn't cross from the gut into the brain, which makes its own supply.

For decades people said that depression comes from low serotonin. A big review in 2022 found no consistent evidence for that simple idea. That doesn't mean serotonin is irrelevant, and many people are helped by medicines that act on it. It means that the story is much more complicated than a single chemical running low, and no one is sure exactly how those medicines work. Mood is the product of many systems, and neat one-chemical explanations are usually too good to be true.

# Dopamine and movement
@src Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science

Dopamine has a reputation as the pleasure chemical, but one of its most important jobs is helping you move. In Parkinson's disease, the cells that make dopamine in a deep part of the brain gradually die. People develop tremors and slowness, and find it hard to start movements.

A drug called L-dopa, which the brain turns into dopamine, can restore movement for a time, and it was one of the first great successes of brain chemistry. The story shows that dopamine isn't simply about feeling good. It helps you get going, whether that's reaching for a cup or reaching for a goal. When it's low, even simple actions can feel like pushing through mud.

# Acetylcholine
@src Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science

Acetylcholine is the messenger a nerve uses to tell a muscle to contract, so every step you take depends on it. It also plays a role in attention and memory in the brain, where it helps you focus on what matters.

Some medicines for Alzheimer's disease try to keep its levels up. And botulinum toxin, the active ingredient in Botox, works by blocking its release, which is why it relaxes muscles and smooths wrinkles. In larger doses, the same toxin causes a dangerous paralysis, because the muscles that breathe stop getting the message. The chemical is both ordinary and powerful, a reminder that the same messenger is used across the body for very different jobs.

# Endorphins
@src Hughes et al., 1975

In the early 1970s scientists found that the brain has receptors that fit opium, which seemed odd. Why would we have receptors for a plant drug? The answer came in 1975, when John Hughes and Hans Kosterlitz isolated the body's own opioid-like molecules, which they named enkephalins.

Endorphins are the broader family. They're released during hard exercise, laughter and pain, and they may be part of why a good run can feel wonderful. The poppy drug works because it happens to resemble something we make ourselves. It's a good example of how a puzzle can lead to a discovery. The question was not "what does the poppy do?" but "what is this receptor for?"

# Oxytocin
@src Nave, Camerer & McCullough, 2015
@conf mixed

The "love hormone" or "cuddle hormone" has had a lot of press. Oxytocin does help with childbirth and breastfeeding, and it plays a role in bonding. Those parts are well established.

But the claims about nasal sprays making people more trusting have been hard to repeat, and some studies found that it can also increase favoritism toward your own group. Its effects depend heavily on the situation and the person, so the nickname oversimplifies. It's best seen as one of many signals that tune how we react to others, and not a switch for love. A hug may feel good, but the chemistry behind the good feeling is more tangled than the headlines suggest.

# Cortisol
@src Clow et al., 2010

Cortisol is the body's main stress hormone, but it's not just for emergencies. It follows a daily rhythm, peaking about 30 minutes after you wake up, which helps you get going, and dropping to its lowest at night.

Short bursts of it are helpful, since they mobilize energy and sharpen attention. When levels stay high for a long time, it can harm memory, immune function and sleep. Both the spike and the recovery matter. A healthy stress system rises to meet a challenge and then settles, like a wave. If you find yourself constantly keyed up, it's worth thinking about what would give your body a chance to come back down, such as a regular bedtime or a break in the afternoon.

# Adrenaline
@src Cannon, 1929

When you sense danger, your adrenal glands, which sit on top of your kidneys, release adrenaline within seconds. It makes your heart pound, widens your airways, sends blood to your muscles and sharpens your senses, preparing you to fight or flee.

In the United States, it's often called epinephrine, which is the same chemical, and it's the active ingredient in an auto-injector for severe allergic reactions. That's no accident. Adrenaline opens the airways and raises blood pressure, which is exactly what someone in anaphylactic shock needs. The pounding heart you feel before a speech is the same chemistry. It isn't a sign that something is wrong. It's your body getting ready to do something hard.

# The prefrontal cortex
@src Gogtay et al., 2004

The front part of your brain, behind your forehead, is where planning, impulse control and weighing of consequences largely happen. It's among the last areas to mature, continuing to develop into your mid-twenties, which helps explain teenage risk-taking.

Damage to it can leave a person's knowledge and intelligence intact, but make it very hard to plan, to hold back impulses and to carry things through. It's also very sensitive to fatigue and stress. When you're exhausted or upset, the prefrontal cortex is the first to falter, which is why you make poorer decisions late at night or in the middle of an argument. Good judgment depends partly on a rested, calm brain.

# The amygdala
@src Adolphs et al., 1994

The amygdala is a pair of almond-shaped clusters deep in the brain that tag things as important, especially possible threats. A woman with damage to hers, known in the research as S.M., felt almost no fear and had trouble recognizing fear on faces.

But the amygdala isn't only about fear. It also responds to what's new, surprising or emotionally charged, whether it's good or bad. It helps decide what's worth remembering, which is part of why emotional events are recalled so well. The common picture of it as the "fear center" is a simplification. A better description is that it's a detector of what matters, and it tells the rest of the brain to pay attention.

# The hippocampus
@src Maguire et al., 2000

The hippocampus, named for its seahorse shape, is crucial for forming new memories and for finding your way around. In 2000 Eleanor Maguire scanned London taxi drivers, who have to memorize thousands of streets, and found that the rear part of their hippocampus was larger than in other people.

The more years they had driven, the bigger it was. It suggests that the adult brain can change with demanding use. It's also one of the first regions affected in Alzheimer's disease, which is why trouble forming new memories and getting lost are among the early signs. It's a part of the brain that rewards exercise, and learning your way around a new city is good for it.

# The cerebellum
@src Azevedo et al., 2009

The cerebellum sits at the back of the brain and its name means "little brain." It's small, about 10 percent of the brain's volume, but it contains about 80 percent of its neurons. It smooths and times your movements, keeps your balance and helps you learn skills like riding a bike.

Researchers have also found that it takes part in language and thinking, so it does more than coordinate muscles. It seems to help the brain refine any process by comparing what was expected with what happened. People with cerebellar damage may not be paralyzed, but their movements become clumsy and shaky, like a person who has had too much to drink. It's a good example of a part that looks modest and turns out to do a great deal.

# The thalamus
@src Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science

Think of the thalamus as a switchboard in the middle of your brain. Almost all of the signals from your senses, except smell, stop there on their way to the outer cortex. It helps decide what gets through.

It also plays a part in regulating attention and sleep. When you fall asleep, the thalamus reduces the flow of information to the cortex, which is partly why you stop noticing the sounds around you. When it's damaged, people can have trouble staying alert or may lose awareness altogether. It's a small structure with a huge job, and it explains why a sense can seem to "turn off" when you are concentrating or drowsy.

# The hypothalamus
@src Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science

The hypothalamus is only about the size of an almond, but it keeps your body in balance. It monitors and adjusts body temperature, thirst, hunger, sleep cycles and the release of many hormones.

It's also the link between the brain and the hormone system, directing the pituitary gland. When you shiver, feel hungry or get sleepy in the evening, the hypothalamus is involved. A tiny cluster of cells in it acts as the master body clock, and it takes its cues from light. For something so small, it holds a great deal of influence over how you feel, and why you feel hungry or tired at certain times.

# The basal ganglia
@src Graybiel, 2008

Deep in the brain, the basal ganglia help you choose which action to take and when. They're involved in starting and stopping movements, and in turning repeated behavior into habit.

Ann Graybiel's work showed that as a routine becomes automatic, these structures change their activity. They begin to treat the whole sequence as a single chunk that starts and ends with a signal. They're affected in Parkinson's disease, where movement is hard to start, and in Huntington's disease, which causes unwanted movements. When you drive home without remembering the trip, you're mostly relying on these structures, which handle the routine so the rest of the brain is free.

# The corpus callosum
@src Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science

The corpus callosum is a thick bundle of about 200 million nerve fibers that connects the left and right halves of the brain and lets them share information. Without it, the two sides would have to work separately.

In the mid-twentieth century, doctors began cutting it in some people with severe epilepsy, to stop seizures from spreading across the brain. The operation often worked. These patients became the subjects of the famous split-brain studies, which taught us a lot about how the two sides work. Outside the lab, most of them led quite normal lives, because the two halves find ways to share what they know through other routes, such as hearing what the other side says.

# The insula
@src Craig, 2002

The insula is a fold of cortex tucked deep inside the brain, and it listens to your body. It's active when you notice your heartbeat, feel nauseated, sense pain or experience disgust.

Many scientists think it helps turn raw signals from your body into emotions. A gut feeling, in a literal sense, may involve your insula. People who are better at sensing their own heartbeat tend to have stronger emotional reactions, and the insula is part of the explanation. It's a reminder that feelings have a physical side, and that paying attention to what your body says can be a way of understanding what you feel.

# The anterior cingulate
@src Botvinick et al., 2004

This region sits in the middle of the brain, curving around the front of the corpus callosum. It's a kind of conflict monitor. It becomes active when two responses compete, as when you have to name the ink color of the word "red" printed in blue.

It also lights up when you realize you've made a mistake, and when you feel social pain, such as being excluded. It seems to signal that something needs attention. That would explain why being left out can feel so much like physical hurt, since some of the same alarm is involved. The little jolt you feel when you catch an error before anyone else does is probably this region at work.

# The brainstem
@src Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science

The brainstem connects the brain to the spinal cord, and it runs the basics: breathing, heartbeat, blood pressure, and the cycle of sleeping and waking. You don't have to think about any of it, and you couldn't stop it if you tried.

That's also why damage to the brainstem is so often fatal. A small injury here can be more serious than a much larger injury in the outer parts of the brain. It's the oldest part of the brain in evolutionary terms, and it's shared by all vertebrates. The thoughtful parts of the brain get most of the credit, but they depend completely on this small stalk below them.

# The motor homunculus
@src Penfield & Boldrey, 1937

In the 1930s the neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield, operating on patients who were awake, stimulated the surface of the brain with a small electrode and noted which part of the body moved. He mapped a strip of cortex where each body part has its own patch.

When drawn as a figure, it looks like a distorted little person with huge hands, lips and tongue, because those parts need the most precise control. The legs and trunk, by contrast, take up very little room. The map shows that the brain doesn't give space according to size, but according to need. A fingertip that can feel and move with great delicacy gets far more cortex than the whole of your back.

# Broca's area
@src Broca, 1861

In 1861 the French doctor Paul Broca treated a man nicknamed "Tan," because that was nearly the only word he could say, although he could understand speech. After the man died, Broca examined his brain and found damage in a spot in the left frontal lobe.

People with damage there still understand language but speak slowly and with effort. It was one of the first clear links between a brain region and a mental function, and it began the modern effort to map the mind onto the brain. We now know that language involves wider networks than a single spot. But Broca's case remains a landmark, because it showed that something as human as speech has a physical home.

# Wernicke's area
@src Wernicke, 1874

In 1874 the German doctor Carl Wernicke described patients who had a different problem from Broca's. They could speak fluently, but what they said made little sense, and they had trouble understanding others. Their damage was in a part of the left temporal lobe.

The two language areas are linked by a bundle of fibers, and the pair showed that language depends on a network, not on one spot. One area helps you make speech, and the other helps you understand it. When you hear a sentence and say a reply, both are working together. The contrast between the two kinds of damage helped scientists see that speaking and understanding are separate abilities, even though they feel like one.

# Split-brain patients
@src Sperry, 1981; Gazzaniga, 2005

When the connection between the two hemispheres is cut, strange things happen. Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga tested these patients in the 1960s. If a word was flashed to the right hemisphere, which can't speak, the patient said they'd seen nothing, yet their left hand could pick out the matching object.

Each half seemed to have its own knowledge, and the talking half had no idea what the other half knew. Gazzaniga found that the speaking half would readily invent an explanation for something the other half had done, without noticing that it was guessing. Sperry won a Nobel Prize in 1981 for the work. It raises a thought-provoking idea. Part of what we call our reasons may be stories that our mind tells afterward.

# Phineas Gage
@src Macmillan, 2000
@conf mixed

In 1848 a railroad worker named Phineas Gage was packing explosive powder when it went off and drove an iron rod through the front of his skull. He survived, and a doctor reported that he changed from a responsible man into an impulsive, profane one.

Later historians found that the story had been exaggerated over time. Gage recovered much of his ability, and spent years working as a stagecoach driver in Chile, which takes planning and self-control, so the full tale is more complicated than the textbooks suggested. His case still showed that damage to the front of the brain can change personality. But it's a good example of how a dramatic story can grow, and it's worth checking before repeating.

# Adult neurogenesis
@src Sorrells et al., 2018; Boldrini et al., 2018
@conf contested

In some animals, the hippocampus keeps making new neurons through adulthood. Whether people do is hotly debated. It would be a hopeful finding, since it would mean that the adult brain can replace some of what it loses.

In 2018 one team reported that new neurons in the human hippocampus stop forming in childhood, and another team, in the same year, found evidence that they continue into old age. The different results may come from how brain samples were handled and how the cells were identified. So the answer, for now, is that nobody is sure. Be wary of claims that a food or a habit "grows new brain cells," since the basic fact is not settled.

# Synaptic pruning
@src Huttenlocher, 1979

A baby's brain has a burst of connection-building, and the number of synapses peaks in early childhood. Then, through adolescence and into early adulthood, the brain trims many of them, keeping the ones that are used.

It may sound like a loss, but it makes the circuits more efficient. Pruning is a bit like trimming a hedge. What you're left with is shaped by experience, which is a good reason to give children varied and rich surroundings. It also helps explain why a child picks up a language so easily and an adult finds it harder. The young brain is open to everything, and as it matures, it commits to what it has found useful.

# Sleep stages
@src Carskadon & Dement, 2011

A night of sleep isn't one steady state. Over about 90 minutes you go from light sleep into deep sleep and then into REM sleep, and then the cycle repeats four or five times in the night.

Deep sleep is concentrated in the first half of the night, and REM sleep gets longer toward morning. That's why cutting your sleep short on the end loses most of your REM, and it may be one reason you feel foggy. It also explains why going to bed very late and sleeping in is not the same as going to bed earlier. The stages come in a particular order, and each has its own work to do for your body and mind.

# REM sleep
@src Aserinsky & Kleitman, 1953

In 1953 Eugene Aserinsky, a graduate student, and his professor Nathaniel Kleitman noticed that sleepers' eyes darted back and forth under their lids at certain times in the night. When they woke people during that phase, they often reported vivid dreams.

It's called rapid eye movement, or REM, sleep. In REM, your body's muscles are mostly paralyzed, which keeps you from acting out your dreams. Sometimes the paralysis lingers for a moment when you wake, which can be frightening but is harmless. In a rare disorder, the paralysis fails, and people do act out their dreams. The sleeping brain is far from idle. In REM it's nearly as active as it is awake.

# The glymphatic system
@src Xie et al., 2013
@conf mixed

In 2013 Maiken Nedergaard's lab at the University of Rochester reported that, in mice, the spaces between brain cells expanded by about 60 percent during sleep. That let cerebrospinal fluid wash through and carry away waste, including proteins linked to Alzheimer's disease.

It suggested that one function of sleep is cleaning house. The evidence in people is still developing, and scientists are debating the details, including how the system works and how much it's affected by sleep. It's an attractive idea, and it fits what we know about the harm of poor sleep. But it's best held as a promising explanation and not a finished fact. For now, it's one more reason to take sleep seriously.

# How much sleep?
@src Watson et al., 2015

In 2015 the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society issued a consensus statement, based on a panel of experts reviewing the research. It said that adults should regularly sleep at least seven hours a night for good health.

Needs vary a little from person to person, and a few people truly do well on less. But for most of us, the evidence is that short sleep over time is linked with problems such as high blood pressure, weight gain and low mood. If you rely on caffeine to get through the day, you're probably not getting enough. It's one of the simplest and most effective things you can do for your health, and it costs nothing.

# Sleep loss feels like being drunk
@src Williamson & Feyer, 2000; Dawson & Reid, 1997

In studies from the late 1990s, researchers compared the effects of staying awake with the effects of alcohol. After 17 to 19 hours without sleep, people's performance on tests of reaction time and coordination was as poor as at a blood alcohol level of 0.05 percent.

After 24 hours it was like 0.10 percent, which is over the legal driving limit in the United States. The troubling part is that tired people often don't feel as impaired as they are, just as drunk people often don't. Drowsy driving is a real danger, and it causes many crashes every year. If you're very tired, the safest choice is to rest before you drive, and a coffee doesn't fix it.
