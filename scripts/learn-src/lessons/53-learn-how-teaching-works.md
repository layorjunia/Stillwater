# The two sigma problem
@src Bloom, Educational Researcher, 1984; VanLehn, Educational Psychologist, 2011
@conf mixed

In 1984 the educational psychologist Benjamin Bloom reported results from studies by his graduate students. Students who learned a subject with one-to-one tutoring, and with mastery methods, did far better than students in a normal class. The average tutored student scored about two standard deviations higher, which means better than ninety-eight percent of the students in the class. Bloom called the challenge of finding a way to give every student this benefit the two sigma problem.

Later reviews have found a smaller effect. In 2011 Kurt VanLehn looked at many studies of human tutoring and of computer tutors, and put the effect closer to three quarters of a standard deviation. That is still very large by the standards of education. Good tutors seem to give students quick feedback, ask them questions, and keep them working at the right level. It's a reason why a patient study partner or a good tutor is hard to beat.

# Is discovery learning best?
@src Alfieri, Brooks, Aldrich & Tenenbaum, Journal of Educational Psychology, 2011

For decades, teachers have argued about how much to tell students and how much to let them find out for themselves. One camp says that students learn best by discovering ideas through exploration. The other says that beginners need direct explanation, since the mind has a limited working memory. In 2011 Louis Alfieri and his colleagues combined the results of more than a hundred studies to see which was right.

The answer was neither. Unassisted discovery, where students are left to find things on their own, was generally worse than explicit teaching. But discovery with guidance did better. Students learned most when they were asked to explore, and also given feedback, examples or hints along the way. The best lessons mix the two. They let the learner struggle with a problem, and step in with help at the right time.

# Feedback that says where to go next
@src Hattie & Timperley, Review of Educational Research, 2007

Feedback is one of the strongest influences on learning, but it can also do nothing, or do harm. In 2007 John Hattie and Helen Timperley reviewed hundreds of studies of feedback. They found that its effects varied widely. The difference was in what the feedback said.

The most useful feedback answers three questions. Where am I going? How am I going? Where to next? Feedback that tells a student about the task and how to improve it was strong. Praise that was about the person, such as "you're so smart," added little. Feedback that comes too late is less useful, and so is feedback that is only a grade. The review suggests that when giving feedback, or asking for it, the next step is the most important part.

# What really works in schools?
@src Hattie, Visible Learning, 2009; Bergeron & Rivard, 2017
@conf contested

In 2009 John Hattie published a book that gathered over eight hundred reviews of research on education. He ranked influences on student achievement by their effect size, such as feedback, class size and homework. He proposed that an effect size of about 0.4 is a hinge point, and that anything above it is worth doing. The book became very influential with teachers and school leaders.

It has also been criticized. Statisticians pointed out that effect sizes from different kinds of studies can't be simply averaged and ranked, since they come from different designs and measures. Some of the figures contained errors, and a single number can hide large differences. The list is a useful source of ideas. But it's not a ranking that you should follow like a recipe.

# Quizzing in a real classroom
@src Roediger, Agarwal, McDaniel & McDermott, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 2011

Most of the research on practice testing was done in labs. In 2011 Henry Roediger and his colleagues tested it in a real middle school in Illinois. Students in sixth grade took short quizzes on parts of their social studies lessons. Other parts of the material were not quizzed, but were covered in the same way. All of it was part of the course.

On the unit tests, students did better on the material that had been quizzed, by about a letter grade. The advantage was still there on the final exam. The quizzes were low-stakes. They counted for little or nothing, and students had the chance to see the right answers afterward. The result shows that the benefits of retrieval practice carry over into everyday classrooms.

# Does SQ3R work?
@src Robinson, Effective Study, 1946; Dunlosky et al., 2013
@conf mixed

Many of us were taught the SQ3R method for reading. The letters stand for survey, question, read, recite and review. The method was devised by the psychologist Francis Robinson in the 1940s. First, you look over the headings. Then you turn them into questions, read to find the answers, recite what you have learned without looking, and finally review.

Its parts include good ideas, such as asking questions and retrieving. Those two have solid evidence. The method as a whole hasn't been tested directly very well, and the review by Dunlosky and his colleagues didn't find enough evidence to rate it. The parts that matter are probably the active ones, the questions and the recitation. Surveying and rereading do less. If you use SQ3R, put your effort into recalling what you have read.

# Starting school later
@src Dunster et al., Science Advances, 2018

Many teenagers don't get enough sleep, since their body clocks shift later in adolescence, and school starts early. In 2018 Gideon Dunster and his colleagues studied a natural experiment in Seattle. Two high schools moved their start time from 7:50 to 8:45 in the morning. The researchers asked students to wear sleep monitors before and after.

Students slept about thirty-four minutes more per night after the change. They went to bed at about the same time, but woke up later. Their grades went up, with the median grade rising by about four percent, and they were late and absent less often. Other studies of later start times have found benefits in test scores and in student well-being. Sleep is not a luxury for teenagers, and starting school later may be one of the simplest ways to help.

# Do we really lose attention after ten minutes?
@src Bradbury, Advances in Physiology Education, 2016

Many teachers have been told that students can pay attention for only ten or fifteen minutes in a lecture. The claim is repeated in books about teaching and on the internet. In 2016 Neil Bradbury traced the studies that were cited for it. He found that the evidence was thin, and that some of the citations led nowhere.

The studies that existed didn't show a steady drop in attention at a fixed time. Attention rose and fell through a lecture, depending on the content and on what the teacher was doing. The most reliable finding was that attention returns when the activity changes. There's no magic number of minutes. But the idea that varying the lesson, with a question, a problem or a story, can help is well supported.

# Wait three seconds
@src Rowe, Journal of Research in Science Teaching, 1974

How long do teachers wait after asking a question? In the early 1970s Mary Budd Rowe recorded hundreds of classrooms. On average, the teachers waited less than one second before calling on a student, or answering the question themselves. The silence was hard for them to bear.

When teachers were trained to wait three seconds or more, many things changed. The answers got longer and more thoughtful. More students took part, including those who rarely spoke. Students asked more questions, and the teachers asked deeper ones. The same applies to a conversation at home. Pausing after a question makes more room for a real answer, even though it feels long.

# Math anxiety and the working mind
@src Ashcraft & Kirk, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2001

Some people feel tense or afraid when they have to do math. In 2001 Mark Ashcraft and Elizabeth Kirk tested what it does to thinking. They measured how anxious people were about math, and how well they did on tasks of working memory, such as keeping track of numbers while doing arithmetic.

People with high math anxiety did worse on the working memory tasks that involved numbers, especially when they were also asked to do a calculation. Worry takes up space in working memory, leaving less for the problem. This was an early explanation of why anxiety affects performance. It also suggests why people who understand the math can fail under pressure. Some of the cures are ones that reduce the worry, such as writing about it before a test.

# Parents' math anxiety
@src Maloney, Ramirez, Gunderson, Levine & Beilock, Psychological Science, 2015

Can a parent's worry about math pass to a child? In 2015 Erin Maloney and her colleagues studied about four hundred first and second graders and their parents. The parents filled out a questionnaire about how anxious they felt about math, and the children took a math test at the beginning and end of the school year.

On average, a parent's anxiety didn't harm the child's progress. But when math-anxious parents often helped their children with homework, the children learned less math during the year, and were more likely to become anxious themselves. The researchers suggested that the parents may have shown their worry, or taught less effective methods. It's not a reason to avoid helping. Parents who are anxious can use a book, a game or an app, and speak about math with ease.

# Spreading practice over years
@src Bahrick, Bahrick, Bahrick & Bahrick, Psychological Science, 1993

How long do we remember a language that we learned? In 1993 Harry Bahrick and his family ran a study that lasted for years. Adults learned Spanish vocabulary with the same number of practice sessions. For some, the sessions were two weeks apart. For others, they were four weeks apart, or eight weeks apart. Then the researchers tested them one, two, three and five years later.

The people who had practiced with longer gaps between sessions remembered more, even after five years. The effect was large. Wide spacing gave better long-term memory than narrow spacing, even when it meant fewer sessions in the same period. It's some of the best evidence that the spacing effect lasts. If you want to keep what you learn for years, space out the practice over months.
