# Thinking about it over and over
@src Nolen-Hoeksema & Morrow, J. Personality and Social Psychology, 1991

When something bad happens, some people go over it again and again in their minds, asking why it happened and what it means. Psychologists call this rumination. On October 17, 1989, a strong earthquake struck near Loma Prieta, in California. Susan Nolen-Hoeksema and Jannay Morrow had, by chance, measured the moods and the ways of coping of 137 college students about two weeks earlier, and they were able to follow them up.

They checked the students again ten days after the earthquake and about seven weeks after. Those who already tended to ruminate had more symptoms of depression and stress at both times. Students who spent more time in the first ten days brooding on the quake were also still more distressed at seven weeks. It was an unplanned natural experiment, and it suggested that how we think about trouble can keep it going.

# Why a lower rung means a shorter life
@src Marmot et al., Whitehall studies, 1967 onward

In the late 1960s the epidemiologist Michael Marmot and his colleagues began to follow more than 17,500 male civil servants in London, all of whom had steady jobs, secure pay and access to health care. The men worked in government offices, in a strict ladder of grades. At the top were the administrators, and at the bottom were messengers and doorkeepers. The researchers recorded each man's grade, and then waited to see who died.

The men in the lowest grade had death rates about three times higher than those in the highest grade. It was not a gap between the rich and the poor, since all of them were in work. The gradient ran through every step, with each grade healthier than the one below. The risk factors of smoking, weight and blood pressure explained only a part. Marmot proposed that a low position, with less control over one's work, is itself a source of stress, and a risk to health.

# The town that was said to be safe from heart attacks
@conf mixed
@src Bruhn & Wolf, The Roseto Story, 1979; Egolf et al., American Journal of Public Health, 1992

In the 1960s a doctor in Pennsylvania mentioned to the physician Stewart Wolf that he had hardly seen any of the patients in the town of Roseto with heart disease. The town had been settled by people from one village in Italy. Wolf studied it, and found that the death rates from heart attack were much lower than in the neighboring towns. The people ate fatty food and smoked, but they lived in close families, with several generations in the same house.

The finding was published in 1964 and became known as the Roseto effect. Wolf's explanation was the closeness of the community. In 1992 a team that included Wolf compared the death certificates from 1935 to 1985. The advantage lasted for about thirty years, and then vanished, as the younger people moved away, and the old customs weakened. The result fits the idea that social ties protect health, but it does not prove it.

# The places where people live to a hundred
@conf contested
@src Buettner, National Geographic, 2005; Newman, 2019 and 2024

Starting in 2005 the writer Dan Buettner, with demographers, described several regions of the world where people were said to live unusually long. They included Okinawa in Japan, Sardinia in Italy, Nicoya in Costa Rica, and Ikaria in Greece. He called them Blue Zones, and listed the habits of their people, such as eating plants, staying active, and keeping close ties with family and friends.

The demographer Saul Justin Newman has argued that the records of age in these places are unreliable. He points to errors in old documents, to pension claims, and to the loss of birth records, and notes that the regions are often poor, with low national life expectancy. The researchers behind the Blue Zones answered that they checked ages from several sources. The dispute is not settled. It is a good reason to check the records behind a claim of extreme age.

# Abilities that peak late
@src Schaie, Seattle Longitudinal Study, from 1956

In 1956 the young psychologist K. Warner Schaie, for his doctoral research at the University of Washington, began to measure the mental abilities of adults of different ages, in Seattle. He kept going for the rest of his career. Every seven years he tested the same people again, and brought in new groups, so that he could tell apart the effect of getting older from the effect of the year that one was born.

The results showed that different abilities follow different courses. Skills in vocabulary and verbal meaning went on improving into middle age, and declined only late, in the seventies. Abilities that require working out new problems, or speed, start to fall earlier. Comparing different people of different ages can make the decline look steeper than it is, since later generations tend to score higher. Most people stay mentally able for a long time.

# Can handwriting reveal character?
@src Ben-Shakhar et al., 1986; Neter & Ben-Shakhar, 1989

Some employers have asked experts in handwriting analysis, called graphologists, to judge job applicants from a sample of writing. The experts claim to read personality in the slant, size and pressure of the letters. In the 1980s the Israeli psychologists Gershon Ben-Shakhar, Maya Bar-Hillel and their colleagues tested it. They gave graphologists handwriting samples and asked them to predict how the writers would do at work.

The graphologists did no better than chance, and a psychologist who knew nothing of graphology did as well. In 1989 Eyal Neter and Ben-Shakhar pooled seventeen studies. The validity was near zero when the script was neutral, such as copying a text. When the writers told about their lives, the predictions did better, but the content of what they said was the reason, and not the handwriting. People believe in graphology because the readings sound right about anyone.

# The born criminal
@conf mixed
@src Lombroso, L'uomo delinquente, 1876; Goring, The English Convict, 1913

In 1876 the Italian doctor Cesare Lombroso published a book that claimed that some people are born criminals. He had examined skulls and bodies of prisoners, and believed that they showed signs of an earlier stage of human evolution, such as a sloping forehead, large jaws and long arms. These signs, he said, could be used to spot a criminal. The idea was very influential in Europe and America.

In 1913 the English prison doctor Charles Goring published a large study. With Karl Pearson's statistical laboratory, he measured ninety-six traits of more than three thousand convicts, and compared them with other groups. He found no physical type of criminal. The convicts were on average a little smaller and lighter, and scored lower on mental tests, but he took those differences to be inherited, a view that was itself criticized. Lombroso's idea lost its standing, but it left a lasting habit of judging character from looks.

# The students who were photographed nude
@conf mixed
@src Sheldon, Atlas of Men, 1954; Rosenbaum, New York Times, 1995

From the 1940s to the 1960s, freshmen at many American universities, including Harvard, Yale and Princeton, were asked to pose for photographs without clothes. The reason given was a study of posture. The psychologist William Sheldon used thousands of the pictures for a different purpose. He rated each body on three scales, which he called endomorphy, mesomorphy and ectomorphy, meaning roundness, muscle and thinness, and claimed that body type reveals temperament.

His 1954 Atlas of Men sorted the types on a scale of one to seven. Other scientists rejected the link to character, and a former assistant said that his methods were unsound. In 1995 the writer Ron Rosenbaum reported in the New York Times that the pictures were in the Smithsonian, and that many of the people photographed had never been told what the pictures were really used for. Most of the photographs were later destroyed. It is a case of bad science and bad consent.

# The self as a performance
@src Goffman, The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life, 1956 and 1959

In the late 1940s the Canadian sociologist Erving Goffman spent more than a year on Unst, one of the Shetland Islands, watching how the islanders behaved in everyday life. Out of it came his book, The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life. His idea was that social life resembles theater. We play roles, with a front stage, where we manage the impression that we make, and a back stage, where we can relax and drop the act.

A waiter who is smooth in the dining room may swear in the kitchen. A teacher who is calm in class may complain in the staff room. He called it impression management, and argued that it is not fake, because it is how we all keep social life running. His work led to a style of research that looks at the small details of gestures and speech. It makes a person wonder which of their own roles they take for granted.

# The one factor behind many tests
@src Spearman, American Journal of Psychology, 1904

At the start of the twentieth century the English psychologist Charles Spearman made an observation about schoolchildren. Those who did well in one subject, say classics, tended to do well in others, such as French, mathematics and music. They also tended to do well on simple tests of discriminating pitch and weight. Every correlation between these tests was positive. Nobody expected that if the mind were a set of unrelated skills.

In 1904 Spearman proposed that there is a general factor, which he called g, that is in play in all mental tasks, along with specific factors for each. The idea became the foundation of intelligence testing. People still argue about what g is and how much it explains. But the finding that mental tests tend to correlate positively, which is sometimes called the positive manifold, has been found over and over, in many countries and in many kinds of tests.
