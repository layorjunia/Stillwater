# Ad hominem

You hear it all the time: "You can't trust his budget plan, he went bankrupt once." His history might be worth knowing about, but whether the plan adds up is a separate question, and you can check the numbers without knowing a thing about the man. The Latin means "to the person," and that is the mistake, answering the speaker when you should be answering the claim.

There is one exception worth keeping in mind. If someone has a real conflict of interest, pointing it out is fair, because it gives you a reason to look more closely at what they say. A salesperson who is paid by commission may well be right about the product. You just shouldn't take the claim on faith. Look at the evidence yourself, and let the evidence, not the person, carry the argument.

# Straw man

Someone says the schools in town need more funding, and the reply is, "So you want to bankrupt the country?" That is a straw man. A weaker, sillier version of the claim has been set up in place of what was actually said, because a scarecrow is much easier to knock over than a real opponent.

It works because the listener sees an argument being won and doesn't notice that it was a different argument. People do it by accident as well as on purpose, since we tend to hear the most alarming version of a view we disagree with. If you catch yourself rebutting a position in its worst form, stop and restate theirs in its best. A good way to check is to ask whether the person you're answering would say, "Yes, that's what I meant."

# Steelmanning
@src Dennett, Intuition Pumps (2013)

If a straw man is an easy version of someone's view, a steelman is the strongest version you can build. It is the opposite habit. The philosopher Daniel Dennett, drawing on the game theorist Anatol Rapoport, suggests a short list of rules for criticizing someone fairly. First, restate their position so clearly and fairly that they say, "I wish I'd put it that way."

Second, list the points you agree with. Only after that are you allowed to say where you disagree. It takes real effort, and it can feel like giving ground to the other side. But it tends to make the argument that follows more honest, and often shorter, because you aren't spending time on a version of their view that nobody holds. It also gives you a chance to find out that you were wrong.

# False dilemma

"You're either with us or against us" is a famous example of a trap in which a complicated situation is squeezed into two options. It sounds decisive, and it makes the speaker's side seem like the only reasonable one. But in real life there is usually a third choice. You might agree with some of it, want to wait, or object to the way the choice has been framed.

When someone offers you an either/or, the useful question is what's missing from the list. Sometimes the best answer is "neither," and sometimes it is "it depends on something you haven't mentioned yet." The trap works best when you feel rushed, so slowing down is itself a defense. Two options are almost never the whole menu, and a person who insists they are is often trying to save you the trouble of thinking.

# Slippery slope

The claim goes like this: if we allow this small thing, the next step will happen, and then the next, until we arrive at a disaster. Sometimes that is true, because chains of events do happen. A policy can set a precedent, and a habit can grow. So the argument isn't always wrong.

It becomes a fallacy when the links are simply asserted rather than shown. A fair slope argument has to explain why each step would really lead to the next, and why nothing could stop the slide along the way. Many societies have allowed a small thing and then stopped, so the steps aren't automatic. Without a reason for each one, a slippery-slope claim is a worry dressed up as a proof, and you can ask which step is the one you're actually afraid of.

# Appeal to authority

We can't check everything ourselves, so relying on experts is normal and usually wise. Nobody tests the safety of every bridge before crossing it. The fallacy lies in leaning on someone's fame or position instead of their actual expertise. A celebrated physicist's opinion on nutrition doesn't count for much, and neither does a doctor's opinion on geology.

What does carry weight is the agreement of people who actually study the subject, because several independent experts agreeing is hard to dismiss. Even then, it is strong evidence and not proof, since experts sometimes revise their views as new findings come in. So a decent question to ask is whether the person is speaking inside their own field, and whether others in that field would say the same.

# Circular reasoning

Here is a classic: "The book is true because it says so, and what it says is always true." The conclusion is hiding inside the premise, so the argument goes around in a circle and never gets anywhere new. It's a bit like trying to pull yourself up by your own shoelaces.

It can be hard to spot when the circle is large, with a few steps in between, because each step sounds reasonable by itself. The older name for it is "begging the question," which in this use means assuming the very thing you're supposed to prove. A good test is to ask whether someone who doubted the conclusion would accept your premises. If the only way to accept a premise is to already believe the conclusion, then the argument hasn't done any work.

# Post hoc ergo propter hoc

The Latin means "after this, therefore because of this." A rooster crows just before sunrise every morning, but nobody thinks the rooster raises the Sun. Yet we make the very same mistake all the time. We start a new vitamin and the cold goes away, or we change a setting and the problem stops, and we give the credit to what we did.

Something happening first is a clue about cause, but only a clue. It could be coincidence. Colds clear up by themselves, and problems often fix themselves. Or something else could be causing both. To get past the guess, you need some way of seeing what would have happened without the change, such as a comparison group, or a few repeats where you do it and don't do it. Otherwise, you only know that one thing followed another.

# No true Scotsman
@src Flew, Thinking About Thinking (1975)

The philosopher Antony Flew gave an example in 1975. A man reads in the paper that a Scotsman has done something terrible, and he says, "No Scotsman would do that." The next day he reads about a Scotsman who did exactly that. He answers, "Well, no true Scotsman would do that."

The group has been quietly redefined to keep the original claim safe. Now "Scotsman" doesn't mean someone from Scotland, but only the Scots who behave in a way the speaker approves. The claim can no longer be wrong, which sounds like a strength but really means it says nothing. Whenever a generalization meets a counterexample and someone changes the definition instead of the claim, you are probably watching this move. It's worth asking, "What would count as a case that proves me wrong?"

# Tu quoque

The Latin is "you too." A doctor who smokes tells a patient to quit, and the patient replies, "You smoke, so why should I listen to you?" The doctor may well be a hypocrite, and that might be worth saying. But the dangers of smoking don't depend on who delivers the message.

The charge of hypocrisy is about the speaker, not about the claim. It can be a fair thing to raise if you're judging someone's character, or whether they're sincere. It just doesn't touch the question of whether the advice is right. If you notice that you're dismissing advice because the person giving it doesn't follow it, ask yourself whether the advice would still be correct if it came from someone who did. Often it would, and the person may have learned it the hard way.

# Red herring

You ask a colleague why the report is late, and the answer is a long account of how hard the team has been working this month. Everything in it may be true, but it doesn't answer what you asked. A red herring changes the subject to something nearby that feels related, and is easier or more comfortable to talk about.

The name comes from the idea of dragging a strong-smelling fish across a trail to throw off the hounds. You can often spot one by comparing the question with the reply. If the reply wouldn't have changed when the question did, you are being steered somewhere else. A polite way to bring the conversation back is to say, "That's helpful, and I'd still like to know why the report is late." Then simply wait for the answer.

# Equivocation

A word can have more than one meaning, and an argument can quietly switch between them. Here's a silly one: "A feather is light. What is light cannot be dark. So a feather cannot be dark." The first "light" means "not heavy," and the second means "not dark." The argument sounds valid until you notice that the word has changed under your feet.

Real examples are less obvious, and they tend to gather around big, slippery words such as "theory," "natural" or "freedom." Someone says that evolution is "just a theory," meaning a guess, when the scientific meaning of a theory is a well-tested explanation. Asking "What do you mean by that word here?" can unravel a lot of confusion. It's a polite question, and it often turns out that two people who seemed to be arguing were using the same word for different things.

# The burden of proof

Whoever makes a claim is responsible for supporting it. If a friend says there is a hidden ninth planet in our solar system, it's up to them to show the evidence. It isn't up to everyone else to prove there isn't one. The side that asserts something new has the job of making the case.

"Prove me wrong" quietly flips the burden, and it's a common tactic because it feels fair. It doesn't mean every claim needs a laboratory study before you can mention it, since plenty of ordinary claims can be accepted on a friend's word. But the more surprising the claim is, the more the person making it has to bring. That's a fair rule, and it protects you from having to disprove everything you are told.

# Russell's teapot
@src Russell, "Is There a God?" (written 1952)

In 1952 the philosopher Bertrand Russell wrote that if he claimed a small china teapot was orbiting the Sun somewhere between Earth and Mars, too small for any telescope to see, nobody could disprove it. But it would be absurd for him to expect people to believe it on that basis. The burden of proof, he said, was his.

His point was about how the burden works. You can't disprove a claim that has been built to be unfalsifiable, and that fact doesn't make the claim any more likely. If it did, anyone could make you believe anything just by wrapping it in enough vagueness. The teapot has become a shorthand for that idea. When a claim can't be tested, the right response isn't to disprove it, but to notice that nothing has been established yet.

# The Texas sharpshooter

Imagine a man who fires a lot of shots at the side of a barn, then paints a bullseye around the tightest cluster and announces that he is a marksman. That's the fallacy. It's looking at a great deal of data, finding a pattern after the fact, and presenting it as though you had predicted it.

With enough numbers, patterns appear by chance. If you check a thousand foods against a hundred diseases, some will line up. A pattern is far more convincing if it was named before anybody looked, or if it shows up again in fresh data that wasn't used to find it. So when someone shows you a striking cluster, a fair question is how many other places they looked before they found it.

# Appeal to nature

The idea that natural things are good for you and artificial things are bad sounds sensible, and it sells a lot of products. But arsenic, hemlock and tobacco are all perfectly natural. Vaccines, seat belts and most modern medicines were made by people. Where something comes from tells you very little about whether it's safe.

There's something appealing about the natural, and it often is gentler, but "often" isn't "always." Disease is natural, and so is a lot of suffering that we have worked to reduce. The question to ask isn't whether something is natural, but what evidence there is for this particular thing, at this dose, for this purpose. A plain-sounding label can be on a product that has never been tested at all.

# Appeal to tradition

For roughly two thousand years, doctors treated many illnesses by draining blood from their patients. The practice only began to fade in the nineteenth century. It lasted because it was traditional, and because people sometimes got better anyway, which seemed to prove that it worked.

That a practice has been done for a long time shows that it has survived, not that it works. Tradition can carry real wisdom, and some old customs encode lessons learned the hard way. But an old practice needs testing the same way a new idea does. A good question is what the tradition was meant to accomplish and whether it actually does that. Sometimes the answer is yes, and sometimes it turns out we've been bleeding people for centuries.

# Bandwagon appeal

For most of human history, most people believed the Sun circled the Earth. They were wrong, and the number of believers had nothing to do with the truth. A crowd can be mistaken as easily as an individual, and sometimes more easily, because people copy one another.

Popular opinion can be a useful clue, since sometimes many people really do know something. That's the case when they are independently informed, like a hundred people who have each tried a restaurant and found it good. It isn't the case when they're all repeating what they heard from each other. How many people believe something tells you about people. It doesn't tell you about the world. So the thing to ask is why they believe it, and whether each of them could have known.

# Appeal to emotion

A charity commercial with a crying child can move you, and there is nothing wrong with that. Feeling something is part of being human, and it's often what gets us to help. But the commercial can't tell you whether the charity actually helps children, how much of the money goes to overhead, or whether your donation would do more good elsewhere.

Feelings are a fine reason to care and to act. The mistake comes when a feeling stands in for evidence on a question that evidence could settle. Fear sells security systems, anger sells votes and pity sells donations, and in each case the emotion may be real while the claim underneath it is unchecked. It's worth letting yourself feel it and then, before you act, asking what the facts say.

# The genetic fallacy

This fallacy is about origins. It judges an idea by where it came from instead of by whether it holds up. A sound argument doesn't become unsound because someone disreputable made it, and a poor argument isn't rescued by a prestigious source. The number zero was a good idea no matter who first used it.

The history of an idea can be interesting, and it sometimes points to a reason for caution. If a study was paid for by the company that sells the product, you may reasonably check it more carefully. But when you want to know whether something is true, you have to look at the idea itself and the evidence for it. Where it started is a fact about its past, not a fact about its truth.

# Fallacy of composition

Just because every part has a property doesn't mean the whole has it. A team made up of the best players in the league may still play badly together, because what matters is how they fit. A building made of lightweight bricks can still be heavy. The properties of the parts don't always add up to the properties of the whole.

Or think of a parade. You can see better by standing on your toes, but if everyone stands on their toes, nobody sees any better. What's true for one person acting alone can fail when everyone does the same thing, and economists see this in many places. One family saving more can be good for that family, while a whole country saving more at once may slow things down. So check the leap from parts to whole.

# Hasty generalization

You meet two rude people from a small town on a single trip, and you decide that the town is full of rude people. But two is too few to say much about a town of thousands. We all do this, and some of it can't be avoided, because we have to form impressions from limited experience.

The trouble is how quickly an impression hardens into a rule. A vivid first meeting can stick in the mind for years. The remedy is to hold your impressions loosely and to ask a couple of questions. How many cases have I really seen? And were they typical, or were they just the ones that stood out? A small sample can be a fine reason to be curious, and a poor reason to be sure.

# The anecdotal fallacy

"My grandfather smoked a pack a day and lived to 95." It's a true story, and it may even be dear to you. But one case can't outweigh studies of millions of people, because there will always be some who beat the odds. The studies describe the odds, and the story is one of the exceptions that the odds predict.

Personal stories are vivid and memorable, and that is exactly why they can feel stronger than they are. A face and a voice stay with us in a way that a percentage never will. Stories are a good way to start asking a question, because they make us care. They are a poor way to answer it. When you hear one, you can ask what the larger pattern looks like, and whether the story is typical of it.

# The middle ground fallacy

If one person says the Earth is round and another says it is flat, the truth isn't that it is slightly curved. The middle ground fallacy assumes that when two sides disagree, the right answer must lie halfway between them. It feels fair and polite, and it lets everyone avoid taking a side.

Sometimes a compromise is wise, as when you split a bill or settle on a time that works for both people. But on factual questions, one side can simply be right, and splitting the difference then just means being half wrong. The position in the middle of two claims has to be judged on its own evidence, like any other. When two people disagree, it's worth asking whether the question is about values, where compromise makes sense, or about facts, where it doesn't.

# The nirvana fallacy

A seat belt doesn't save everyone in a crash. That is true, and it's no reason to skip it. The nirvana fallacy rejects a good-enough solution because it isn't perfect, comparing it with an imaginary ideal instead of with the real alternatives.

The useful comparison is always between the options you actually have. Locks don't stop every burglar, and vaccines don't prevent every case, and diets don't work for everyone. If they reduce the harm, they have done what they could. A policy, a diet or a plan doesn't need to solve everything in order to be worth doing. It only needs to be better than the alternative, and "do nothing" is one of the alternatives. When you hear a perfect-or-nothing objection, ask what the better choice would be.

# The loaded question

"Have you stopped cheating on your taxes?" Whether you answer yes or no, you have admitted to cheating. The question has an assumption built into it, and it traps the person answering. A courtroom lawyer might use one deliberately, and it can also happen by accident in everyday conversation, when a question carries a hidden claim.

The best response is to refuse the premise. You might say, "I haven't cheated on my taxes, so the question doesn't apply," before answering anything else. It can feel awkward to stop and object, because a quick yes or no seems expected. But if you let the premise stand, you've agreed to it. It helps to listen to a question for what it takes for granted, as well as for what it asks.

# Special pleading

Someone says that rules about being on time are important, except in their own case, where they have good reasons. Maybe they do. But special pleading skips the step of showing why this case is different, and simply asks for an exemption from a rule that everyone else is expected to follow.

It's easy to spot in others and hard to spot in ourselves, because our own circumstances always feel unusual to us. We know all the details of our own situation, and we see only the outline of everyone else's. A fair test is to ask whether you would accept the same excuse from someone else. If you wouldn't, then you may be asking for something you wouldn't give, and it is worth thinking again.

# Moving the goalposts

Someone says, "Show me one study that proves it." You find one. "Well, I meant a large study." You find a large one. "I meant a randomized trial." Each time the evidence is delivered, the standard shifts, so the finish line always stays a step ahead.

If nothing could satisfy the person, then the question was never really about evidence. They may not even know it, since the habit often comes from not wanting to lose. A good-faith discussion starts by agreeing on what would change each person's mind, before anyone goes looking for it. It's a fair thing to ask out loud: "What would you accept as proof?" And it's useful to ask yourself the same thing before you start.

# Poisoning the well

Before you've heard a single word from a speaker, someone says, "Don't listen to him, he's a liar." That can make everything the person says afterward sound suspect. And yet, even if the charge were true, what he says next still has to be judged on its own merits. A liar can say true things, and an honest person can be wrong.

The harm is done in advance, which is why it works so well. By the time the speaker opens his mouth, the audience has already been told how to hear him. When someone tries to discredit a source before you've heard from it, ask for the specific points to check. If the points hold up, the warning wasn't needed. If they don't, then the warning was a way to avoid having to answer them.

# Cherry-picking

Say there are thirty studies on a question, and three of them found an effect. A person who wants you to believe in it cites just those three. Each citation is accurate, and yet the picture they give is misleading, because the twenty-seven others never appear.

Cherry-picking can hide inside honest facts, so it's hard to catch by checking each claim one at a time. Every sentence can be true and the whole thing still false. The better question to ask is "What does all the evidence say?" That's why reviews that pool many studies are worth more than any single paper, and why it's reasonable to be suspicious when someone cites only a few. It can happen to us too, when we gather only the evidence that fits.

# The ecological fallacy

Looking at countries, you might find that places with higher average income also have higher rates of some disease. It's tempting to conclude that richer people are more likely to get it. But within each country, the wealthy could actually be healthier than the poor. The country-level pattern and the individual-level pattern can point in different directions.

A pattern across groups doesn't have to hold for the individuals in them. Statistics about averages describe populations, and they can mislead when we apply them to a single person. A group's average height says little about the height of the person next to you. The rule of thumb is to match the level of the evidence to the level of the question. If the data is about countries, it can tell you about countries.

# The prosecutor's fallacy
@src Royal Statistical Society statement, 2001; Court of Appeal, 2003

In 1999 Sally Clark, a British solicitor, was convicted of murdering her two baby sons, who had died suddenly. A pediatrician told the court that the chance of two such deaths happening in one family was 1 in 73 million. The jury heard that number as a sign of her guilt.

The number was wrongly calculated. It also confused two different questions: how rare the evidence would be if she were innocent, and how likely she is to be innocent given the evidence. Those can be very far apart, and a rare event is still much more likely than a double murder, which is rarer still. The Royal Statistical Society raised the alarm in 2001, and her conviction was overturned in 2003. The lesson is that "this would be very unlikely by chance" isn't the same as "so it wasn't chance."

# Argument from ignorance

"Nobody has shown that ghosts don't exist, so they must." The lack of proof against something isn't proof for it. You can reverse it as well: "No one has proven it's real, so it isn't." Both versions treat the absence of an answer as if it were an answer.

There's a subtle exception. If you've searched properly in the places where evidence would show up, and found none, that absence does count for something. A thorough search that comes up empty is informative. A doctor who runs the right test and finds nothing has learned a lot. An absence of looking isn't the same, though. So the question is whether anyone has really checked, and whether the checking would have found it.

# Argument from incredulity

"I just can't imagine how that could be true, so it can't be." The limit of one person's imagination isn't a law of nature. Quantum mechanics is hard to picture, and so is the size of the universe, or how a single cell becomes a human being. They are all true anyway.

Finding something hard to believe is a signal to learn more and to check, but it isn't a reason to conclude that the claim is wrong. Our intuitions evolved for a world of everyday sizes and speeds, and they often fail outside it. It can help to separate "I can't see how" from "it can't be." The first is a fact about you and the second is a claim about the world, and only the second needs evidence.

# Whataboutism

Someone criticizes one action, and the reply is, "But what about when your side did it?" It shifts the discussion from the topic to the other person's record. It can feel like fair play, and sometimes the point is worth making, since consistency matters.

But both things might be wrong, or one might be worse, or they might be quite different. Pointing at someone else's fault doesn't make the first complaint go away. If you hear it, you can say, "Maybe so, and we can talk about that too. Right now, we're discussing this." It keeps the conversation on one subject at a time. And if you catch yourself reaching for a "what about," it's worth asking whether you are answering the point or avoiding it.

# False equivalence

Two things are described as the same because they share one feature, though they differ enormously in scale or kind. Treating a small slip and a deliberate fraud as equivalent because both are "mistakes" is an example. The shared word hides the difference that matters most.

It can sound balanced and fair, which is what makes it appealing. Saying that both sides did something sounds even-handed. But a parking ticket and a bank robbery are both about breaking a rule, and no one thinks they're equal. A good check is to ask how the two things compare in size, intent and consequences, and not just whether the same word applies. The differences in degree are usually where the truth is.

# The fallacy fallacy

Imagine someone argues for a true conclusion with a bad argument. You point out the flaw. Did you just prove that the conclusion is false? No. A bad argument for something doesn't make it untrue. It only means that this particular reasoning doesn't establish it.

Someone can be right for the wrong reasons. A friend might tell you rain is coming because their knee aches, and the rain might come anyway. Knowing about fallacies is a useful skill, but it can turn into a way of winning arguments instead of finding the truth. The fair response is to say the argument fails, and then to look for better evidence on both sides. That way you stay curious about the answer, whoever offered it.

# Appeal to novelty

A new treatment, gadget or idea is sometimes sold on being new alone, as if newer meant better. Newness tells you nothing about quality. Many new things are improvements, and many aren't, and the ones that aren't tend to be forgotten, so they don't stay around to remind us.

It's the mirror image of the appeal to tradition. The old way might be old because it works, and the new way might be new because nobody has had time to find its problems. The right test is the same one you'd use for anything else: what's the evidence that it works, and compared with what? A product with a shiny label that says "new and improved" has still only told you one thing, and it's that it's new.

# Appeal to consequences

"There has to be an afterlife, or life would be pointless." That moves from how we'd like things to be to how they are. Wanting an answer to be true, or fearing what follows if it is false, tells us nothing about whether it's correct.

The world doesn't owe us comfortable facts. It can be a tough habit to notice, because the wish feels like a reason. A person who dreads a diagnosis may argue that the test must be wrong, and a person who hopes for a result may argue that the data must be fine. In both cases the feeling is speaking where the evidence should be. It's worth asking what the evidence says if you set the wish aside, and then deciding what to do about what you find.

# Affirming the consequent

The pattern goes like this: if it rained, the street is wet. The street is wet. Therefore, it rained. It sounds right, but a sprinkler, a street-cleaning truck or a burst pipe would also leave a wet street. The "if" gave one route to a result, and the result can have many causes.

Doctors, detectives and scientists all have to watch for this one. If you have a fever, then you may have an infection, but having an infection doesn't follow from a fever. Seeing an effect doesn't tell you which cause produced it. What it does is give you a list of suspects, and the work is in narrowing the list. A conclusion like "it must have rained" should be offered as the first guess, with a check for the other explanations.

# Denying the antecedent

"If you study, you'll pass. You didn't study, so you won't pass." That doesn't follow, because you might pass anyway. The test could be easy, or you could already know the material. The "if" described one way to reach the outcome, and not the only way.

It's the mirror image of affirming the consequent, and it's just as easy to slip into when you're thinking fast. "If it's a dog, it has four legs. It's not a dog, so it doesn't have four legs" is plainly wrong when you put it that way, since a cat has four legs too. The trouble is that real versions are less obvious. When an "if" appears in an argument, check whether it gives the only route to the result.

# Modus ponens

This is the basic pattern of valid reasoning. "If it's raining, the ground is wet. It's raining. Therefore the ground is wet." If both of the first two statements are true, the conclusion has to be true. There's no way around it.

Logic calls an argument valid when its conclusion follows from its premises, whether or not the premises are actually true. The Latin means "the way that affirms." It sounds almost too simple to be worth naming, yet nearly all everyday deduction runs on it. When you decide that the store is closed because it's Sunday and Sunday closing is the rule, you are using it. Knowing the pattern helps you see what the other patterns, the invalid ones, are trying to imitate.

# Modus tollens
@src Popper, The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934)

This pattern runs backward: "If the theory is right, the planet will appear at this spot. It didn't. So the theory is wrong." The Latin means "the way that denies," and it's the logic of disproof. If a result follows from a claim and the result fails to happen, the claim has to go.

Karl Popper built his picture of science on it. A theory can never be completely proven by confirming cases, however many pile up, but one clear failure can sink it. That's why scientists look hard for results that would contradict their ideas, and why a theory that has survived many serious attempts to break it earns trust. In everyday life, the same move works when you say, "If he were home, the lights would be on. They're off, so he's out."

# Valid versus sound

"All birds can fly. Penguins are birds. Therefore penguins can fly." The reasoning is valid, meaning that the conclusion follows if the premises are true. But the first premise is false, because not all birds can fly, so the argument isn't sound.

A sound argument is valid and also has true premises. It's a distinction that is easy to lose when an argument sounds smooth. A tight chain of reasoning can still lead somewhere false if one of its starting facts is wrong. When you check an argument, you have to ask two questions, and they are different. Does the conclusion actually follow from the starting points? And are the starting points themselves correct?

# Deduction and induction

Deduction guarantees its conclusion if the premises are true. All men are mortal, Socrates is a man, so Socrates is mortal. Nothing new is added that wasn't already contained in the starting points, but the conclusion is certain.

Induction only makes a conclusion likely. The Sun has risen every day so far, so it will probably rise tomorrow. Most of science and daily life runs on induction, because we can rarely be certain, and we have to reason from what we've seen to what we haven't. It's the reason we talk about evidence building up, not about proof. And one surprising case can overturn an inductive rule, as it did for Europeans who had only ever seen white swans until black ones turned up in Australia.

# Falsifiability
@src Popper, The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934)

In 1934 the philosopher Karl Popper suggested that what makes a claim scientific is that it could, in principle, be shown wrong by some observation. A theory that fits whatever happens, like a horoscope that can be read to match any day, doesn't tell you very much. It forbids nothing.

Philosophers still argue about exactly where to draw the line, and real science is messier than the tidy rule suggests. But the habit it teaches is useful, and you can use it on yourself. Ask what result would change your mind. If the answer is "nothing," then notice that. It may mean your belief is based on something other than evidence, which isn't always bad, but it's good to know.

# Hitchens's razor
@src Hitchens, God Is Not Great (2007)

Christopher Hitchens, the writer, put it this way in 2007: what can be asserted without evidence can be dismissed without evidence. It places the burden on the person making a claim, and it gives you permission not to take a bare assertion seriously.

It's a rule of thumb, though, and it's worth being careful about what it says. Dismissing something doesn't prove it false. It just means that you have no reason yet to believe it, which is a different thing. A claim that has been waved aside can still be true, and it can come back with real evidence. The razor is a way of deciding where to spend your attention, and a polite way of saying, "That's interesting, and I'll need more than that."

# The Sagan standard
@src Sagan, Cosmos (1980)

Carl Sagan popularized the idea that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. The more a claim conflicts with what we already know, the stronger the evidence needs to be before we should accept it. If your neighbor says he has a dog, you'd accept that easily. If he says he has a dragon in the garage, you'd want to see it.

It isn't a bar set to be impossible, because extraordinary claims have turned out to be true when the evidence was strong enough. Continental drift and germs both once sounded strange. It's a way of matching your trust to how surprising the claim is, and it recognizes that a blurry photo might be enough for a rumor about a neighbor's dog. For a claim that would overturn a lot of established science, though, you'd want far more.

# Appeal to ridicule

Someone describes an opposing idea in a cartoonish way, and the audience laughs. A laugh isn't a rebuttal, though, and a funny version of an idea isn't the idea. It can feel like a win, because the mood in the room has shifted, but nothing has been shown.

Many true ideas were laughed at when they were new. Continental drift, the idea that the continents slowly move, was mocked for decades before the evidence won. Mockery works because it's easier and more fun than argument, and it makes people afraid to speak up. Be cautious when it has replaced an argument, especially when you find yourself enjoying it. If a view is truly wrong, a straight account of the evidence will usually be enough.

# The association fallacy

"Hitler liked dogs, so liking dogs is bad." It sounds absurd, but the same move is made in subtler ways all the time. We reject an idea because a disliked person or group believes it, or accept one because someone we admire does.

A claim stands or falls on its own evidence, no matter who else agrees with it. A stopped clock is right twice a day, and even a villain can be right about the weather. This doesn't mean you should ignore who's saying what, since sources are a useful clue about reliability. It only means that guilt by association doesn't settle the question. If you want to know whether something is true, look at what the evidence says and not at its fan club.

# Bulverism
@src C. S. Lewis, God in the Dock (1970)

C. S. Lewis named this one after an invented person called Ezekiel Bulver. Bulver, at the age of five, hears his mother say to his father, "Oh, you say that because you are a man." He realizes that you can dismiss anything simply by explaining why the speaker says it, and he goes on to build a career on the discovery.

Lewis's point was that you must first show a claim is wrong. Only then does it become interesting to ask why someone came to believe it. If you start with the explanation, "You only say that because you're rich, or young, or afraid," you haven't addressed the claim at all. And the trick can be turned around on anyone, including you. A claim can be defended or attacked only by looking at the reasons for it.

# Weasel words

"Studies show." "Many experts agree." "Some people say." These phrases sound like evidence while committing to nothing. Which studies? How many experts, out of how many? Who are the "some people"? If the writer can't name them, then nothing has really been claimed that you can check.

They are common in advertising and in news headlines, because they let a writer suggest something without being responsible for it. "Up to 50% off" and "may help reduce" are cousins of the same habit. A good habit is to ask who exactly is being referred to, and what exactly was found. When a sentence gives you a name, a number or a link, you can test it. When it only gives you a feeling of authority, you can't.

# The motte-and-bailey
@src Shackel, 2005

In medieval castles, the motte was a fortified hill you could retreat to, and the bailey was the more comfortable but harder-to-defend courtyard below. In arguments, the bailey is the bold claim someone really wants to make, and the motte is a modest, easy-to-defend claim they fall back on whenever they're challenged.

Once the challenge passes, they go back to the bold one. For example, someone says "the establishment is hiding the truth," and when pressed says only "well, some institutions sometimes hide things," which no one disputes. The philosopher Nicholas Shackel named the pattern in 2005. If someone keeps switching, ask which claim they're actually defending. You can say, "Let's agree on one, and talk about that."

# The Gish gallop

It's named for the debater Duane Gish, who would fire off a rapid string of weak arguments, so many that his opponent couldn't answer them all in the time available. It works because it takes far longer to refute a claim than to make one. A false statement costs a few seconds, while a careful correction can cost several minutes.

In a long list, unanswered points can look like points conceded, and audiences often judge by the count. A sensible response is to pick the two or three strongest and handle those properly, or to say plainly that the list can't be fairly answered at that speed. If you're the one making a case, this is also a reminder that fewer, better arguments are more convincing than many weak ones.

# The ludic fallacy
@src Taleb, The Black Swan (2007)

Nassim Taleb coined this term, from the Latin word for "game," in his 2007 book The Black Swan. It's the mistake of treating real life as if it were a casino, where the odds are known and the rules are fixed. In a casino, the chances of a roulette wheel can be computed exactly. In life, we rarely know the rules, let alone the odds.

Neat probability models are built on assumptions, and the real world can break them. The risks that hurt most are often the ones nobody put in the model, because nobody imagined them. A model is a good tool for thinking, but a poor guarantee. When someone tells you that the chance of failure is one in a million, it's fair to ask what that number assumes, and what happens if the assumption is wrong.

# The narrative fallacy
@src Taleb, The Black Swan (2007)
@conf mixed

After a company succeeds, we tell a story about the founder's grit and vision, and it all seems to fit together. The story is tidy, and it feels like an explanation. We learn the childhood lessons, the bold decisions and the turning points, and they seem to lead naturally to the result.

But luck may have done much of the work, and the same story could be told about the founders who failed, whom we never hear about. Nassim Taleb warned us to be careful of stories that explain too neatly. A good one makes you feel that you understand, and that feeling isn't evidence. The idea is partly a matter of judgment, since stories do help us remember and teach. But when a story is the only support for a claim, it deserves a second look.

# Spurious correlation
@src Vigen, Spurious Correlations (2015)

The author Tyler Vigen collects charts of numbers that move together purely by chance. One of them shows that per-person cheese consumption in America tracked, over ten years, the number of people who died tangled in their bedsheets. The match is striking, and it means nothing at all.

If you compare enough data sets, some will line up by accident. There are so many possible pairs that a few are bound to look like a relationship. A correlation needs a plausible reason, and ideally a repeat on new data, before it means anything. When you see a chart with two lines that rise and fall together, try asking what could make one cause the other, or what could be causing both, before you start believing in the connection.
