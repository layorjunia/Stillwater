# The invention of zero
@src Brahmagupta, 628; Oxford Bodleian, 2017

Many ancient peoples used a mark to hold an empty place in a number, so that 305 could be told from 35. But treating zero as a number in its own right, one you can add and multiply, came from India. It was an idea that took a long time to arrive, and it changed the way people could calculate.

The Bakhshali manuscript, a birch-bark text carbon-dated to somewhere between the 3rd and 4th centuries, uses a dot for zero. In 628 the astronomer Brahmagupta wrote down rules for arithmetic with zero, including what happens when you add it to or subtract it from other numbers. From India the idea traveled through the Arab world to Europe. Without it, our whole way of writing numbers would be impossible.

# Negative numbers
@src Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art

Chinese mathematicians were working with negative numbers around 2,000 years ago. The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art used red counting rods for positive numbers and black ones for negative, which is the opposite of the way accountants use red ink today.

European mathematicians were much slower to accept them, and for centuries many called negative answers "absurd" or "fictitious," since you can't have less than nothing of something. They only became standard in the 1600s and later. It's a good reminder that ideas we find obvious were once hard to swallow. A debt, a temperature below freezing and a position below sea level are all things we now describe easily with a minus sign.

# Pi
@src Lambert, 1761; Lindemann, 1882

Pi is the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter, about 3.14159, and the digits go on forever with no repeating pattern. Every circle, big or small, has the same ratio. It's one of the few numbers that people of every culture have discovered for themselves.

In 1761 Johann Lambert proved that it can't be written as a fraction. In 1882 Ferdinand von Lindemann proved the stronger result that it's transcendental, not the solution of any ordinary algebraic equation. That showed that the ancient puzzle of "squaring the circle" with a compass and straightedge is impossible. The Greeks had tried for two thousand years, and the answer was that no one ever could.

# How many digits of pi do we need?
@src NASA/JPL

You might think space navigation would need thousands of digits of pi, but NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory says that it uses 15 for its most exacting calculations. Fifteen is more than enough for any spacecraft.

They've calculated that even for a circle with a radius of about 15 billion miles, farther than Voyager 1 has traveled, 15 digits would put the circumference off by only about an inch. Computers have calculated 100 trillion digits, for the sheer fun of it. Such records don't help with engineering. They're done as a test of computers and of programmers' skill, and because people are curious. For everyday work, 3.14 is plenty.

# The number e
@src Bernoulli, 1683

The number e, about 2.71828, appears wherever things grow or decay continuously. Jacob Bernoulli found it in 1683 while asking a question about money. If a bank pays 100 percent interest, what happens if it's compounded more and more often, every month, every day, every second?

The amount doesn't grow forever. It approaches e. If you start with one dollar and compound continuously for a year at 100 percent, you end up with about $2.72. The number turns up in population growth, radioactive decay and the bell curve. It's as basic as pi, but it's less well known, perhaps because its story starts with a puzzle about interest.

# Euler's identity
@src Euler, 1748

Euler's identity is e raised to the power of i times pi, plus one, equals zero. It joins five of the most fundamental numbers in mathematics, e, i, pi, 1 and 0, in a single short equation using addition, multiplication and exponents. Each number came from a different branch of mathematics, and yet here they are together.

It comes from a formula discovered by Leonhard Euler in 1748. Many mathematicians consider it the most beautiful equation, and in a survey of readers of a mathematics magazine, it was voted that. The beauty is a matter of taste, but the surprise is real. Nobody would guess, looking at those five numbers, that they had anything to do with each other.

# Imaginary numbers
@src Cardano, 1545; Bombelli, 1572

The square root of minus one can't be an ordinary number, since any number times itself is positive. Yet in 1545 the Italian Girolamo Cardano found that using such "impossible" numbers in the middle of a calculation could give correct, real answers, though he thought they were useless.

Rafael Bombelli worked out the rules for them in 1572. Today, mathematicians write the square root of minus one as i, and use such numbers for alternating current, signal processing and quantum mechanics. The name "imaginary" was meant as an insult, but it stuck. They are no less real than any other number. They're a tool that lets us describe things that go in circles, like waves, and that's why they are so useful.

# There are infinitely many primes
@src Euclid, Elements; GIMPS, 2024

A prime number is divisible only by one and itself. Around 300 BC the Greek mathematician Euclid proved that there are infinitely many. His argument is short: suppose you had a complete list of all the primes. Multiply them together and add one. The result isn't divisible by any prime on your list, so there must be another prime, and the list wasn't complete after all.

It's still a model of an elegant proof. Even so, the biggest primes we know are enormous. The largest found as of October 2024 has more than 41 million digits. A former NVIDIA engineer, Luke Durant, found it by running software on graphics processors in cloud computers across many countries. It's two raised to the power 136,279,841, minus one, a kind of number called a Mersenne prime. Such records are usually broken every few years, with the help of volunteer computing.

# Goldbach's conjecture
@src Goldbach, 1742

In 1742 the mathematician Christian Goldbach wrote to Leonhard Euler suggesting that every even number greater than two is the sum of two primes. For example, 10 is 3 plus 7, and 28 is 5 plus 23. You can try it for yourself, and it always seems to work.

Computers have checked it for every even number up to about four quintillion, and it's never failed. But nobody has found a proof, and it remains one of the oldest unsolved problems in mathematics. It's a striking thing, that a statement a child can understand has defeated the best mathematicians for nearly three hundred years. It's a reminder that "obviously true" and "proved" are not the same.

# Fibonacci numbers
@src Fibonacci, Liber Abaci

In 1202 Leonardo of Pisa, known as Fibonacci, published a book that included a puzzle about how many pairs of rabbits there would be after a year, if each pair has a new pair every month. The answer gave the sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, where each number is the sum of the two before it.

Indian mathematicians had noticed the sequence earlier. Pine cones, sunflower heads and pineapples often show spirals that count in Fibonacci numbers, such as 8 spirals one way and 13 the other. The reason is that the arrangement packs the seeds efficiently as the plant grows. The book itself did something more important. It introduced the Hindu-Arabic numerals, 0 through 9, to Europe, where people were still using Roman numerals.

# The golden ratio
@src Livio, The Golden Ratio (2002)
@conf mixed

The golden ratio, about 1.618, is the number you get when a line is cut so that the whole is to the longer part as the longer is to the shorter. It's the limit of the ratio of consecutive Fibonacci numbers, which get closer to it the further you go.

Many people say it's the secret behind the Parthenon, the Mona Lisa and beauty itself, but those claims are mostly myth and wishful measuring. You can draw a rectangle on almost anything and find the ratio, if you aren't particular about where the edges are. The number itself is genuinely interesting, and it turns up in pentagons and in some plant growth. It's a lovely number without needing the legend.

# The Pythagorean theorem
@src Neugebauer & Sachs, 1945

In any right-angled triangle, the square of the longest side equals the sum of the squares of the other two. If the two short sides are 3 and 4, the long one is 5, since 9 plus 16 is 25. It's the basis of how we measure distance.

A Babylonian clay tablet from about 1800 BC, known as Plimpton 322, lists number sets that fit the rule, so people knew it a thousand years before Pythagoras. Pythagoras's name is attached to it because the first proofs known to us came from the Greek tradition. More than 300 different proofs have been found since, including one by a US president, James Garfield, in 1876. Builders have used the 3-4-5 triangle for thousands of years to make square corners.

# The irrational square root of 2
@src Euclid, Elements

The diagonal of a square with sides of length one is the square root of two, and it can't be written as a fraction. The ancient proof is a clever argument by contradiction. Suppose it were a fraction p over q, in lowest terms. Then you can show that both p and q would have to be even, which contradicts "lowest terms."

A legend says that the Pythagoreans, who believed all numbers were ratios, were deeply disturbed by the discovery. According to one version of the story, a member who revealed it was thrown from a boat, though no one knows whether it is true. What is certain is that the discovery was a shock, because it showed that some lengths can't be measured exactly by whole numbers and their ratios.

# Infinity comes in sizes: Cantor and Hilbert's hotel
@src Cantor, 1891; Hilbert, 1924

It sounds absurd, but there are different sizes of infinity. In 1874 and again in 1891, the German mathematician Georg Cantor showed that you can't make a complete list of all the real numbers, the decimals, even an infinite one. Whatever list you offer, you can build a new number by changing one digit in each entry, and it can't be on the list. So the real numbers are a bigger infinity than the whole numbers. Some contemporaries thought he was mad.

The mathematician David Hilbert used a thought experiment to illustrate how strange infinity is. Imagine a hotel with infinitely many rooms, all full. A new guest arrives. No problem: ask each guest to move to the room with double their number, which leaves all the odd-numbered rooms empty. With infinite sets, a part can be as big as the whole, which is bizarre, and yet it follows from the rules.

# Zeno's paradoxes
@src Aristotle, Physics

Around 450 BC the Greek philosopher Zeno of Elea argued that the fast runner Achilles could never catch a tortoise with a head start. By the time Achilles reaches where the tortoise was, it's moved on a bit, and so on forever. Every time he gets to where it was, it has moved a little further.

The puzzle bothered thinkers for two thousand years, until mathematicians showed that an infinite series of ever smaller steps can add up to a finite total, so that Achilles does catch up. If a half, a quarter, an eighth and so on are added up, they come to exactly one. It's an example of how a simple paradox can lead to deep ideas about infinity, and about how to add up an endless list of numbers.

# Calculus
@src Newton; Leibniz

Calculus is the mathematics of change. It rests on two ideas: finding the slope of a curve at a point, which tells you the rate of change, and finding the area under a curve, which adds up many small pieces. The two turn out to be opposites of each other, and that's the central fact of the subject.

Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz developed it independently, between the 1660s and 1680s, and their followers carried on a bitter dispute over who deserved the credit. It underlies physics, engineering and economics. Anything that changes, such as a falling stone, a growing population or a cooling cup of coffee, can be described with it. Leibniz's notation is the one that most students still learn today.

# The seven bridges of Königsberg
@src Euler, 1736

The city of Königsberg, now Kaliningrad, had seven bridges that connected two islands and the banks of a river. Residents wondered whether you could walk through the city and cross each bridge exactly once. No one could find a way, but no one could prove it was impossible, either.

In 1736 Leonhard Euler showed that you could not. He simplified the map to dots and lines, which created graph theory. The key was to count how many bridges lead to each land mass. It now helps describe networks of all kinds, from roads to the internet to friendships. It's a good example of how the right simplification, throwing away most of the details, can reveal a deep truth.

# The Möbius strip
@src Möbius & Listing, 1858

Take a long strip of paper, give it a half twist and tape the ends together. You get a Möbius strip, a loop with only one side and one edge. August Möbius and Johann Listing described it independently in 1858.

If you draw a line along the middle until you return to the start, you'll find you've covered what looked like both sides. If you cut it down the middle, you don't get two loops. You get one longer loop. It's a classic example of a surface with surprising properties, and you can make one in a minute. Conveyor belts are sometimes made as Möbius strips, so that both sides wear evenly.

# Topology
@src Standard topology text

Topology is a branch of mathematics that studies shapes in terms of what stays the same when you stretch, bend or squash them, without tearing. To a topologist, a coffee cup and a doughnut are the same shape, because each has exactly one hole, and one can be smoothly deformed into the other.

It might sound like a game, but topology has real uses in physics, biology and data analysis. It helps describe how DNA tangles, how the universe might be shaped and what the structure of a large data set is. The subject grew out of Euler's solution of the bridge problem. Its main idea is that for many questions, the exact distances don't matter, and only how things are connected does.

# The four color theorem
@src Appel & Haken, 1976

In 1852 a mapmaker's observation raised a question: can any map be colored with just four colors, so that no neighboring regions share a color? It seems like it should be easy to settle, and yet for over a century, nobody could prove it.

In 1976 Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken did, with the help of a computer that checked nearly 2,000 cases, using over 1,200 hours of computer time. It was the first major theorem proved with a computer, and some mathematicians were uneasy about that, since no person could check every step by hand. Since then, the use of computers in proofs has become more accepted, and the theorem has been verified in other ways.

# Fermat's last theorem
@src Wiles, 1995

Around 1637 the French lawyer and mathematician Pierre de Fermat scribbled in a book margin that no whole numbers satisfy a to the n plus b to the n equals c to the n for n above two. He added that he had a marvelous proof, but the margin was too small.

Nobody found a proof for 357 years. Andrew Wiles finally proved it in 1994, after seven years of working in secret in his attic, using mathematics that didn't exist in Fermat's day. An error was found and fixed in 1995, with the help of a former student, Richard Taylor. Most mathematicians doubt that Fermat's own proof, if he had one, was right. The story shows how a single line in a margin can drive a search for centuries.

# Gödel's incompleteness theorems
@src Gödel, 1931

In 1931 the young logician Kurt Gödel proved something startling. In any consistent system of rules rich enough to include arithmetic, there are true statements that can't be proved within the system. He was only 25 years old.

It ended the dream of David Hilbert and others of finding a complete and consistent set of rules for all of mathematics. It showed that there are built-in limits to what proof can do. The proof works by constructing a statement that says, in effect, "this statement can't be proved." If it could be proved, it would be false, which would be a contradiction. So it's true and unprovable. The result is among the most profound in the history of logic.

# The halting problem
@src Turing, 1936

In 1936 Alan Turing asked whether it's possible to write a program that can look at any other program and tell, in advance, whether it will eventually stop or run forever. He proved that it can't be done, using a clever self-referring argument.

It's a real limit on what computers can do, not just a limit of today's technology, and it was one of the first such results in computer science. It means that there will always be questions about programs that no program can answer. Turing worked this out before the first electronic computer had been built, by thinking about an imaginary machine. His abstract machine is still used to define what it means to compute.

# P versus NP
@src Clay Mathematics Institute

Some problems, like solving a big Sudoku or finding the best route through many cities, are hard to solve but easy to check once someone shows you an answer. The question of whether every problem whose answer can be checked quickly can also be solved quickly is called P versus NP.

It's one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, with a million-dollar prize. Most experts believe the answer is no, that some problems really are harder to solve than to check, but nobody has proved it. If it were yes, it would transform the world. Codes used to protect online banking would be breakable, and many tasks in science and industry could be done much faster. It's one of the deepest open questions in all of science.

# The Millennium Prize Problems
@src Clay Mathematics Institute

In 2000 the Clay Mathematics Institute named seven of the deepest unsolved problems in mathematics and offered $1 million for the solution of each. They were chosen to echo the famous list of problems posed by David Hilbert in 1900.

Only one has been solved so far: the Poincaré conjecture, by the Russian mathematician Grigori Perelman, in 2002 and 2003. He declined the prize and the other honors, including the Fields Medal, saying that he wasn't interested in money or fame. The rest, including the Riemann hypothesis and P versus NP, remain open. Anyone who can solve one will have solved a question that has stumped the best minds for generations.

# Two surprising patterns: Benford and Zipf
@src Newcomb, 1881; Benford, 1938; Zipf, 1935

In many real collections of numbers, like river lengths, populations and stock prices, about 30 percent of them begin with the digit 1, and fewer than 5 percent begin with 9. Simon Newcomb noticed in 1881 that the early pages of logarithm tables were more worn than the later ones, and Frank Benford rediscovered it in 1938. Auditors use it to catch fraud, since invented numbers tend not to follow the pattern.

Another strange regularity applies to words. In nearly any large body of text, the most common word appears about twice as often as the second, three times as often as the third, and so on. The linguist George Zipf described it in 1935. In English, "the" makes up about 7 percent of words. Strikingly similar patterns appear in the sizes of cities and the traffic to websites, though no one fully understands why.

# The bell curve and why it appears
@src Gauss, 1809; Laplace, 1810

Many measurements, such as heights, test scores and measurement errors, cluster around an average and thin out symmetrically on either side, in what's called the normal distribution. About 68 percent of values fall within one standard deviation of the average, 95 percent within two, and 99.7 percent within three. Carl Friedrich Gauss used it in the early 1800s to handle errors in astronomical measurements.

Why does this shape show up so often? The central limit theorem says that if you take the average of many independent random things, the result tends to form a bell curve, whatever the shape of the original distribution. A general version was proved by Pierre-Simon Laplace in 1810. It explains why so many things in nature look bell-shaped, and it is one of the main reasons that statistics works.

# The law of large numbers
@src Bernoulli, 1713

If you flip a fair coin only a few times, you might get seven heads in ten. But as the number of flips grows, the share of heads gets closer and closer to a half. This is the law of large numbers, proved by Jacob Bernoulli, and published in 1713.

It's why casinos always win in the long run. A casino may lose to a lucky player in a single night, but over millions of bets, the small edge in its favor adds up with certainty. It's also a warning that small samples can easily mislead. A hot streak in a few games, or a surprising result from a small survey, doesn't tell you much. The law works only when there are many trials. It doesn't mean that luck will "even out" in the short term.

# The birth of probability
@src Pascal & Fermat, 1654

In 1654 the French mathematicians Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat exchanged letters about a gambling problem. How should the stakes be divided fairly if a game of chance is interrupted before it's finished? A nobleman who liked to gamble had put the question to Pascal.

Their solutions laid the foundations of probability theory. Before then, people had played games of chance for thousands of years but had no mathematics to describe them. The approach they took, to count the possible outcomes and see how many favor each player, is still the basic method. It's a nice case of a practical problem leading to a whole new branch of mathematics. Today, probability is the basis of insurance, genetics, weather forecasts and much else.

# Shuffling a deck
@src Standard combinatorics

The number of ways to arrange a deck of 52 cards is 52 factorial, which is about 8 followed by 67 zeros. That's so large that every time you properly shuffle a deck, you almost certainly create an order that has never occurred before in the history of the world.

If every person on Earth shuffled a deck every second since the start of the universe, they'd barely scratch the surface. The number grows so fast because each card you add multiplies the possibilities by the size of the deck. It's a striking example of how quickly combinations can grow. The same fact is the reason why a good password, built from many characters, is very hard to guess.

# The infinite monkey theorem
@src Borel, 1913

The idea that a monkey hitting keys at random on a typewriter would eventually type the complete works of Shakespeare goes back to Émile Borel in 1913. It's true in theory, if given infinite time. Any particular string of letters has a definite chance, however small, of turning up.

But it would take far longer than the age of the universe for any realistic number of monkeys. The chance of even a short phrase, such as a line of a sonnet, is astronomically small. The theorem is really about the nature of large numbers and chance, not about literature. It's a reminder that "possible" and "likely" can be very far apart, and that a very rare event is not the same as an impossible one.

# Logarithms and the slide rule
@src Napier, 1614; Oughtred, c. 1622

A logarithm turns multiplication into addition. In 1614 the Scottish mathematician John Napier published tables of them, which saved astronomers and navigators years of tedious arithmetic. To multiply two big numbers, you looked up their logarithms, added them, and looked up the answer.

Around 1622 the English minister William Oughtred used the idea to invent the slide rule, two sliding scales marked with logarithms. For about 350 years it was a standard tool, and engineers carried them in their pockets. Apollo astronauts carried slide rules as a backup. Pocket calculators replaced them in the early 1970s. Many quantities are still measured on logarithmic scales, including earthquakes on the Richter scale, sound in decibels and acidity in pH.

# The chessboard and the rice
@src Standard exponential growth example

A legend says the inventor of chess asked a king for a reward of one grain of rice on the first square of the board, two on the second, four on the third, doubling each time. The king agreed, thinking it modest. It sounded like a few bags of rice at most.

By the 64th square, the amount would be about 18 quintillion grains, more rice than has ever been grown. The first half of the board adds up to a manageable pile, and it's in the second half that the numbers become huge. It's a classic illustration of exponential growth, which can look slow at the start and then suddenly overwhelm everything. That's the pattern of an epidemic, a bank balance with compound interest, and a chain reaction.

# The Collatz conjecture
@src Lagarias, 2010

Pick any positive whole number. If it's even, halve it. If it's odd, triple it and add one. Keep going. The conjecture is that you'll always eventually reach 1. Try it with 7: it goes 22, 11, 34, 17, 52, 26, 13, 40, 20, 10, 5, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1.

Lothar Collatz posed it in 1937, and computers have tested it for every number up to nearly 300 quintillion, but there's no proof. The mathematician Paul Erdős said that mathematics may not be ready for such problems. It's a deceptive puzzle. It's so simple that a child can try it, and yet it has defeated the world's best mathematicians for almost a century.

# How we count: binary and base sixty
@src Leibniz, 1703; Standard history of mathematics

Computers use the binary system, where there are only two digits, 0 and 1, instead of our ten. In 1703 the German mathematician Gottfried Leibniz wrote about it, and was fascinated that all numbers could be made from just those two symbols. Each digit is a bit, and eight bits make a byte. Any number, letter, picture or sound can be written in binary, which suits machines that have switches that are either on or off.

We divide hours into 60 minutes and minutes into 60 seconds because the ancient Babylonians counted in base 60. It's a handy number: 60 can be divided evenly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30, so fractions are easy. We still use it for time, and the 360 degrees of a circle probably also come from the Babylonians. The numbers we count in are a human choice.

# Euclid's Elements
@src Euclid, Elements

Around 300 BC the Greek mathematician Euclid wrote the Elements, a textbook that built all of geometry from a handful of simple assumptions, called axioms, by logical proof. Each result was supported by those that came before it.

It was used for over 2,000 years and became one of the most influential books ever written. Abraham Lincoln is said to have studied it as a young lawyer, to learn how to argue and prove a point. The book's lasting influence is the method more than the content. The idea that you can start from simple, clear assumptions and reason your way to surprising conclusions is the basis of mathematics, and of much of science and philosophy.

# Non-Euclidean geometry
@src Gauss; Bolyai; Lobachevsky

Euclid assumed that, given a line and a point not on it, there's exactly one line through the point that never meets the first. For centuries, mathematicians tried to prove this, until in the 1820s and 30s, Gauss, János Bolyai and Nikolai Lobachevsky showed that you can build consistent geometries without it.

On a curved surface, the angles of a triangle add up to more or less than 180 degrees. Think of a triangle drawn on a globe, with one corner at the North Pole and two on the equator, where each angle is 90 degrees. A century later, Einstein used these ideas to describe curved spacetime. It's a good example of how pure mathematics, explored for its own sake, can become the language of physics.

# Fractals
@src Mandelbrot, 1967, 1975

A fractal is a shape that looks similar at every scale, like a coastline, a fern or a snowflake edge. The mathematician Benoit Mandelbrot named them in 1975. He asked a famous question in 1967, "How long is the coast of Britain?" and showed that there's no single answer: the more finely you measure, the longer it gets.

Fractals help describe many natural shapes, such as clouds, mountains, lightning and the branching of blood vessels and lungs. They're also used in computer graphics to make realistic landscapes. A fractal can be generated from a very simple rule repeated over and over. That's the secret of its beauty, since the simple rule creates a complicated and endlessly detailed picture.

# The butterfly effect
@src Lorenz, 1963

In 1961 the meteorologist Edward Lorenz was running a weather simulation on a computer. To save time, he restarted a run in the middle, typing in 0.506 instead of the full figure, 0.506127. The tiny difference grew until the forecast was completely different.

It showed that small changes can lead to large differences later, and it's one reason that weather can only be forecast about ten days to two weeks ahead. The name comes from a talk in which Lorenz asked whether a butterfly flapping its wings in Brazil could set off a tornado in Texas. The point wasn't about butterflies. It was that some systems are so sensitive that long-term prediction is impossible, even when the rules are perfectly known.

# Proof by contradiction
@src Hardy, A Mathematician's Apology (1940)

In this method, you assume the opposite of what you want to prove and show that it leads to an absurdity, so the assumption must be false. It's used to prove that the square root of 2 is irrational, and that there are infinitely many primes.

The English mathematician G. H. Hardy wrote that it "is one of a mathematician's finest weapons." It's like checking a door by assuming it's open and finding you'd walk through a wall. It's also a useful way to think in everyday life. If someone's claim leads to a result that can't be true, the claim has to be wrong, and you don't need to look at every detail to say so.

# Adding 1 to 100: Gauss and induction
@src Standard mathematics history; Standard mathematics text
@conf mixed

According to a well-known story, when Carl Friedrich Gauss was about ten, his teacher asked the class to add up the numbers from 1 to 100, hoping to keep them busy. Gauss gave the answer in moments. He saw that 1 plus 100 is 101, 2 plus 99 is 101, and so on, making 50 pairs of 101, which is 5,050. The tale may have been embellished in the retelling, but the method is real and useful.

The same sum can be proved another way, by induction, a way of showing that something is true for all whole numbers. First show that it's true for the first case. Then show that, if it's true for any case, it must be true for the next one. It's like a line of dominoes: if the first falls, and each one knocks over the next, then they all fall. It proves that the sum of the first n numbers is n times n plus one, divided by two.

# The Mars Climate Orbiter
@src NASA, 1999

In September 1999 NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter, which had traveled nine months to reach Mars, approached the planet too low and was lost. The cause was a mix-up in units. Software built by one team gave the thrust in pound-force seconds, which is the English system, and the navigation team expected newton-seconds, which is metric.

The $125 million spacecraft probably burned up in the atmosphere. A pound-force is about 4.45 newtons, so every correction to the course was off by that factor. It's a vivid reminder that a number without its unit is meaningless. The investigation found that the problem could have been caught, and the lesson led to stricter checks on how teams share data. Even the best engineers can fail on something as basic as units.

# Honeycomb
@src Hales, 2001

Honeybees build their wax cells as hexagons, and a mathematician explains why: it's the most efficient shape. The honeycomb conjecture, which goes back to the ancient Greeks, says that dividing a flat surface into equal-area cells with the least total boundary is best done with a hexagonal grid.

Thomas Hales proved it in 1999. Bees use the least wax to store the most honey. Wax is costly for a bee to make, since it takes roughly six to eight pounds of honey to produce a pound of wax. Circles would leave gaps, and squares or triangles would need more wall. The bees don't know any mathematics, of course. Over a long time, natural selection has favored the colonies that saved wax.

# Noether's theorem
@src Noether, 1918

In 1918 the mathematician Emmy Noether proved a result that physicists consider one of the most beautiful and important. Every symmetry in nature leads to a conservation law. If the laws of physics are the same at all times, energy is conserved. If they're the same everywhere in space, momentum is conserved.

Noether worked for years at the University of Göttingen without pay or a formal position, because she was a woman. When she died in 1935, Einstein wrote that she was the most significant creative mathematical genius since women were admitted to higher education. Her theorem is a pillar of modern physics, and it explains why the conservation laws hold. They aren't a coincidence. They follow from the symmetry of the world.

# Periodical cicadas
@src Goles et al., 2001
@conf mixed

In the eastern United States, some cicadas spend 13 or 17 years underground and then emerge together in huge numbers. Both numbers are primes. One hypothesis is that prime-numbered cycles make it hard for predators or other cicada broods with shorter cycles to line up with them.

If a cicada came out every 12 years, a predator with a cycle of 2, 3, 4 or 6 years would often catch up with it. With a 17-year cycle, an overlap with shorter cycles is rare. It's an elegant idea, but it's a hypothesis, and not all biologists are persuaded. The mathematical argument is attractive, but the evidence is not conclusive. Still, the emergences are a spectacle, with billions of insects coming up at once, and a neat puzzle.

# Pi Day
@src Exploratorium, 1988

Pi Day is celebrated on March 14, because 3.14 are the first digits of pi. The physicist Larry Shaw organized the first celebration at the Exploratorium museum in San Francisco in 1988, with a march around a circular space, followed by fruit pie.

March 14 is also Albert Einstein's birthday. In 2019 UNESCO proclaimed it the International Day of Mathematics. The celebration is playful, but it brings a lot of people together to think about numbers, and many schools and museums hold events. It's a good reminder that mathematics can be fun. If you want to join in, you can bake a pie, recite as many digits as you can, or just measure a circle and divide.
