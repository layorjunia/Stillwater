# The two hundred millisecond gap
@src Stivers et al., PNAS, 2009

When one person finishes a sentence and another begins, the pause between them is astonishingly short. In 2009 Tanya Stivers and a large international team recorded everyday conversations in ten languages, from Danish to Japanese to one spoken in Papua New Guinea, and measured the gaps. In every language, the typical gap was around 200 milliseconds, about the time it takes to blink. Japanese speakers were the quickest, with only a few milliseconds between turns, and the slower languages were still only a few hundred.

That's faster than the brain can produce a sentence from scratch, which takes about half a second or more. So we must be planning our reply while the other person is still speaking, predicting where they'll finish and getting ready to jump in. It's a skill that nobody teaches us, and it's why a long pause on a phone call feels so odd. A silence of one second is, in conversational terms, a very long time.

# How we take turns
@src Sacks, Schegloff & Jefferson, Language, 1974

In 1974 Harvey Sacks, Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson published a paper that started the modern study of conversation. They recorded ordinary phone calls and dinners and noticed that people follow an unwritten set of rules. Usually one person talks at a time, the speaker changes at points where a sentence or thought could be complete, and the next speaker is chosen by the current one, by a glance, a question or a name, or by whoever starts first.

The rules explain a lot of everyday annoyances. Interruptions happen when someone jumps in at a point where the speaker wasn't finished. A person who keeps going without leaving a gap is holding the floor. People who say "um" at the end of a sentence are often signaling that they aren't done. None of it is conscious, which is why conversation can feel effortless when it works and unnerving when it doesn't.

# Why we say uh and um
@src Clark & Fox Tree, Cognition, 2002

Most of us were taught to cut "uh" and "um" from our speech, as if they were mistakes. In 2002 Herbert Clark and Jean Fox Tree looked at large collections of recorded speech and argued that they're words, with jobs to do. "Uh" tends to announce a short delay and "um" a longer one, and listeners can use that to guess how long the wait will be.

They also help speakers hold the floor. A person who says "um" is saying, in effect, that they're still going to talk, so please don't jump in. Listeners seem to pick up the signal, since in some experiments they recognized a word faster after "um" or "uh" than after a silence. That doesn't mean that you should fill every pause. It does mean that a few of them are natural, and not a sign that you're nervous.

# The small sounds of listening
@src Yngve, 1970; Bavelas, Coates & Johnson, 2000

When someone tells you a story, you probably say "mm-hm," "yeah," or "right," or you nod. In 1970 the linguist Victor Yngve called these small signals back-channels. They don't take the floor from the speaker. They show that you're following, and they invite the speaker to continue.

Back-channels matter more than they seem. On the phone, where there's no nodding, a listener who says nothing can make the speaker feel that the line has gone dead. In experiments where listeners were stopped from giving feedback, storytellers became less fluent and told worse stories. And the timing is delicate. A back-channel that comes at the wrong moment can interrupt. A good listener's small sounds fit into the speaker's rhythm like a quiet drum.

# What we mean and what we say
@src Grice, 1975

In 1975 the philosopher Paul Grice asked a simple question: how do we manage to communicate so much more than our words say? If you ask someone how the new colleague is doing and they reply, "Well, he's very punctual," you understand that they have nothing good to say about his work. Nobody said that. You worked it out.

Grice suggested that conversation runs on an agreement to cooperate, with four rules of thumb. Say as much as is needed and no more, say only what you believe to be true, be relevant, and be clear. We don't always follow them, but we assume that others are. When someone seems to break a rule, we look for a reason, and that's how hints, jokes and irony work. Understanding a speaker means guessing their intent, and not only decoding their words.

# Saving face
@src Brown & Levinson, 1987

Why do we say "Could you possibly pass the salt?" when "Pass the salt" would do? In 1987 Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson proposed that it's about face, the public image that each person wants to keep. Everyone wants to be liked and approved of, and everyone wants to be free to act without being imposed on. A request threatens the second, and a criticism threatens the first.

Politeness is the set of tools we use to protect these two needs. We soften requests, hedge opinions, add thanks and use humor. They found similar strategies in languages from English to Tamil to Tzeltal, though the details differ. It means that when someone seems oddly indirect, they may be trying to be kind. And when you want to say something hard, it's worth asking how you can say it without making the other person lose face.

# Common ground
@src Clark, Using Language, 1996

Two people can't understand each other unless they share a base of knowledge. The psychologist Herbert Clark calls it common ground, and it consists of what we both know, and know that we both know. When you say "the new place" to a friend who helped you move, you rely on it. To a stranger, you'd say more.

In conversation, we keep building common ground. We say "uh-huh," ask "you mean the blue one?" and repeat back what we heard, and each is a way of making sure that we're talking about the same thing. Clark calls it grounding. Many misunderstandings come from assuming common ground that isn't there, such as using jargon or an old story. A good habit is to check what the other person already knows, before you start explaining.

# Listening that helps
@src Rogers & Farson, 1957

In 1957 the psychologist Carl Rogers and his colleague Richard Farson wrote a short paper on what they called active listening. Rogers had spent years as a therapist, and he'd noticed that clients often solved their own problems if he simply listened without advising, judging or reassuring. The listener's job is to understand the speaker's feelings and meaning, and to show that they've understood.

The method is simple to describe and hard to do. You restate what you've heard, such as "It sounds like you felt dismissed," you ask for correction, and you resist the urge to fix. It works partly because most people, when they're upset, don't need advice. They need to feel heard first. If you check with someone, "Do you want help with a solution, or do you want me to listen?" you'll often save yourself a lot of trouble.

# Say it back
@src Weger, Castle Bell, Minei & Robinson, 2014

A simple technique from active listening is to paraphrase, which means to say back, in your own words, what you've just heard. In 2014 Harry Weger and his colleagues tested it with pairs of students. Those who had their partner paraphrase their words felt more understood than those whose partner only acknowledged them with nods and "uh-huh." The paraphrase worked better than the less demanding kind of listening.

Paraphrasing does two things. It checks that you've got it right, so you can fix a misunderstanding early. And it shows the speaker that you were listening, which is the part that they feel. The skill is to be brief, and to use your own words and not a parrot's echo. "So the deadline moved and nobody told you" works better than a long summary, and it leaves the other person room to say, "Exactly," or, "Not quite."

# The power of a follow-up question
@src Huang et al., JPSP, 2017

Do people like those who ask more questions? In 2017 Karen Huang, Michael Yeomans, Alison Wood Brooks, Julia Minson and Francesca Gino looked at conversations between strangers and at speed dating events. People who asked more questions were better liked by their partners, and the effect was strongest for follow-up questions, which build on what the other person has just said.

In the speed-dating study, people who asked more follow-ups were more likely to get a second date. Follow-up questions show that you've listened and that you care about the answer. They also give the other person a chance to talk about themselves, which most people enjoy. The next time you're talking with someone, try asking one more question than you usually would, one that picks up on something that they've just said.

# People like you more than you think
@src Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom & Clark, 2018

After a first conversation with a stranger, most of us walk away with a nagging worry about how it went. We replay what we said, and we judge ourselves harshly. In 2018 Erica Boothby and her colleagues asked people to talk with a stranger and then rate how much they liked the other person and how much they thought the other person liked them. Over and over, people thought that they were liked less than they actually were.

They called it the liking gap. It showed up in lab conversations, in workshops, and among college students who lived together for months. The researchers suggest that we're hard on ourselves because we know all our own slips, and we only see the other person's friendly surface. It's a comforting finding. If you think the conversation went badly, there's a good chance the other person enjoyed it more than you did.

# Talking to strangers is better than we expect
@src Epley & Schroeder, 2014

Most commuters would rather sit quietly than talk to the person beside them. In 2014 Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder asked train and bus commuters to do one of three things: talk to a stranger, sit in solitude, or do what they usually do. The people told to talk had the most pleasant commute, and they were no less productive. Others who were asked in advance had predicted that talking would be worse.

The mistake seems to come from imagining that strangers don't want to be bothered, when most people are glad to chat. We expect rejection, and we mostly don't get it. The researchers call it mistakenly seeking solitude. A short, friendly conversation with someone you don't know can lift your mood, and the other person probably enjoys it too.

# The value of weak ties
@src Sandstrom & Dunn, 2014

Most of us think about happiness in terms of close friends and family. But in 2014 Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn asked students to keep track of their interactions, and found that those who had more conversations with acquaintances, such as the barista or a classmate, felt happier and more connected on that day. The effect added to the benefit of time with close friends.

Sociologists call these acquaintances weak ties. They matter for jobs and information, as Mark Granovetter showed in 1973. And they also add small bits of belonging to the day. Saying hello to the neighbor, or chatting for a minute with the person in line, takes little effort and tends to repay it. A life with few close ties can still be warm if there are many small friendly moments in it.

# Deeper talk is less awkward than we fear
@src Kardas, Kumar & Epley, JPSP, 2022

When we meet someone new, we usually stick to the weather and work. In 2022 Michael Kardas, Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley tested what happens when strangers are asked to go deeper, with questions like "What's something you'd like to be more honest about?" Before they began, people expected the deep conversation to be awkward and the shallow one to be pleasant. In fact, the deep conversations were more enjoyable, and they left people feeling closer.

People also underestimated how much their partner would care. We worry that personal talk will embarrass the other person, but they're usually glad that we asked. The lesson isn't that you should skip small talk, which has its own uses. It's that the next level of conversation is often safer and more rewarding than it seems from outside.

# Fast friends
@src Aron et al., 1997

In 1997 the psychologist Arthur Aron and his colleagues devised a procedure for creating closeness between strangers in a single sitting. Pairs of students took turns answering a set of 36 questions that grew more personal as the 45 minutes went on, from "Would you like to be famous?" to "When did you last cry in front of another person?" Other pairs did small talk for the same time. The first group felt significantly closer.

The questions work because they involve mutual, gradual self-disclosure. Each person shares a little, the other responds, and the sharing deepens. The list became famous after a 2015 essay that called it the questions that lead to love. But they don't produce love on their own. What they show is that closeness is built by taking turns being a bit vulnerable, and that it can start sooner than we think.

# How to end a conversation
@src Mastroianni, Gilbert, Cooney & Wilson, PNAS, 2021

Everyone has been stuck in a chat that they wanted to leave, or wondered whether the other person wanted to go. In 2021 Adam Mastroianni and his colleagues asked people about their recent conversations, and had strangers talk in the lab. They found that conversations almost never ended when both people wanted them to. Only about 2 percent did, and only about 30 percent ended when even one of the two people wanted them to. In nearly half, both people had wanted to stop earlier.

People also had no idea when the other person wanted to leave. Most believed that they were in sync and weren't. The reason is that we don't say what we want, because it feels rude, so we guess and guess wrong. A simple fix is to be honest and warm about it, with something like, "I should get back to work, but this was great." Most people would rather know than keep going out of politeness.

# Just the words are a small part
@src Mehrabian & Wiener, 1967

You may have heard that 7 percent of communication is words, 38 percent is tone and 55 percent is body language. It's one of the most repeated numbers in the field, and it's misused. It comes from two studies by Albert Mehrabian in the 1960s, in which people heard single words, such as "dear," spoken in a tone that either matched or didn't match the word, and were asked to judge how the speaker felt.

When the signals conflicted, people relied more on tone and face. That is all that the studies show. Mehrabian himself has said that the numbers apply only to the communication of feelings and attitudes, in cases where the signals are mixed. They don't mean that the words in a speech or an email carry only 7 percent. In most conversations, the words carry a great deal, and it's the tone that shows how to take them.

# Where we look
@src Argyle & Dean, 1965; Kendon, 1967

If you watch two people talking, you'll notice a pattern in their eyes. In the 1960s Michael Argyle, Mark Cook and Adam Kendon found that people look at their partner more when they're listening than when they're speaking. Speakers tend to look away while they're thinking of what to say, and glance back at the end of a sentence, as if handing over the floor.

Eye contact is also a delicate balance. Too little reads as disinterest or shyness, and too much feels like staring. Argyle and Dean proposed in 1965 that people adjust distance and eye contact to keep a comfortable level of closeness. If you stand closer, you tend to look less. It's a reminder that there's no fixed rule, like "always keep eye contact." Natural conversation is a rhythm of looking and looking away.

# The chameleon effect
@src Chartrand & Bargh, JPSP, 1999

People unconsciously copy each other. We cross our legs when the other person does, we pick up their gestures, and even their accent. In 1999 Tanya Chartrand and John Bargh set up an experiment in which a person, who was working with the researchers, either mimicked the participant's posture and movements or didn't. Participants whose movements were mirrored liked their partner more and said that the interaction went more smoothly.

The researchers called it the chameleon effect. It seems to be a quiet social glue that happens when we feel rapport. It also works in reverse, since consciously copying someone can lift their liking of you, though if they notice, it feels odd and false. The most useful takeaway isn't a trick. It's that when you feel in sync with someone, your bodies are probably saying so before your words.

# What rapport is made of
@src Tickle-Degnen & Rosenthal, 1990

Rapport is that feeling of clicking with another person. In 1990 Linda Tickle-Degnen and Robert Rosenthal analyzed what it's made of, and they proposed three ingredients. The first is mutual attention, where each person is focused on the other. The second is positivity, which is warmth and friendliness. The third is coordination, a smooth, balanced rhythm in which each person's moves fit the other's.

They found that the balance of the three shifts over time. At the start of a relationship, positivity matters most, and later coordination counts for more. The model is a useful checklist. If a conversation isn't clicking, ask whether you're paying full attention, whether you're being warm, and whether you're matching the other person's pace. Often the fix is one of the three.

# Email does not carry tone
@src Kruger, Epley, Parker & Ng, JPSP, 2005

You may have sent a joke by email that landed badly. In 2005 Justin Kruger and his colleagues tested how well people convey tone in writing. Participants wrote messages that were either serious or sarcastic, and sent them to a partner, who judged the tone. The senders were confident that the tone would come across. The partners who read the messages got it right only about 56 percent of the time, not much better than chance.

The senders knew what they meant and could "hear" the tone in their heads, so they assumed it was in the text. It wasn't. It's the same curse of knowledge that shows up in tapping a tune. In writing, we lose the face and the voice, so we have to say more, with a clear word or an emoji or a plain sentence like, "I'm joking."

# The voice makes you human
@src Schroeder & Epley, Psychological Science, 2015

In 2015 Juliana Schroeder and Nicholas Epley had job candidates give a short pitch about why they should be hired. Evaluators either read a transcript of the pitch or heard the recording, and then rated the candidates. The people who heard the voice rated the candidates as more thoughtful and intelligent, and were more likely to want to hire them, even though the words were identical.

The researchers think that a voice carries signs of a mind, such as the pauses, the pitch and the emphasis, that text lacks. It's why a phone call can settle a disagreement that a long email thread couldn't, and why a hard message is often better delivered out loud. Writing is efficient, but a voice makes you seem like a person.

# Four seconds of silence
@src Koudenburg, Postmes & Gordijn, 2011

In 2011 Namkje Koudenburg and her colleagues ran a clever experiment. People watched a six-minute video of three people chatting. In one version the talk flowed. In the other, there was a single silence of four seconds in the middle. Most viewers didn't consciously notice the silence, yet those who saw it reported feeling less sure of themselves and more rejected and left out than those who saw the smooth version.

Four seconds isn't long, but in conversation it seems endless. The finding shows how closely we monitor the rhythm of talk, and how much we read into a gap. A silence seems to say, "We have nothing in common." It's useful to know when you're the one who feels awkward. The gap is probably more noticeable to you than to anyone else, and a simple question can fill it.

# Celebrating good news
@src Gable, Reis, Impett & Asher, JPSP, 2004

How do you respond when a friend tells you about a promotion? In 2004 Shelly Gable and her colleagues described four ways to react. Active-constructive responding is enthusiastic and curious: "That's wonderful! Tell me what happened." Passive-constructive is a quiet "That's nice." Active-destructive points out the downside, and passive-destructive ignores the news.

Couples in whom the partners responded actively and constructively to each other's good news were happier, more committed and more likely to stay together. It mattered more than how they responded to bad news. It's easy to be supportive in a crisis. The test of a relationship may be how well people share in each other's joy. Ask a question, smile and let the person tell the story.

# Turning toward each other
@src Gottman, The Relationship Cure, 2001

The psychologist John Gottman has watched thousands of couples talk in his lab in Seattle, and he noticed a pattern in small moments. A partner makes a "bid" for connection, which might be a comment about a bird outside the window, a sigh, or a request to look at something. The other can turn toward the bid, by answering or showing interest, or turn away by ignoring it.

In one study, newlyweds who were still together six years later had turned toward each other's bids about 86 percent of the time. Those who had divorced had done it only about 33 percent of the time. The bids are tiny and everyday, so love is built or worn away in small moments. Noticing a bid and answering it, even briefly, is one of the simplest and most powerful things you can do for a relationship.

# The four horsemen
@src Gottman, Why Marriages Succeed or Fail, 1994
@conf mixed

Gottman gave names to four habits that he found in couples who were heading for trouble: criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling. Criticism attacks the person and not the behavior. Contempt is the worst, with eye rolling, sarcasm and mockery that say, "I'm better than you." Defensiveness is answering a complaint with excuses or a counterattack. Stonewalling is shutting down and withdrawing.

He said that contempt was the best predictor of divorce. His claims of predicting divorce with very high accuracy have been criticized, since they were based on fitting models to past data, and the studies were partly in one setting. The patterns themselves ring true to many people, and therapists use them. Their antidotes are easy to remember. Complain about the behavior, show respect and appreciation, take responsibility, and ask for a break instead of going silent.

# Starting a hard conversation
@src Carrère & Gottman, 1999

Gottman and his colleagues found that how a hard conversation starts tends to predict how it will end. If a person opens with blame, as in "You never help around here," the other person usually becomes defensive and the talk goes downhill. If they open gently, by describing how they feel about a specific situation and what they need, the conversation tends to go better.

He calls it a soft start. A harsh start might be, "You're so lazy, you never do the dishes." A soft one is, "I'm feeling overwhelmed with the kitchen, and I'd really appreciate your help tonight." The second states a feeling and a request and leaves out the attack. It doesn't guarantee a good outcome, but it makes one much more likely. The first thirty seconds of a difficult talk can set the tone for the whole of it.

# What makes an apology work
@src Lewicki, Polin & Lount, 2016

What makes someone feel that an apology was real? In 2016 Roy Lewicki, Beth Polin and Robert Lount asked people which elements mattered. They identified six: an expression of regret, an explanation of what went wrong, an acknowledgment of responsibility, a declaration of repentance, an offer of repair, and a request for forgiveness. The more elements an apology had, the better it worked.

The most important was acknowledging responsibility, as in "It was my fault," and the second was offering to repair the damage. Asking for forgiveness ranked last, and the authors say that it's the part to leave out if you have to. A weak apology, such as "I'm sorry if you were offended," shifts the blame to the other person. A good one is specific, takes ownership and promises to make it right. The best apologies are short and plain. "I'm sorry I was late and didn't call. That was inconsiderate, and I'll text you next time."

# Asking for advice
@src Brooks, Gino & Schweitzer, Management Science, 2015

Many people hesitate to ask for advice because they fear looking incompetent. In 2015 Alison Wood Brooks, Francesca Gino and Maurice Schweitzer tested the idea in a series of experiments. Participants who asked for advice on a task were seen by their partners as more competent, not less. The effect was strongest when the task was difficult.

Asking for advice flatters the other person and shows that you have the judgment to know what you don't know. It also pulls them into your project. So the fear of looking foolish is a mistake in most cases. If you're unsure and you've a person who knows more, ask. Just do it in a way that shows you've thought about the problem and aren't handing it off.

# The favor that makes friends
@src Jecker & Landy, 1969

Benjamin Franklin wrote that when he wanted to win over a rival in the Pennsylvania legislature, he didn't do him a favor. He asked to borrow a rare book. The man lent it, and from then on he was friendly. Franklin concluded that the person who has once done you a kindness will be more ready to do you another than someone whom you have yourself obliged.

In 1969 Jon Jecker and David Landy tested the idea. They gave participants a sum of money in an experiment, and afterward some were asked by the experimenter to return it as a favor. These people liked the experimenter more than those who weren't asked. It seems that when we do something kind for someone, we reason that we must like them. So don't be afraid to ask a small favor, since it may be taken as a compliment.

# Compliments land better than you think
@src Boothby & Bohns, PSPB, 2021

You probably have thought of a nice thing to say to someone and kept it to yourself. In 2021 Erica Boothby and Vanessa Bohns asked people to give sincere compliments to others, and had both sides rate how it felt. The people giving compliments consistently underestimated how good the compliment would make the receiver feel, and overestimated how awkward it would be.

We focus on our own discomfort at saying it, and we don't see how rare and welcome it is to hear something kind. Related work on thank-you letters found the same: people underestimate how pleased the recipient will be. So the advice is simple. If you think something good about someone, tell them. It costs you a moment of awkwardness, and gives them something that they may remember for a long time.

# Writing a thank you
@src Kumar & Epley, Psychological Science, 2018

In 2018 Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley asked people to write a short gratitude letter to someone who had helped them, and then rate how they expected the recipient to feel. Later the researchers asked the recipients how they did feel. The senders consistently underestimated how happy and surprised the recipients were, and overestimated how awkward they'd feel.

Writers worry about their prose, and the readers don't care about that. What they care about is that someone took the time to say it. A good thank-you note names what the person did, and how it affected you. "Thank you for staying late on Tuesday. It meant I could make my daughter's recital," beats "Thanks for everything." The letter doesn't need to be long to be remembered.

# A story that lines up two brains
@src Stephens, Silbert & Hasson, PNAS, 2010

In 2010 Greg Stephens, Lauren Silbert and Uri Hasson recorded the brain activity of a woman telling an unrehearsed story, and then scanned the brains of people listening to a recording of it. The listeners' brain activity followed the speaker's, with a short delay, in many areas. The better the listeners understood the story, the more closely their brains matched hers.

Some areas of the listeners' brains even fired a moment before the speaker's did, as if they were predicting what would come next. The researchers called it neural coupling. It suggests that communication is more than sending information. For a moment, two brains are doing something similar, and the more alike they are, the more the message gets across. It's one reason why a good story can make strangers feel as though they've shared an experience.

# Transported by a story
@src Green & Brock, JPSP, 2000

Why does a good story pull you in so deeply? In 2000 Melanie Green and Timothy Brock studied what they called narrative transportation, the feeling of being absorbed in a story, losing track of your surroundings and being swept up in the characters. They found that people who were more transported by a story were more likely to adopt beliefs that were suggested in it, and noticed fewer flaws in the argument.

The finding is a reminder of how powerful stories are as a way of persuading. A statistic may be forgotten, but a story about one person is felt. It also is a caution, since a story can carry a message past our critical thinking. When you tell a story, you can use that power well. When you're listening to one, it's worth asking afterward what was it trying to make you believe.

# What we talk about most
@src Dunbar, Marriott & Duncan, Human Nature, 1997

The evolutionary psychologist Robin Dunbar wondered what people actually talk about. In 1997 he and his colleagues listened in on conversations in places like university cafeterias and trains, and recorded the topics every thirty seconds. About two thirds of the time, the talk was about social matters: who was doing what, who liked whom, relationships and personal experiences. Only a minority was about work, politics or abstract ideas.

Dunbar suggested that language evolved partly as a replacement for the grooming that other primates use to keep their social bonds. Chatting about other people keeps track of who's trustworthy, and helps us to stay close. It means that gossip isn't a failing. It's a core function of language, though it can be cruel. Much of what we call small talk is the work of staying connected.

# Getting people to hear opposing views
@src Yeomans, Minson, Collins, Chen & Gino, 2020

When you disagree with someone, how you phrase your point affects whether they listen. In 2020 Michael Yeomans and his colleagues analyzed thousands of conversations about contested topics and identified what they called conversational receptiveness. It includes acknowledging the other person's points, such as "I see where you're coming from," hedging your claims, as in "I think," and stating areas of agreement, instead of using negative words like "obviously" or "but."

In experiments, people who wrote in a more receptive style were seen as more open and better liked, and their opponents were more willing to talk to them again. The words signal that you're listening, which lowers the other person's defenses. You don't have to give up your position to do it. It's the difference between starting a fight and starting a conversation.

# Ask, don't imagine
@src Eyal, Steffel & Epley, JPSP, 2018

When you want to know what someone is thinking, you can imagine yourself in their position, which is called perspective taking. Or you can ask them, which Tal Eyal, Mary Steffel and Nicholas Epley call perspective getting. In 2018 they ran a series of experiments, including one with married couples, and found that people who tried to take their partner's perspective weren't more accurate than those who didn't, and often were less so. But those who asked were.

The problem with imagining is that you use your own beliefs to fill in the blanks. If you think your partner is irritated, you'll find reasons for it, and be confident, and be wrong. Asking is a simple and underused habit. "What did you think of that?" or "How are you feeling about this?" gets you the information that your imagination can't supply.

# Changing minds one conversation at a time
@src Broockman & Kalla, Science, 2016

In 2016 David Broockman and Joshua Kalla tested whether a conversation could reduce prejudice. Canvassers went door to door in Miami and talked with voters for about ten minutes about transgender people. They didn't lecture. They asked how the voter felt, listened, and invited them to recall a time when they'd been treated badly for being different, and then shared a story of a transgender person.

Voters who had these conversations were more accepting afterward, and the effect lasted for at least three months. Conversations that just gave facts had no effect. The approach, called deep canvassing, works because people are asked to think and feel, instead of being told what to think. The study suggests that listening, storytelling and shared experience can change minds when arguments can't.
