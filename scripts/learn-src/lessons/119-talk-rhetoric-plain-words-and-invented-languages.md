# The method of the midwife
@src Plato, Theaetetus; Socratic method

The ancient Greek philosopher Socrates is said to have taught by asking questions and not by giving answers. His way of talking with people is now called the Socratic method. He would start with something that a person believed, such as what courage is, and ask for a definition. Then he would test it with more questions until the person saw a contradiction, or had to admit that he did not know.

In Plato's dialogue Theaetetus, Socrates compares what he does to the work of a midwife. His mother was one. He says that he cannot himself give birth to wisdom, but he can help others deliver their own ideas, and he can tell whether the offspring is healthy or not. The Greek word for it, maieutic, comes from the word for midwifery.

The method begins with common beliefs and checks whether they agree with one another. It has been changed over the centuries, and versions are used today in law schools and in many classes. Its lesson for conversation is plain. A good question can do more than a statement. When people reach a conclusion through their own reasoning, it tends to stay with them.

# The rule that sarcasm needs a label
@src christianforums.com, 2005; Jerry Schwarz, Usenet, 1983

In 2005 a user of an online forum named Nathan Poe made a remark in a debate about creationism that has become known as Poe's law. Without a winking smiley or some other plain sign of humor, he said, it is impossible to parody an extreme view in a way that no one will mistake for the real thing.

The idea is older than his post. As early as 1983 a user of Usenet, Jerry Schwarz, advised people to avoid sarcasm online, since without the tone of voice and body language of a face to face talk, it was easily misunderstood. He noted that a sideways smile, a colon, a hyphen and a closing parenthesis, was becoming widely used to mark a joke.

The law has a double edge. Satire can be taken as sincere. People who do mean extreme things can also claim afterward that they were only joking, if they get too much criticism. The practical lesson is that in writing, where we lose the voice, the face and the timing, it is more likely than we think that our meaning will be lost, and a sign of our intent may be needed.

# The smiley that was proposed in a message
@series tech
@src Scott Fahlman, Carnegie Mellon University bulletin board, 1982

On September 19, 1982, a computer scientist at Carnegie Mellon University, Scott Fahlman, wrote a message to the university's online bulletin board. People there had been confused by jokes that others took seriously. He proposed that the characters colon, hyphen and closing parenthesis be used, if read sideways, to mark a joke, and colon, hyphen and opening parenthesis to mark a remark that was serious.

The suggestion spread across the early networks. The pictures made from characters came to be called emoticons, from emotion and icon. People had made faces from typed characters before, in one-off uses going back hundreds of years, but Fahlman is credited with the plan of using them as a code for the mood of a message.

In Japan, a different style grew, called kaomoji, which can be read without turning the head. It arose on a network called ASCII NET in 1986. Later the faces were replaced by little pictures, and then by emoji. The need behind them has not changed. Writing leaves out the tone of voice and the expression on a face, and people look for ways to put them back.

# The headline that ends in a question mark
@series talk
@src Ian Betteridge, 2009; Andrew Marr, My Trade, 2004

Betteridge's law of headlines says that any headline that ends in a question mark can be answered by the word no. It is named for Ian Betteridge, a British technology writer, who wrote about it in February 2009. He was commenting on a headline on the website TechCrunch, which asked whether the music service Last.fm had just handed over the listening data of its users to the recording industry.

His reasoning was that journalists use the question form because they suspect the story is not solid, and do not have the sources to say it outright, but they want to run it. If they were confident that the answer was yes, they would say so. By asking, they make no claim for which they can be held to account.

The idea is older than his name for it. A 1991 collection of Murphy's law variants called it Davis's law, and the British editor Andrew Marr made a similar remark in a 2004 book. It is not a law of nature, and some headlines with question marks do have an answer of yes. But as a rule of thumb it is useful. When a headline asks a question, ask why it is not a statement.

# Why a lie costs ten times more to answer
@series think
@src Alberto Brandolini, 2013

In January 2013 an Italian programmer, Alberto Brandolini, was reading Daniel Kahneman's book Thinking, Fast and Slow, and then he watched an Italian television talk show with the former prime minister Silvio Berlusconi and a journalist. He wrote down an observation that has since been called Brandolini's law, or the bullshit asymmetry principle. The amount of energy needed to refute nonsense is an order of magnitude bigger than that needed to produce it.

The point is about the difference in work. A false claim can be made in a sentence. To answer it, one has to find the facts, explain them, and then deal with the fact that many people have already heard the claim and trusted it. The effort is made harder because it can mean challenging one's own community.

The standard example is the false claim that vaccines cause autism. A fraudulent paper in 1998 by Andrew Wakefield said that there was a link with the MMR vaccine. It was retracted and his medical license was revoked. Many studies since have found no link, yet the false idea has had a lasting effect on public health. The law does not say to give up. It says that prevention, such as teaching people to question claims before they spread, is worth more than cure.

# The language made to stop thought
@series words
@src Orwell, Nineteen Eighty-Four, 1949; Politics and the English Language, 1946

In George Orwell's 1949 novel Nineteen Eighty-Four, the ruling Party of Oceania has a language called Newspeak. It is designed to have a smaller vocabulary every year. The aim is to make certain thoughts impossible to express, such as thoughts about personal freedom, and so impossible to think. If a language has no words for an idea, the idea is harder to hold.

In an appendix, The Principles of Newspeak, Orwell explains that it follows most rules of English grammar, but reduces ideas to simple terms. Many of its words are contractions. The Ministry of Truth is Minitrue and the Ministry of Plenty is Miniplenty. Orwell saw the same kind of contractions in the twentieth century, in words such as Gestapo, politburo and Comintern.

From it and from the idea of doublethink came the word doublespeak, though it is not used in the novel. It means language that deliberately hides or reverses the meaning, as in calling layoffs downsizing or bombing servicing the target. Orwell's essay of 1946, Politics and the English Language, made a similar charge. Political speech, he argued, is often designed to make lies sound truthful, and to give an appearance of solidity to pure wind.

# The score that measures how hard a page is
@series words
@src Flesch, 1948; Kincaid et al., U.S. Navy, 1975

How hard is it to read a passage of English? The Austrian-born writer Rudolf Flesch, who campaigned for plain English, devised a formula in 1948 called the Reading Ease score. It uses two things only, the average length of the sentences and the average length of the words. The longer the sentences and the longer the words, the harder the text is said to be.

In 1975 a psychologist, J. Peter Kincaid, and his team did a project for the United States Navy, and turned the formula into a grade level. The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level gives a number that matches a school year in the United States. A score of 8 means that the text should be understood by someone at about the eighth grade. It was first used by the Army to check the difficulty of manuals.

The tests are crude. They count the length of words, but cannot tell whether the words are familiar or whether the ideas are hard. A short sentence full of technical terms may score as easy. Even so, such formulas are built into word processors, and are used for legal documents, insurance policies and health information. They show a simple truth. Short words and short sentences are easier to take in.

# The language that had a million speakers for a moment
@series words
@src Volapük, Johann Martin Schleyer, 1879 and 1880

In 1879 and 1880 a Roman Catholic priest in Baden, Germany, Johann Martin Schleyer, created a language that he called Volapük, which means the language of the world. It was the first major constructed language meant for international use. Its grammar was based mostly on German, and its words mostly on English, with changes. Schleyer shortened and altered many of them until they were hard to recognize.

It spread fast. Conventions were held in 1884 in Friedrichshafen, in 1887 in Munich, and in 1889 in Paris, where the proceedings were held only in Volapük. By 1889 there were an estimated 283 clubs, 25 periodicals and 316 textbooks in 25 languages, and the language claimed nearly a million followers.

Then it collapsed. There were quarrels in the movement, and Schleyer wanted to keep control over changes to the language. In 1887 a Polish doctor, Ludwik Zamenhof, published a language that he called Esperanto, and many members of the Volapük clubs moved to it. A small revival of Volapük came in the 1930s, after reforms by Arie de Jong. It still has a few users. A new language needs not only a good design but a community that can agree.

# The English of eight hundred and fifty words
@series words
@src Ogden, Basic English: A General Introduction, 1930; Ogden & Richards, The Meaning of Meaning, 1923

In 1930 the Cambridge linguist and philosopher Charles Kay Ogden published a book describing Basic English. It was a smaller form of the language with a vocabulary of only 850 words, and a much simplified grammar. His claim was that almost anything could be said with it. A person could learn it in weeks, and use it as a second language for the whole world.

It was based on ideas in a book that he wrote with I. A. Richards in 1923, The Meaning of Meaning. Richards later promoted it in schools in China. After the Allied victory in the Second World War, it gained great publicity as a tool for peace. Ogden was convinced that the world needed a single language, and he thought that it should be English, in either a simple or a full form.

Basic English itself did not take off. But the idea survives. The Voice of America built a simplified broadcast form, called Special English, for news for learners. A form of controlled language, called Simplified Technical English, is used to write aircraft manuals. The belief behind it was that clarity comes from a small, well-chosen set of words.

# The alphabet that a playwright left money for
@series words
@src Shavian alphabet; Ronald Kingsley Read; will of George Bernard Shaw

George Bernard Shaw, the Irish playwright, was a lifelong critic of English spelling. He argued that it wasted time and money, since the same sound could be spelled in many ways, and one spelling could have many sounds. When he died in 1950, his will left money for a new alphabet for English.

A competition was held, and the winning design was the work of Ronald Kingsley Read, a professional sign-writer. It is now called the Shavian alphabet. Shaw had asked for three things. There had to be at least 40 letters, and in the end there were 48. The letters should match the sounds, one to one. And it should not look like the Latin alphabet, so that the new spellings would not seem to be only mistakes.

The letters come in three kinds. Tall ones, with a stroke going up, stand for sounds made without voice, such as p and t. Deep ones, with a stroke going down, are the same letters turned upside down, standing for the voiced sounds such as b and d. Short ones stand for vowels and for sounds such as m and n. The alphabet never caught on, but people still use it. It remains a serious attempt to fix a real problem.

# The man who put America in the dictionary
@series words
@src Noah Webster, 1758 to 1843; An American Dictionary of the English Language, 1828

Noah Webster was born in West Hartford, Connecticut, in 1758, and graduated from Yale in 1778. He studied law and passed the bar, but could not find work as a lawyer, so he opened a private school. To teach his pupils he wrote books, and his Blue-Backed Speller became the main book from which American children learned to read and spell.

He believed that the new nation should have its own culture and its own language, free of English models. In 1828 he published An American Dictionary of the English Language, which is the origin of the modern Merriam-Webster dictionaries. It recorded the words that Americans used, and many of the spellings he preferred, such as color without the u and center with the r before the e. Some of his other proposals, such as tung for tongue, did not catch on.

In 1793 Alexander Hamilton had recruited him to come to New York and edit a newspaper for the Federalist Party. He was a writer of political essays as well as books for schools. His work helped to make a standard American English, and to fix the differences in spelling from British English that persist.

# The towers that sent news faster than a horse
@series tech
@src Chappe telegraph, 1792; optical telegraph systems

Before the electric telegraph, there was a telegraph made of towers. In 1792 a Frenchman, Claude Chappe, invented a system of signals that could be seen from far away. Each station had a tall mast with movable arms on top, which could be set in many positions. A position stood for a letter, a number or a code word. It was called a semaphore telegraph.

Towers were built within sight of each other, five to twenty miles apart. An operator at each tower looked at the neighboring one through a telescope. When the arms began to move, spelling out a message, he copied the positions on his own tower, which passed the message on to the next. Chappe used the word télégraphe for the machine, and so gave the English language the word telegraph.

It was much faster than a rider on a horse, and in the long run it cost less. France built a network of lines. But it could be used only in daylight and fair weather, since the stations had to be within sight of each other. About fifty years later, the electric telegraph replaced it, because it was cheaper, faster and more private. The towers show how a simple code and a chain of watchers can carry a message across a country.

# A language of one hundred and twenty words
@series words
@src Sonja Lang, Toki Pona: The Language of Good, 2014; Toki Pona Dictionary, 2021

Toki Pona is a language created by a Canadian translator, Sonja Lang. She first published drafts online in 2001, and the complete form in a 2014 book, Toki Pona: The Language of Good. The name means good language. It was designed to be as small and simple as possible, with a purpose of simplifying her own thoughts.

The language has only 14 sounds, and its first book presented about 120 words. A dictionary in 2021 lists 137 essential words, and a few more that are less used. Words are combined to express more. A friend may be a good person, and a teacher a person who gives knowledge. It is partly inspired by Taoist philosophy, and by the idea, from the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, that a language shapes thought. A small vocabulary is meant to make the speaker concentrate on the simple and positive things.

It was not intended as a world language. But it has a community of speakers, and it has been used for art and for translation. In 2024 a third book appeared, a translation of The Wonderful Wizard of Oz. Toki Pona is an experiment in whether a language can be changed to change how a person thinks, and it tests how much can be said with almost nothing.

# The language made for a television series
@series words
@src David J. Peterson; HBO; Language Creation Society

In the novels by George R. R. Martin on which the television series Game of Thrones is based, the nomadic horse lords called the Dothraki speak their own language. But the books gave only a few words and phrases. When the series was made, the producers at HBO wanted a real language. They turned to the Language Creation Society, and more than 30 people who invented languages applied.

The job went to David J. Peterson, who got the part by building from Martin's words. He delivered over 1,700 words before filming began. He took ideas from several languages, including Estonian, Inuktitut, Turkish, Russian and Swahili. By September 2011 the language had 3,163 words, though not all were public. He went on to create the Valyrian languages, and many others for film.

The result was well received. The magazine The Economist called the Dothraki and Valyrian languages the most convincing fictional tongues since Elvish. The effect went beyond the screen. In 2012, 146 newborn girls in the United States were given the name Khaleesi, the Dothraki word for the wife of a ruler. A made-up language can give a world a feel of being real, and sometimes become real for its fans.

# The sound law that showed words obey rules
@series words
@src Rask, 1818; Grimm, Deutsche Grammatik, 1822; Verner, 1877

Why does the Latin word pater look like the English word father, and the Latin pes like our foot? The Latin p became an f in the Germanic languages, and the same thing happens again and again. Latin tres goes with English three, and Latin cornu with English horn. Such regular matches are called Grimm's law, after the German scholar Jacob Grimm.

The first to note the pattern was Friedrich von Schlegel in 1806, who saw that Latin p matches Germanic f. In 1818 a Danish scholar, Rasmus Rask, extended it to Sanskrit and Greek and the whole range of consonants involved. In 1822 Grimm laid it out in his book Deutsche Grammatik, and extended it to German. His name stuck.

It was the first time that scholars saw that sound change is systematic and not random. That gave historical linguistics a method. Grimm himself saw many exceptions, and they puzzled scholars for decades. Then in 1877 another Dane, Karl Verner, showed that they too followed a rule, which depended on where the stress fell in the word. The law is why a person who knows how sounds shift can trace words back to a shared ancestor.

# The judge who saw that Sanskrit was a cousin of Greek
@series history
@src Sir William Jones, 1746 to 1794; Asiatic Society, 1784

William Jones was born in London in 1746, and was a gifted student of languages from an early age. His father, also William Jones, was a Welsh mathematician who introduced the use of the symbol pi. The younger man learned Greek, Latin, Persian, Arabic, Hebrew and the basics of Chinese writing, and by the end of his life he knew eight languages with great thoroughness.

He became a judge in Calcutta, then the center of British rule in India, and in 1784 he founded the Asiatic Society there to study the culture of Asia. He studied Sanskrit, the ancient language of India. In a talk in 1786 he said that Sanskrit was more perfect than Greek and more copious than Latin, and that it had a strong likeness to both, in roots and in grammar, which could not be by chance.

He argued that the three had come from a common source, which perhaps no longer existed. He was not the first to see the link. But his clear statement helped to start the study of the Indo-European family of languages. He died in Calcutta in 1794, aged 47. His idea changed how people think about the languages of Europe and of India, and their shared history.

# The fable written in a language no one has heard
@series words
@src Schleicher, 1868; revisions by Hirt, Lehmann and Zgusta, Adams, and Kortlandt

In 1868 the German linguist August Schleicher did something no one had done. He wrote a story in Proto-Indo-European, the ancestor of English, Latin, Greek, Sanskrit and many other languages, which was spoken thousands of years ago and never written down. He built it from what the descendant languages have in common. He called it The Sheep and the Horses.

In the fable a sheep with no wool sees horses, one pulling a heavy wagon, one carrying a big load, and one carrying a man swiftly. The sheep says that its heart pains it to see a man driving horses. The horses reply that their hearts pain them too, when they see that a man, the master, makes the wool of the sheep into a warm garment, and the sheep have none. On hearing this the sheep ran off into the plain.

Scholars have rewritten the fable several times, as ideas about how the language sounded have changed. There are versions by Hermann Hirt in 1939, by Winfred Lehmann and Ladislav Zgusta in 1979, by Douglas Q. Adams in 1997, and by Frederik Kortlandt in 2007. Placing them side by side shows how much the reconstruction has changed in 150 years, and how much is a matter of careful guesswork.
