# The superstitious pigeons
@src Skinner, Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1948

In 1948 B. F. Skinner put hungry pigeons in a box. A feeder dropped a little food every fifteen seconds, regardless of what the birds did. There was nothing that the pigeons could do to get the food. Skinner expected them to ignore it. Instead, most of the birds developed a habit of their own. One turned counterclockwise in the cage. Another thrust its head into a corner. A third swung its head back and forth like a pendulum.

Skinner's explanation was that whatever the bird happened to be doing when the food arrived was accidentally rewarded, so it did it more, and so was more likely to be doing it when the next food came. He called it superstition. People have similar habits, such as wearing lucky socks. Later researchers offered other explanations, but the experiment is a vivid example of how we may see a link between our action and an outcome that isn't there.

# The sickness that taught a rat
@src Garcia & Koelling, Psychonomic Science, 1966

In the 1950s John Garcia noticed that rats exposed to radiation, which made them sick, later avoided the plastic water bottles that they had used in the chamber. It seemed to contradict the standard theory of conditioning, which said that learning needed the cause and the effect to come close together in time. The nausea came hours after the drinking.

In 1966 Garcia and Robert Koelling tested it. Rats drank flavored water, or water with lights and clicks. Then some were given a shock to the feet, and others were made sick. The rats that were made sick learned to avoid the taste, but not the lights and clicks. The rats that were shocked learned to avoid the lights and clicks, but not the taste. Animals are prepared to link nausea with food. It's why one bad meal can put you off a food for life.

# The chimpanzee who saw the answer
@src Köhler, The Mentality of Apes, 1917

During the First World War, the German psychologist Wolfgang Köhler was stuck on the island of Tenerife, in the Canary Islands, where he ran a research station for apes. He gave problems to chimpanzees. A banana was hung from the ceiling, out of reach, and boxes were scattered about the room. A chimpanzee named Sultan, after trying to jump, sat for a while, and then suddenly got up, stacked the boxes and climbed up to get the fruit.

Köhler said that the animal had insight. He didn't solve the problem by trial and error, but by seeing the relationship between the objects, and the goal, all at once. Sultan also joined two sticks together to make a pole long enough to reach a banana outside the cage. The work challenged the view that animals learn only through blind trial and error. The word aha has been used for such moments of insight ever since.

# The cat in the puzzle box
@src Thorndike, Animal Intelligence, 1898

In 1898 the American psychologist Edward Thorndike, then a graduate student, put hungry cats in boxes with a latch. To get out, and to reach a piece of fish outside, the cat had to pull a string or press a lever. At first, they scratched and clawed, and by accident, hit the right device. Each time, he timed how long it took them to escape.

Over repeated trials, the times fell, slowly and gradually, without any sudden jump. Thorndike concluded that the cats were not reasoning. They were learning to repeat the behavior that led to a good result. He called it the law of effect. Actions that are followed by satisfying consequences become more likely, and ones followed by discomfort become less likely. The idea was the basis of Skinner's later work on operant conditioning.

# The goslings that followed a man
@src Lorenz, Journal für Ornithologie, 1935

In the 1930s the Austrian zoologist Konrad Lorenz divided a clutch of goose eggs. One half hatched with their mother. The other half were put in an incubator, and he was the first thing they saw when they hatched. The goslings followed him around, as they would have followed their mother, and they ignored real geese. They would line up behind him, and even swim after him.

Lorenz called this imprinting. It occurs in a short sensitive period soon after birth, and seems to be irreversible. The young animal learns who is its parent, and also, later, what species to mate with. He shared the Nobel Prize in 1973. Later work showed that imprinting is more flexible than he thought. The research has influenced the study of human attachment, though human babies are far less rigid.

# The filter in the mind
@src Broadbent, Perception and Communication, 1958

In the 1950s Donald Broadbent, a British psychologist, was working on a practical problem. Air traffic controllers and pilots had to listen to several messages at once, and he wanted to understand why they missed things. He tested people by playing different digits to each ear at the same time, and asking them what they heard.

People could repeat all the digits from one ear and then the other, but not mix them. Broadbent proposed that attention works like a filter. Many sensory signals reach the brain, but only one channel is let through for deeper processing, chosen by physical features such as the ear it comes from. The others are blocked. The model was soon found to be too simple, but it opened the study of attention.

# The leaky filter
@src Treisman, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1960

Broadbent had proposed that unattended messages are blocked. In 1960 Anne Treisman tested it by a method called shadowing. People wore headphones, and heard a different message in each ear. They were told to repeat aloud the message in one ear, and ignore the other. In one experiment, a story switched ears in the middle of a sentence.

Some people followed the meaning of the story to the wrong ear, for a word or two, though they were meant to ignore it. So the unattended message was not completely blocked. Treisman proposed that the filter works like a volume control. It turns down the unattended channel, but doesn't switch it off. Important words, such as your own name, can get through. It's a model that explains the cocktail party effect.

# The memory that vanishes in a second
@src Sperling, Psychological Monographs, 1960

In 1960 George Sperling, a graduate student, flashed a grid of twelve letters, in three rows of four, on a screen for a twentieth of a second. When he asked people to recall as many as they could, they could say only about four or five. Yet they insisted that they had seen all of them, and that the letters faded as they tried to name them.

He then used a trick. After the grid disappeared, he played a tone, high, middle or low, to signal which row to report. People could then report nearly all four letters in the row, whichever was asked. So they must have had all twelve letters available in memory, for a moment. The memory faded within about a second. Sperling called it iconic memory. It's a brief visual store that precedes attention.

# How fast we forget
@src Peterson & Peterson, Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1959

In 1959 Lloyd Peterson and Margaret Peterson, at Indiana University, tested how long a small amount of information stays in memory without being repeated. People were given a three-letter nonsense syllable, such as CHJ. Then they were asked to count backward by threes from a number, to stop them rehearsing it. After a delay, they were asked to recall the syllable.

After three seconds, they remembered about eighty percent. After eighteen seconds, they remembered only about ten percent. It seemed that information in short-term memory is lost in about twenty seconds, if it isn't rehearsed. Later work suggested that it was not simply decay but interference from the counting. The study led to the idea of short-term memory as a limited store, and showed why we have to repeat a phone number to ourselves.

# Reading as a burglar
@src Anderson & Pichert, Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 1978

In 1978 Richard Anderson and James Pichert had students read a story about two boys who skip school and go to the home of one of them. The house is described in detail, with its features, such as a fireplace, a new color television, a leaky roof and a damp basement. Some of the students were told to read it as a prospective home buyer, and others as a burglar.

Later, they were asked to recall the story. The ones who read as home buyers remembered the details that would matter to a buyer, such as the leaky roof. The burglars remembered details such as the television. Then, the researchers asked each of them to switch to the other viewpoint, and recall again. They came up with new details that they hadn't mentioned before. What we remember depends on the perspective we took when we read.

# The paragraph that made no sense
@src Bransford & Johnson, Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 1972

In 1972 John Bransford and Marcia Johnson gave people a short passage that described a procedure in dull, general terms. It said that you should first arrange things into different groups, that one pile might be enough if there isn't much to do, and that you may have to go somewhere else if you lack the facilities. It never said what the things were. They asked people to recall it later.

People did poorly, and rated it as hard to understand. But another group was told that the title was Washing Clothes. These people understood it, and remembered about twice as much. Once you know the context, every sentence makes sense. The study showed that comprehension depends on having a framework. It's also a good reason to give the big picture before details, when you teach.

# The words that changed a drawing
@src Carmichael, Hogan & Walter, Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1932

In 1932 Leonard Carmichael and his colleagues showed people ambiguous line drawings, and read a word to describe each one. One group heard one label, and another group heard a different label for the same figure. For example, a shape like two circles joined by a line was called eyeglasses by one group, and dumbbells by the other. Afterward, they were asked to redraw them from memory.

Their drawings tended to be distorted toward the label. The ones who heard eyeglasses drew something more like glasses, and the ones who heard dumbbells drew something more like weights. The experiment suggests that memory is altered by the words that we use. It was an early demonstration of how language can shape what we recall. Later work has found that the effect is real, though it may be smaller than the original suggested.

# The office that wasn't there
@src Brewer & Treyens, Cognitive Psychology, 1981

In 1981 William Brewer and Janet Treyens asked students to wait for about thirty-five seconds in a room that they were told was the experimenter's office, before the experiment began. It was a normal graduate student's office, with a desk, a typewriter, a coffee pot and shelves. But it had no books. Then they were taken to another room, and asked to write down everything that they could remember.

Almost a third of them recalled books, though there were none. They remembered the desk and the chair, which fit with their idea of an office. They were less likely to recall odd items, such as a skull, or a piece of wood. The mind seems to fill gaps with what it expects to see. It's an example of a schema, a mental framework that guides perception and memory, and sometimes leads to errors.

# Does arousal need a label?
@src Schachter & Singer, Psychological Review, 1962
@conf contested

In 1962 Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer injected volunteers with adrenaline, which raises the heart rate and makes the hands tremble. Some were told what to expect. Others were told nothing. Then each was placed in a room with a confederate, who behaved either in a joyful, silly manner or in an angry one. The researchers observed how the volunteers acted, and asked how they felt.

Those who didn't know why their bodies were aroused were more influenced by the confederate, and tended to take on the mood. The authors concluded that emotion has two parts, a physical arousal and a mental label based on the situation. It was one of the best-known studies in psychology. But it has been criticized, and attempts to repeat it have failed to find the pattern. The theory is probably too simple. But the idea that context shapes how we read our own feelings has held up.
