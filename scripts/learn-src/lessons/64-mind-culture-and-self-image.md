# One word, warm or cold
@src Asch, Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1946; Kelley, Journal of Personality, 1950

In 1946 Solomon Asch read people a list of traits describing an imaginary person: intelligent, skillful, industrious, warm, determined, practical, cautious. Then he asked them to say what the person was like. A second group heard the same list, with one word changed. The word warm was replaced by cold.

The impressions were quite different. The warm person was seen as generous, happy and good-natured. The cold person was seen as unsympathetic and ungenerous. Changing only one word altered the meaning of all the others. Asch called warm and cold central traits. In 1950 Harold Kelley showed that the effect works with real people. Students who were told that a guest lecturer was warm took part more and liked him more than those who were told he was cold, though the lecture was the same.

# Why my mistake is the situation's fault
@src Jones & Nisbett, 1971

If someone cuts in front of you in traffic, you may think that they're a rude person. If you cut in front of someone yourself, you probably think that you were in a hurry, or didn't see them. In 1971 Edward Jones and Richard Nisbett noted this pattern, and called it the actor-observer difference. When we explain our own behavior, we tend to look at the situation. When we explain the behavior of others, we tend to look at the person.

The reason may be that we are aware of the pressures that we face, and that we see only other people's actions. We know our own history of good intentions. The bias works in both directions. It causes people to be too harsh on others and too easy on themselves. A useful habit is to give other people the same explanation that you would give yourself, when you've made the same mistake.

# Setting yourself up to fail
@src Berglas & Jones, JPSP, 1978

In 1978 Steven Berglas and Edward Jones invited students to take a test of intellectual ability. After the first part, some of the students were told that they'd done very well, but on questions that had no right answer, so that they could not understand why. Then they were offered a choice of two drugs before the second part, one said to improve performance, and the other said to impair it.

The students who had been given unexplained success, and were afraid that they couldn't repeat it, often chose the drug that would impair performance. If they failed, they could blame the drug. This is self-handicapping. We do it by not studying before a test, or by staying up late, so that a poor result has an excuse. It protects the ego in the short term, and costs performance in the long term.

# We won, they lost
@src Cialdini et al., JPSP, 1976

In 1976 Robert Cialdini and his colleagues counted the number of students at seven American universities who wore school colors or insignia on the Mondays after football games. They found more people wearing them after a win than after a loss. They also asked students about the results of the games. After a win, they said we won. After a loss, they said they lost.

The researchers called it basking in reflected glory. We like to be connected with success, and to put distance between ourselves and failure, even when we have had nothing to do with the result. It's not limited to sport. People mention famous people they know, or the success of their schools. The pattern shows how much of our self-image depends on the groups we belong to.

# The big fish in the little pond
@src Marsh & Hau, American Psychologist, 2003

Suppose that a student goes from an ordinary school to one that selects only the highest achievers. You would expect their confidence to rise, since they are now with brilliant people. In fact, the opposite is more likely. In the 1980s the psychologist Herbert Marsh described the big-fish-little-pond effect. Students with the same ability have a lower academic self-concept in a school of high achievers than in one of average achievers.

In 2003 Marsh and Kit-Tai Hau studied about a hundred thousand students from twenty-six countries. The effect appeared in every one. Students compare themselves with their classmates, and not with the world. It suggests that going to a more selective school may harm confidence, even while it helps the education. It's a reminder that how we feel about our abilities depends on who we compare ourselves with.

# Warmth and competence
@src Fiske, Cuddy, Glick & Xu, JPSP, 2002

How do people judge groups? In 2002 Susan Fiske and her colleagues proposed that we judge on two dimensions. One is warmth, whether the group has good or bad intentions. The other is competence, whether it has the ability to carry them out. They tested it with surveys in several countries.

The results sorted groups into four types. Groups that are seen as warm and competent, such as one's own group, and close allies, get admiration. Groups seen as warm but not competent, such as elderly people, get pity. Cold but competent groups, such as rich people or some immigrant groups, get envy. Cold and incompetent ones, such as homeless people, get contempt. The model shows that prejudice is not a single feeling, and that different stereotypes lead to different emotions and actions.

# Two ideas of the self
@src Markus & Kitayama, Psychological Review, 1991

Do people everywhere think of themselves in the same way? In 1991 Hazel Markus and Shinobu Kitayama argued that they don't. In many Western countries, people tend to see themselves as independent. The self is a separate individual, with its own traits, abilities and goals, and a good person is true to themselves and stands out.

In many East Asian countries, and in many other cultures, people tend to see themselves as interdependent. The self is defined by relationships, roles and duties to others, and a good person fits in and adjusts. It's a matter of degree, and people in every culture have both. The difference affects what makes people happy, how they handle conflict, and how they explain events. It also shows that much of what we call human nature depends on where we grew up.

# The fish tank
@src Masuda & Nisbett, JPSP, 2001

In 2001 Takahiko Masuda and Richard Nisbett showed Japanese and American students short animated underwater scenes. Each had a large fish in the foreground, and other fish, plants and rocks. They were asked to describe what they had seen.

The Americans started by describing the large fish, and its features. The Japanese were more likely to start with the setting, saying something like there was a pond. They also mentioned more of the background and the relationships between objects. In later tests, the Japanese were better at remembering the background, and their memory of the main object was affected by a change of background. It suggests that culture may shape what people notice, with Westerners focusing on objects, and East Asians on context.

# Rice and wheat
@src Talhelm et al., Science, 2014

Farming rice and farming wheat are very different. Rice paddies need a lot of work, and an irrigation system shared among neighbors. People must cooperate, and share the water and the labor. Wheat is grown on dry land, with much less work, and farmers can work more independently.

In 2014 Thomas Talhelm and his colleagues tested people in six regions of China, in provinces that grow rice and ones that grow wheat. In the rice provinces, people were more likely to show interdependent thinking. They tended to draw themselves about the same size as their friends, and to see objects in terms of relationships. People in wheat provinces were more individualist. Divorce was also higher in wheat areas. The results suggest that culture can be shaped over centuries by the way that people make a living.

# The people we study
@src Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan, Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 2010

In 2010 Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine and Ara Norenzayan pointed out a problem. Most psychological research is done on people from Western, educated, industrialized, rich and democratic societies. They gave this the acronym WEIRD. Many studies are done on college students in the United States, who are not typical of the world.

They reviewed cross-cultural studies and found that WEIRD people are often outliers. In the Müller-Lyer illusion, two lines of equal length look different because of the arrowheads at the ends. Americans are strongly fooled, while some groups, such as the San people of the Kalahari, are hardly fooled at all. The same is true for fairness, moral reasoning and thinking style. It means that findings about human nature may only be about a small part of humanity.

# Reading faces across cultures
@src Jack, Garrod, Yu, Caldara & Schyns, PNAS, 2012
@conf mixed

Do people everywhere read emotions in the same way from faces? In 2012 Rachael Jack and her colleagues at the University of Glasgow tested Western Caucasian and East Asian volunteers. The volunteers watched computer-generated faces whose muscles moved randomly, and said which emotion they saw in them. The researchers then worked out what pattern of movement each person associated with each emotion.

The two groups had different mental pictures of the emotions. Westerners used the mouth and the eyebrows. East Asians put more emphasis on the eyes, particularly the way that they look. The authors suggested that the universal six expressions are not as universal as supposed. Others have argued that the basic emotions are recognized well across cultures, and that the differences are about the details. The debate continues.

# Lost wallets
@src Cohn, Maréchal, Tannenbaum & Zünd, Science, 2019

How honest are people? In 2019 Alain Cohn and his colleagues ran an experiment in forty countries. They handed in more than seventeen thousand wallets to people working in places such as banks, hotels and post offices, saying that someone had left it behind. Each wallet had a business card, so that it could be returned. Some wallets held no money, and others held the equivalent of about thirteen dollars.

In most countries, the wallets with money were returned more often than those without. In the countries overall, around forty percent of wallets with no money were reported, and over half of the ones with money. It's the opposite of what economists would predict, since keeping more money is more tempting. The researchers suggested that people care about not being thieves, and that taking more makes them feel more like one. Honesty is often driven by self-image.

# Gender similarities
@src Hyde, American Psychologist, 2005

Popular books say that men and women are very different in the way that they think and behave. In 2005 Janet Hyde reviewed forty-six reviews of research that had compared the sexes on a range of traits, including math ability, language, aggression, self-esteem and many others. She looked at the size of the differences.

She found that most of the differences were small or close to zero. About three-quarters were in this range. A few were larger, such as throwing distance and some aspects of sexuality, and some were moderate, such as aggression. She called it the gender similarities hypothesis. The two sexes overlap greatly on most psychological traits. A difference between averages tells little about any one person, and it is not a reason to expect a person to be a certain way.
