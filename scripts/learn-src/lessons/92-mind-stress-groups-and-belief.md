# The baboon troop that turned peaceful
@conf mixed
@src Sapolsky & Share, PLoS Biology, 2004

In the 1980s the neuroscientist Robert Sapolsky was studying a troop of wild olive baboons in Kenya, which he called the Forest Troop. Baboon troops are usually harsh places, where males fight for rank and take out their stress on those below them. Then the most aggressive males in this troop died, after eating food from a garbage dump. The females and the less aggressive males, who had not gone to the dump, were left.

Over the next years the troop became unusually relaxed. The remaining males groomed the females more, the hierarchy was less harsh, and their stress hormone levels were lower than in neighboring troops. Even more striking, males who joined later from other troops took up the same relaxed habits. The authors described it as a culture that was passed on. It was one troop, so it is a case and not a rule, but it shows how much a social setting can shape what a primate does.

# The ratio that wasn't
@conf mixed
@src Fredrickson & Losada, American Psychologist, 2005; Brown, Sokal & Friedman, 2013

In 2005 the psychologist Barbara Fredrickson and Marcial Losada reported a number that sounded like a rule for a good life. People who had at least 2.9013 positive feelings for each negative one, they said, flourished, and below that they languished. The number was said to come from a mathematical model borrowed from the study of fluid flow, called the Lorenz equations, and it spread through books, talks and corporate training.

In 2013 Nicholas Brown, Alan Sokal and Harris Friedman examined the mathematics, and found no reason to use equations about fluids to describe emotions. Fredrickson and Losada withdrew the modeling part of their paper. Fredrickson still holds that positive emotions matter, and points to other studies. But the exact ratio has no basis. The episode is a lesson that a precise number can sound more scientific than the evidence behind it.

# Does the body know before you do?
@series brain
@conf mixed
@src Bechara, Damasio, Tranel & Damasio, Science, 1997

In the Iowa Gambling Task, a player picks cards from four decks, and wins or loses money. Two of the decks give big wins but even bigger losses, and two give small wins and smaller losses, so the first pair is a bad bet. In 1997 Antoine Bechara, Antonio Damasio and their colleagues tested healthy volunteers, and patients with damage to the front of the brain who had made poor decisions in real life. They also measured the sweat on the players' skin.

Healthy players began to prefer the good decks before they could say which were good, and showed a sweat response just before reaching for a bad deck. The patients never developed that response, and kept choosing the bad decks even after they knew the better strategy. It suggested that the body gives hunches before conscious knowledge does. Later, Maia and McClelland argued that people knew more than the early tests showed, and the debate continues.

# Does a correction make a false belief stronger?
@series think
@conf mixed
@src Nyhan & Reifler, Political Behavior, 2010; Wood & Porter, 2019

Some people fear that correcting a false belief only makes it stronger. In 2010 Brendan Nyhan and Jason Reifler gave people news stories that contained a false claim, followed by a correction, such as the claim that weapons of mass destruction had been found in Iraq. For some people whose politics favored the claim, the correction seemed to make them believe it more. It was called the backfire effect, and it was widely repeated.

In 2019 Thomas Wood and Ethan Porter tested 52 issues, in five studies with more than ten thousand people. They did not find a correction that caused backfire, even on divisive issues. People who saw the facts moved toward them, even when they disliked them. Backfire may happen in some cases, but it does not look like the rule. It seems to be worth correcting a false claim.

# Why good news sticks
@src Sharot, Korn & Dolan, Nature Neuroscience, 2011

Most people think that they are less likely than average to suffer a divorce, a disease or a theft. In 2011 Tali Sharot and her colleagues set out to see how that belief survives the facts. They asked people to estimate their own chances of eighty bad events, such as getting cancer or having a car stolen. Then they showed them the true average risk, and asked again, while a scanner recorded their brains.

People changed their estimates more when the truth was better than they had feared than when it was worse. Parts of the prefrontal cortex tracked the surprises about good news well. The most optimistic people tracked the surprises about bad news less. Later work found that disrupting a region of the right frontal cortex with magnetic stimulation reduced the difference. So optimism is not only a feeling. It is partly a way of updating.

# The same film, a different soundtrack
@src Speisman, Lazarus, Mordkoff & Davison, J. Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1964

How much stress a scene causes depends on more than the scene. In the early 1960s Richard Lazarus and his colleagues showed volunteers a disturbing film, called Subincision, about a crude surgical ritual among Aboriginal Australians. The picture was always the same, but the sound was changed. One version had a soundtrack that dwelt on the pain and the danger. Another denied the pain, and said that the people were happy. A third gave a calm anthropological commentary.

The viewers' heart rates and skin responses were measured, and they also rated how they felt. The soundtrack that stressed the trauma produced the most stress, while the denial and the detached commentary produced less. Lazarus later built a theory on this, that stress depends on how we appraise an event, and not only on the event. It suggests that the story we tell ourselves about something can change how the body reacts.

# Six steps by email
@src Dodds, Muhamad & Watts, Science, 2003

In 1967 Stanley Milgram gave packages to people in the American Midwest, and asked them to get each one to a stranger near Boston by passing it only to someone they knew on a first-name basis. Some chains reached the target, which led to the idea of six degrees of separation. In 2003 Peter Dodds, Roby Muhamad and Duncan Watts repeated the experiment with email, on a global scale. More than sixty thousand people tried to reach one of eighteen targets, in thirteen countries.

Most of the chains broke, since people did not pass the message on, so the researchers corrected for the dropouts. They estimated that it takes a median of five to seven steps to reach a stranger anywhere in the world. The chains that succeeded went mostly through weaker ties, such as work contacts, and not through close friends. Your acquaintances matter because they know different people than you do.

# Remembering more than you saw
@series brain
@src Intraub & Richardson, J. Exp. Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 1989

When you recall a photograph, how much of the scene do you think was in it? In 1989 Helene Intraub and Michael Richardson showed people pictures of scenes, and some time later asked them to judge whether new pictures were the same as the ones they had seen, or closer, or wider. Some of the pictures were close-ups, and others were wider views.

People tended to remember the close-up views as wider than they had been. When they were shown the same close-up again, they said that it looked too close, as if it had been cropped. The mind seems to fill in the space just beyond the edge of what we see, based on what we expect. The effect has been called boundary extension. It is a small example of how a memory is partly built from expectation, and is not only a recording.

# Confident, not accurate
@series brain
@src Talarico & Rubin, Psychological Science, 2003

Where were you when you heard about the attacks of September 11? Many people feel that their memory of that day is as clear as a photograph. In 2003 Jennifer Talarico and David Rubin tested that feeling. The day after the attacks, students at Duke University wrote down how they had heard the news. They also wrote down a recent everyday event. Later, groups of them were tested again, after one, six or thirty-two weeks.

The students' accuracy fell at the same rate for the news of September 11 and for the everyday events. But their confidence did not fall in the same way. They stayed as sure of the vivid memory as they had been, while their belief in the ordinary memories declined. So memories of big events are not special in being right, but in feeling right. The more emotion people felt at the time, the more sure they were later, but not the more accurate.

# A touch and a bigger tip
@series sell
@src Crusco & Wetzel, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 1984

Can a very small gesture change how much people pay? In 1984 April Crusco and Christopher Wetzel arranged for waitresses in a restaurant to touch some of the customers briefly, when they returned the change. For some customers the touch was on the hand, for others on the shoulder, and for the rest there was no touch. The researchers recorded the tips.

Customers who were touched left larger tips than those who were not, and it made little difference whether the touch was on the hand or the shoulder, or whether the customer was a man or a woman. The touch did not change how the customers rated the waitress or the restaurant, so it worked without changing what they said they felt. Small signs of warmth can change behavior, but this was one early field experiment in a single setting.

# Meeting without a plan
@series talk
@src Schelling, The Strategy of Conflict, 1960

Suppose that you must meet a stranger in New York City tomorrow, and neither of you can communicate with the other beforehand. Where, and when, do you go? In his 1960 book The Strategy of Conflict, the economist Thomas Schelling put the question to his students. The most common answer was the information booth at Grand Central Terminal, at noon. They had no way of agreeing, but they landed on the same spot.

Schelling called such a spot a focal point. It is a choice that stands out, so each person expects the other to pick it. People use focal points when they choose heads over tails, or a round number. Schelling shared the Nobel Prize in economics in 2005 for his work on conflict and cooperation. In a negotiation, a clear landmark, such as a round number or a past precedent, can help two sides to settle.

# The man who lived in the present
@series brain
@src Deborah Wearing, Forever Today, 2005; Sacks, The New Yorker, 2007

In March of 1985 the British musician Clive Wearing, a leading expert on early music who worked for BBC Radio 3, caught a viral infection of the brain, called herpes encephalitis. He survived, but the infection damaged parts of the brain which are needed to form new memories. He could remember for only a few seconds, between seven and thirty by different estimates. After that, each moment felt to him as if he had just woken up.

His diary shows the effect. Again and again he wrote that he was awake for the first time, and he recorded it with relief. Yet he kept his musical skill, and his love for his wife, Deborah, which she said never faded. His case shows that there are different kinds of memory, and that the memory for skills and for feelings can survive when the memory for events is lost.

# Why a hard initiation makes a group seem better
@conf mixed
@src Aronson & Mills, J. Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1959

Do people value something more if they had to suffer to get it? In 1959 Elliot Aronson and Judson Mills invited female volunteers to join a discussion group about the psychology of sex. To get in, some had to read a list of obscene words, and a vivid passage, aloud to a man. For others, the words were mild, and others went through no test. Then all of them listened to a recording of the group's discussion, which had been made to be dull.

The women who had gone through the severe test rated the group and its discussion as more interesting than the others did. The authors explained it by cognitive dissonance, since it is hard to believe that you went through something awful for nothing. Critics noted that contrast might explain the result, and later replications gave mixed results. It still helps to explain why hazing may raise loyalty, and why we should be wary of the things that we paid for dearly.

# Scanning an imagined map
@series brain
@conf mixed
@src Kosslyn, Ball & Reiser, J. Exp. Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 1978

When you picture a map in your head, can you travel across it? In 1978 Stephen Kosslyn, Thomas Ball and Brian Reiser asked volunteers to memorize a map of an imaginary island, with landmarks such as a hut, a well and a tree. Then the map was taken away. The volunteers were asked to picture it, and to imagine a small dot moving from one landmark to another.

The longer the real distance between two landmarks, the longer the scan took, and the time grew in step with the distance. The mind's picture seemed to behave like a real map. Critics, such as Zenon Pylyshyn, argued that the volunteers may have known what was expected of them, and simply took longer for the farther places. The debate over mental images, and what they are made of, went on for years.

# A survey of a hundred thousand IBM workers
@series talk
@conf mixed
@src Hofstede, Culture's Consequences, 1980

In the late 1960s and early 1970s the Dutch researcher Geert Hofstede analyzed attitude surveys of employees of IBM in dozens of countries. There were more than a hundred thousand questionnaires. Because the people worked for one company, in similar jobs, he reasoned that differences in their answers came from national culture. He found that countries differed along a few dimensions, such as power distance, which measures how far people accept that power is unequal.

Another dimension was individualism, which is the degree to which people see themselves as individuals more than as members of groups. The ideas have been used widely, in business and in the study of communication across cultures. Critics point out that IBM employees are not typical of their countries, that the surveys were not made to study culture, and that cultures change. It is a useful starting point for talking about differences, but it should not be used to predict a single person.

# Where stress got its name
@conf mixed
@src Selye, Nature, 1936

In the mid-1930s Hans Selye, a young researcher at McGill University in Montreal, injected rats with an extract from the ovary, hoping to discover a new hormone. The rats showed three changes. The outer part of the adrenal glands grew larger, the thymus gland shrank, and the stomach and intestine developed ulcers. At first he believed that he had found a hormone. Then he found that the same changes followed other irritating substances, and also cold, injury or exhaustion.

In a short letter to Nature in 1936 he described a common reaction of the body to many kinds of harm. He later called it the general adaptation syndrome, and he became famous for bringing the word stress into the study of health. Many details of his picture have been revised since. But the idea that very different kinds of strain can trigger a shared response in the body remains central.
