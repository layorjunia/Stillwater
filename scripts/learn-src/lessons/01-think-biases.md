# Optimism bias
@src Weinstein, 1980

Ask a roomful of people whether their own future will go better or worse than average, and nearly everyone says better. In 1980 the psychologist Neil Weinstein turned that hunch into a test. He gave college students a list of life events and asked them to rate their own chances compared with their classmates'. For good things, like a long life or a satisfying job, they put themselves above average. For bad things, like a divorce or a serious illness, they put themselves below.

That can't be true for everyone, because half of any group sits at or below the middle. A little optimism isn't a flaw. It helps you keep going when things are hard. The trouble starts when it talks you out of the insurance, the savings account or the checkup, because all of those exist for the bad outcome you have quietly assigned to somebody else.

# Overconfidence
@src Alpert & Raiffa, 1982; Russo & Schoemaker, 1992

There is a simple test that researchers have been running for decades. You are asked a question like "How long is the Nile?" and told to give a range you are 90% sure contains the right answer. If people were well calibrated, about nine out of ten of those ranges would catch the truth. In the classic studies, only about half to two-thirds did.

The people weren't ignorant. They knew roughly how long the Nile is. What they got wrong was how much that rough knowledge could be trusted, so they drew their lines too narrow and felt sure about it. The cheap fix is to take whatever range feels right and stretch it until it feels uncomfortably wide. Then keep score for a while. Most people find they are right less often than they expected, and the discomfort of a wide range turns out to be about the right size.

# Endowment effect
@src Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, 1990

In 1990 Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch and Richard Thaler ran a neat experiment with ordinary coffee mugs. Half the students in a classroom were given a mug, and the rest got nothing. Then the two groups were allowed to trade. The students who owned a mug wouldn't sell for less than about $7. The ones without a mug wouldn't pay more than a little under $3.

If people simply valued mugs differently, you would expect plenty of deals, because the mugs would end up with whoever wanted them most. Instead very few trades happened. The mug hadn't changed at all. Only who was holding it had changed, and giving something up hurts more than never having had it in the first place. The same pull shows up when we hang on to a car, a house or a box of old clothes because they are ours, not because they are worth what we think.

# Status quo bias
@src Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988

When William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser studied how people made choices in the late 1980s, they found a strong pull toward whatever a person already had, or whatever was marked as the default. At Harvard, for example, long-time employees mostly stayed in their old health plans, while newly hired employees picked the newer plans far more often. The older plans weren't better. They were just already there.

Doing nothing feels safe because the present arrangement becomes the baseline, and every alternative gets measured against it as a possible loss. So the cost of this bias is invisible. It is the better phone plan, the better route or the better habit that you never looked at, because looking at it would have meant making a choice. A useful question to ask is what you would pick if you were starting from scratch today.

# The default effect
@src Johnson & Goldstein, 2003

In some countries you become an organ donor only if you tick a box to opt in. In others you are a donor unless you tick a box to opt out. Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein compared the two kinds of system in 2003. In Germany, where people have to opt in, about 12% were registered. In Austria, where people have to opt out, almost everyone was, at 99.98%.

Nobody believes Austrians are eight times more generous than Germans. The difference is that most people did nothing, and in each country, doing nothing meant something different. The pre-selected option carries a quiet message that this is what most people do, and it also saves the effort of deciding. That is why the way a form is set up can matter more than a long argument. It also means you can use it on yourself, by making the helpful choice the automatic one.

# The halo effect
@src Thorndike, 1920; Nisbett & Wilson, 1977

In 1920 the psychologist Edward Thorndike noticed something odd about army officers who were asked to rate their soldiers on separate qualities such as intelligence, leadership and physical bearing. The scores were suspiciously alike. A soldier rated well on one trait was usually rated well on all of them, as if a good impression in one area glowed onto the rest.

A later experiment by Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson made it even clearer. Students watched a lecturer who behaved either warmly or coldly, and then rated him. Those who saw the cold version found his accent and his mannerisms more irritating, although both groups had watched the very same man. Afterward they insisted that liking him had nothing to do with their ratings. That last part is the real lesson, because the halo works without our noticing it.

# Self-serving bias
@src Miller & Ross, 1975; Mullen & Riordan, 1988

When something goes well, most of us explain it by pointing to our ability and effort. When it goes badly, we tend to point to bad luck, an unfair test or other people. The pattern flips when we look at someone else, where we are quicker to blame their failures on their character. Researchers have found this habit across many kinds of tasks and many kinds of people.

Some of it is honest guesswork, because you know your own effort better than you know anyone else's, and you can see your own bad circumstances while theirs stay out of view. But part of it clearly protects how we feel about ourselves. The habit can leave you repeating a mistake without knowing it, because the lesson is always filed under "not my fault." A fair test is to ask what you would say if the same thing had happened to a friend.

# The just-world belief
@src Lerner & Simmons, 1966

Melvin Lerner became curious about why people so often blame victims, and in 1966 he and Carolyn Simmons designed a study to look at it. Volunteers watched what appeared to be a woman receiving painful shocks as part of a memory experiment. When the volunteers learned they couldn't do anything to stop it, and she seemed to keep suffering, they began to rate her as less likable and less worthy.

Lerner's explanation was that most of us need to believe the world is basically fair, so that good things happen to good people and bad things to bad ones. When something terrible happens to a person who did nothing to deserve it, that belief is threatened. One easy way out is to decide that she must have done something. It protects our comfort at her expense, and it is worth catching when you notice yourself wondering what a victim did wrong.

# Mere exposure
@src Zajonc, 1968

In the 1960s Robert Zajonc showed people a series of nonsense words and Chinese-looking characters. Some appeared once, and others appeared many times. Later he asked which of them seemed to mean something good. The ones people had seen more often got better ratings, even though no one had any idea what the symbols meant.

The effect seems to work without our noticing it. Familiar things are easier for the brain to process, and that ease feels a lot like liking. It helps explain why a song you didn't care for at first becomes a favorite after a few listens, and why advertisers repeat themselves so shamelessly. It's also a mild, kind fact about people. Someone you were unsure of at first can grow on you, simply because you kept running into each other.

# Primacy and recency
@src Murdock, 1962

If you read someone a list of twenty words and ask them to write down as many as they can remember, the results tend to form a U-shape. People recall the first few words well, probably because they had time to rehearse them into long-term memory. They also recall the last few well, because those are still sitting in short-term memory. The words in the middle mostly fade.

These two effects have been known since early memory experiments, including a well-known one by Bennet Murdock in 1962. They explain why the opening and the ending of a talk stick with an audience, and why a middle section can vanish. If you have something that matters, in a speech, a meeting or an email, put it in one of those two places. And if you're the one studying, spend extra time on the middle of the list, because that is where your memory is thinnest.

# The Barnum effect
@src Forer, 1949

In 1948 the psychologist Bertram Forer gave each of his students a personality sketch. He told them it had been written for them individually, based on a test they had taken. In fact every student got exactly the same text, which he had assembled from lines in a newsstand horoscope. It included things like "you have a strong need for other people to like you" and "at times you are doubtful about yourself."

The students rated how well the sketch described them on a scale from 0 to 5, and the average was 4.26. Statements that are vague enough to fit nearly everyone feel uncannily personal once we believe they were meant for us. It's the quiet engine behind horoscopes and many personality readings. A good check is to ask whether a description could be said about almost anyone. If it could, it hasn't told you anything yet.

# Cognitive dissonance
@src Festinger & Carlsmith, 1959

Leon Festinger's idea was simple and sharp. When two of our thoughts clash, or when we act against what we believe, it feels uncomfortable, and we work to ease the tension. The easiest thing to change is often our attitude. In the classic 1959 experiment with James Carlsmith, people spent time on a very dull task and were then paid either $1 or $20 to tell the next person it was fun.

Afterward, those who had been paid $1 rated the task as more enjoyable than those who had been paid $20. The $20 group had a perfectly good reason for saying something untrue. The $1 group had no such excuse, only the uncomfortable thought "I said it was fun." So some of them quietly decided that it must have been. It's worth remembering that we sometimes talk ourselves into things not because the evidence changed, but because we needed to feel consistent.

# The overjustification effect
@src Lepper, Greene & Nisbett, 1973

Mark Lepper, David Greene and Richard Nisbett studied preschoolers who liked to draw. They split the children into three groups. Some were promised a "good player" certificate for drawing, some received the same certificate as an unexpected surprise, and some got nothing. A week or two later, the children who had expected a reward spent less time drawing on their own than the other two groups.

The finding is that a reward given for something you already enjoy can turn the activity into work. You start to think you're doing it for the prize, and when the prize goes away, so does the reason. It's why a hobby sometimes loses its charm once it becomes a job, and why praising a child's effort has to be done with some care. The effect is not universal, but it's a real caution against paying people to do what they love.

# Illusory correlation
@src Chapman & Chapman, 1967

Loren and Jean Chapman wanted to know why clinicians kept trusting a popular test called the Draw-a-Person test, even though it wasn't predicting anything well. They showed students a set of drawings, each paired with a symptom of the person who supposedly drew it. The pairings were random. Yet the students came away reporting the very same links the clinicians believed in, like big eyes going with suspiciousness.

That showed that those links were coming from the students' heads and not from the data. When we expect two things to go together, we notice the cases that fit and forget the many that don't. A full moon and a busy night in the emergency room is a familiar example. The way to guard against it is to look at all four cells of the table, including the times when neither thing happened.

# The ostrich effect
@src Karlsson, Loewenstein & Seppi, 2009

Researchers looked at how often investors in Sweden logged in to check their accounts. They found a pattern. After the stock market had gone down, people checked less often than they did after it went up. Looking would have meant seeing a loss, so they simply didn't look. The researchers named it the ostrich effect, after the old, mistaken idea that ostriches bury their heads in the sand.

It's a very human response. Avoiding bad news feels like avoiding the bad thing itself. But it can be costly, because the bill, the test result or the bank statement is waiting whether you open it or not. Not looking won't change what it says, and it takes away your chance to respond early, when a response would be cheapest. Often the dread of opening it is worse than what is inside.

# Zero-risk bias
@src Baron, Gowda & Kunreuther, 1993

Suppose you could make one danger disappear entirely, or you could cut a bigger danger by an even larger amount. Many people prefer the first option, because getting to zero feels like a clean win. Jonathan Baron and his colleagues looked at this in surveys about cleaning up hazardous waste sites, and found that people leaned toward plans that removed a small risk completely, even when another plan would reduce the total risk by more.

The comfort of "none" is powerful. A risk that is gone is easy to picture and easy to celebrate, while a risk that has only been shrunk still feels like something to worry about. The cost is that resources can drift away from where they'd do the most good. When you're facing a choice like this, it helps to ask how much total harm each option removes, and to set aside the pull of the number zero.

# The hot hand
@src Gilovich, Vallone & Tversky, 1985; Miller & Sanjurjo, 2018
@conf mixed

Basketball fans have long believed that a player who has made several shots in a row is more likely to make the next one. In 1985 Thomas Gilovich, Robert Vallone and Amos Tversky looked at the actual numbers and concluded that the belief was an illusion. Their paper became one of the most famous examples of a bias that fools even experts.

Then, in 2018, Joshua Miller and Adam Sanjurjo found a subtle statistical error in the way streaks had been counted in that kind of analysis. When they corrected it, a small but real hot-hand effect appeared. So the story is not as tidy as it was once told. It's a good reminder that even well-known findings can be revised, and that a debunking can itself need debunking. The sensible position is that streaks may exist, but they're smaller than fans feel they are.

# The curse of knowledge
@src Newton, 1990; Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber, 1989

In 1990 a Stanford graduate student named Elizabeth Newton ran a simple game. One person tapped out the rhythm of a well-known song, like "Happy Birthday," on a table, while a listener tried to guess what song it was. Before they began, the tappers predicted that the listeners would get about half of them right. In fact, listeners guessed correctly only about 2.5% of the time.

The tappers couldn't imagine not hearing the melody. In their heads the song was playing along with every tap, and they had no way to switch it off. That's the curse of knowledge. Once you know something, it's very hard to remember what it was like not to know it. It's why experts can make poor teachers, and why a first draft often makes sense only to its author. The usual remedy is to test your explanation on someone who doesn't already know the answer.

# Mental accounting
@src Kahneman & Tversky, 1984

The economist Richard Thaler suggested that people don't treat money as one big pool. Instead we keep separate mental accounts, each with its own rules. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky made this vivid with two short stories. In the first, you've paid for a ticket to a play and you lose the ticket on your way in. In the second, you were about to buy a ticket and discover you've lost $10 in cash.

In the first story, only 46% of people said they'd buy another ticket. In the second, 88% said they'd still buy one. In both cases you're out exactly $10. But the lost ticket seems to come out of the "entertainment" account, which makes a second one feel like paying twice, while lost cash seems to be a general loss. A dollar is a dollar, but we don't always act like it, which is why a tax refund gets spent more easily than a paycheck.

# Hyperbolic discounting
@src Ainslie, 1975; Laibson, 1997

Most of us value a reward now more than the same reward later. That part is ordinary. The odd part is how the discount changes with distance. Someone might take $50 today rather than wait a year for $100. Yet the same person, offered a choice between $50 in five years and $100 in six years, will usually wait for the larger sum.

The gap between the two choices is exactly the same in both cases, one year for another $50. But when the first reward is close enough to touch, it becomes almost irresistible. That explains a lot of everyday behavior. We say we'll start saving or exercising "next month," and then when next month arrives, it feels like now again. The best defense is to make the decision early, when the reward still feels far away, and to make it hard to change your mind later.

# Illusory superiority
@src Svenson, 1981

In 1981 the Swedish psychologist Ola Svenson asked a group of American drivers to rate their driving skill compared with the other drivers in the study. Fully 93% said they were better than the median driver. That's mathematically impossible, since only half of any group can be above the middle.

The same pattern has been found for intelligence, for a sense of humor and for many other traits. Most of us rate ourselves above average, at least on the things we care about. There are some honest reasons for this, such as picking our own definition of "good." But it's also a comfortable story, and it can make us a bit less likely to practice and improve. An honest check is to ask someone you trust, or to look for an objective measure, rather than relying on the feeling.

# The bias blind spot
@src Pronin, Lin & Ross, 2002

Emily Pronin and her colleagues asked people how susceptible they were to a list of common biases, and then how susceptible the average American was. People rated themselves as less biased than others. In another part of the study, they were told about a particular bias, such as the tendency to take credit for good outcomes and blame bad ones on circumstances. Many still felt they were exempt from it.

It's an uncomfortable finding, because it means that knowing about the biases doesn't protect us from them. We feel that our own thinking is clear, because we can see our honest reasons, while we only see other people's results. The practical lesson is a modest one. Instead of asking whether you're biased, which most of us will answer with no, ask what you'd expect a biased person in your position to be doing, and check whether you are.

# Naive realism
@src Ross & Ward, 1996

The psychologists Lee Ross and Andrew Ward described a feeling most of us share without noticing it. We feel that we see the world as it really is. We feel that reasonable people see it the same way. And so, when someone disagrees with us, it seems that they must be uninformed, lazy or biased. That's what they called naive realism.

The trouble is that everyone feels this way, which means two people can each believe the other is the one wearing the filter. It's a central idea in research on conflict, and it explains why arguments can become so heated so quickly. It suggests a better first question when we disagree. Rather than asking what's wrong with the other person, ask what they have seen or experienced that I haven't. The answer is often enough to turn a fight into a conversation.

# The illusory truth effect
@src Hasher, Goldstein & Toppino, 1977; Fazio et al., 2015

In 1977 Lynn Hasher, David Goldstein and Thomas Toppino asked people to rate how true a list of statements was, repeating the exercise over several weeks. Statements they had seen before were rated as more likely to be true than new ones, and this was so whether the statements were actually true or not. Simply hearing something again made it feel more believable.

A 2015 study by Lisa Fazio and her colleagues found that this holds even when people actually know better. Repetition makes a claim easy for the mind to process, and ease can feel a great deal like truth. That's an uncomfortable fact in an age of slogans and endlessly shared posts. The defense is to treat the feeling of "I've heard that before" as a reason to look something up, not as a reason to believe it.

# The false consensus effect
@src Ross, Greene & House, 1977

In a 1977 study, Lee Ross, David Greene and Pamela House asked students whether they'd be willing to walk around campus for thirty minutes wearing a sandwich board that read "Eat at Joe's." Then they asked them to guess how many other students would agree to do it. Those who said yes thought most other students would say yes too. Those who said no thought most would say no.

Each side saw its own choice as the common-sense one. We tend to use our own views as a sample of what people in general think, and since we mostly know people who think like us, that sample is lopsided. It can lead us to be surprised when an election goes the other way, or when a friend doesn't share our taste. The cure is simple but takes effort, which is to ask around before assuming that everybody agrees.

# Pluralistic ignorance
@src Prentice & Miller, 1993

In the early 1990s Deborah Prentice and Dale Miller asked Princeton students how comfortable they were with the heavy drinking on campus, and how comfortable they thought the average student was. Individually, the students were less comfortable than they imagined everyone else to be. Each person seemed to assume they were the odd one out.

So nobody said anything, and the norm that everyone privately doubted stayed firmly in place. The same thing happens in classrooms when no one asks a question because everyone else looks like they understand, and in meetings when a bad plan goes ahead because each person thinks they're the only one with doubts. It's a strange kind of trap, because a single honest voice can break it. Often when someone finally speaks up, the first reaction is relief.

# Social loafing
@src Latané, Williams & Harkins, 1979

In the 1880s a French engineer named Max Ringelmann noticed that when people pulled on a rope together, each person pulled less hard as the group got bigger. A century later, in 1979, Bibb Latané and his colleagues tested the idea with noise. They asked people to shout and clap as loudly as they could, either alone or in a group, with blindfolds and headphones so they couldn't tell how loud the others were being.

Each person was quieter in the group. The more people there were, the less each one gave. When your own effort isn't visible, or when it blends into the total, it's easy to let others carry it, even without meaning to. The remedies are fairly practical. Give people separate parts with their own names on them, keep teams small, and make each person's contribution something that can be seen.

# Self-fulfilling prophecy
@src Merton, 1948

In 1948 the sociologist Robert Merton described how a false belief can come true simply because of the way people react to it. His example was a bank that was perfectly healthy until a rumor started that it was about to fail. Customers rushed to withdraw their money, and with so much money leaving at once, the bank did fail. The prediction had caused the outcome.

It works on a smaller scale too. A teacher who expects little from a student may call on them less, challenge them less and encourage them less, until the student does indeed fall behind. A person who is sure a date will go badly may act distant and confirm the guess. The idea is a good reminder that some beliefs about the future aren't just forecasts. They're a push on the thing being forecast.

# Misattributing arousal
@src Dutton & Aron, 1974
@conf mixed

Donald Dutton and Arthur Aron's 1974 experiment is one of the most famous in social psychology. Men crossing a high, swaying suspension bridge, or a low, solid one, were met on the far side by a female interviewer who gave them her phone number. The men on the scary bridge were more likely to call her later. The researchers suggested they had mistaken the excitement of the heights for attraction to her.

Later studies of this particular setup have had mixed results, and the experiment is not as clean as its reputation. But the broader idea is well supported. Our bodies send signals, like a racing heart, and our minds then have to guess the cause, and sometimes the guess lands on whatever is nearby. It's one reason a first date at a thrilling place may feel more romantic, and one reason it helps to pause and ask what is really making your heart pound.

# The decoy effect
@src Huber, Payne & Puto, 1982
@conf mixed

Imagine choosing between two options, where each is better in some way. Maybe one is cheaper and the other is higher quality. Now add a third option that is clearly worse than one of them but not the other. In studies that began with Joel Huber, John Payne and Christopher Puto in 1982, people tended to pick the option that made the third look bad. The decoy didn't have to be chosen to do its job.

It works because a choice between two different things is hard, while comparing something to a plainly inferior version of itself is easy. The effect has shown up in many tests but not in all of them, and some later studies found it fades when the numbers are easy to compare side by side. So it's best held as a real but delicate effect. If you notice a menu or a price list that includes an option nobody would really want, ask what it is there to make you choose.

# Choice overload
@src Iyengar & Lepper, 2000; Scheibehenne et al., 2010
@conf mixed

In 2000 Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper set up a tasting table of jam in a grocery store. On one day it offered 24 kinds. On another day it offered 6. More people stopped at the large display, but when it came to buying, 30% of the people at the small display bought a jar, compared with only 3% at the large one. It became a classic story about how too many choices can freeze us.

The story has aged less well than it first seemed. When researchers pooled many studies of the idea in 2010, the average effect was close to zero. It appears that choice overload happens in some situations, such as when the options are hard to compare and you have no clear preference, and not in others. So more choices won't always hurt. But if you find yourself frozen in front of a long list, it's a sign that narrowing it down first may help.

# The Pygmalion effect
@src Rosenthal & Jacobson, 1968
@conf mixed

In 1968 Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson told teachers at an elementary school that a test had identified certain pupils as likely to bloom intellectually that year. In fact the pupils had been chosen at random. At the end of the year, some of those children, mainly the youngest ones, had gained more on an IQ test than their classmates.

The study was criticized for its methods, and the size of the result is still argued over. But later research found that teachers' expectations do have a small, real effect, partly because they shape how much attention, encouragement and challenge each pupil receives. The lesson isn't that belief works like magic. It's that when we expect more from someone, we often treat them differently without realizing it, and that treatment can help them rise to it.

# Stereotype threat
@src Steele & Aronson, 1995
@conf mixed

In 1995 Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson gave Black and white students a set of hard verbal questions. When the test was described as a measure of intellectual ability, Black students did worse than when the same test was described as a problem-solving exercise. Steele suggested that worry about confirming a negative stereotype can use up mental effort that would otherwise go into the task.

Later attempts to repeat the finding have turned up weaker and less consistent results, so the size of the effect and the conditions under which it appears are still debated. It remains a useful idea about how circumstances shape performance, and it suggests that how a task is introduced can matter more than we'd expect. But it's best held loosely, as an idea that is partly supported and still being tested, not as a settled rule.

# Groupthink
@src Janis, 1972
@conf mixed

The psychologist Irving Janis wanted to understand how smart, capable groups could make terrible decisions. One of his main examples was the Kennedy administration's disastrous Bay of Pigs invasion of Cuba in 1961. He argued that when a close-knit group values agreement above everything else, it silences doubts, assumes it can't fail and ignores outside opinions. He named the pattern groupthink in 1972.

Later research has found that the model fits some famous failures but not all of them, and that careful, well-run groups can avoid the trap. So it isn't an iron law. But the habit it points to is still wise. If a group is deciding something important, someone should be assigned to argue the other side, outsiders should be consulted, and the leader should speak last, so that the first opinion voiced doesn't settle the matter.

# Power posing
@src Carney, Cuddy & Yap, 2010; Ranehill et al., 2015
@conf contested

In 2010 Dana Carney, Amy Cuddy and Andy Yap reported that standing in an expansive "power pose" for two minutes, like hands on hips or feet up on a desk, raised testosterone, lowered cortisol and made people more willing to take risks. The idea spread widely, helped along by one of the most-watched TED talks ever given.

Then it began to unravel. A larger replication in 2015 by Eva Ranehill and her colleagues found no effect on hormones or on risk-taking behavior. People did say they felt a bit more powerful, which is a real but much smaller claim. Dana Carney herself later said that she no longer believed the original effects were real. Standing tall may still make you feel a little better, but there is no good evidence that it changes your body chemistry or your performance.

# The facial feedback hypothesis
@src Strack, Martin & Stepper, 1988; Wagenmakers et al., 2016
@conf contested

The idea is that the muscles of your face can influence how you feel, so that smiling may make you a bit happier. In 1988 Fritz Strack, Leonard Martin and Sabine Stepper had people hold a pen in their teeth, which forces a smile, or with their lips, which blocks one, while they rated how funny cartoons were. The people who were smiling found the cartoons funnier.

It became a textbook example. But in 2016 a group of seventeen labs tried to repeat the experiment and, taken together, found no effect. Researchers still debate whether small effects of this kind exist under the right conditions, and the question isn't closed. For now, a fair summary is that the original result didn't hold up in the largest test, and that "fake it until you feel it" isn't something we can count on.

# The Mozart effect
@src Rauscher, Shaw & Ky, 1993; Pietschnig et al., 2010
@conf contested

In 1993 Frances Rauscher, Gordon Shaw and Katherine Ky reported that college students who listened to ten minutes of a Mozart sonata did slightly better on a spatial reasoning task afterward. The press turned it into "listening to Mozart makes you smarter," and a US state even gave a classical music CD to every newborn.

The original effect was small, and it lasted only a short while. A later review of many studies, in 2010, concluded that any boost seems to come from a general effect of mood and alertness, so it appears with any music a person enjoys, and it isn't specific to Mozart or to intelligence. Music is a fine thing to listen to, and it may lift your mood before a task. But there is no good reason to think it makes a baby, or anyone, smarter.

# Pareidolia
@src NASA/JPL Cydonia images, 1976 and 2001

The human brain is tuned to find faces, and it does so quickly, even in clouds, electrical outlets and burned toast. That's pareidolia, the habit of seeing a meaningful pattern in something random. In 1976 the Viking 1 orbiter photographed a mesa in the Cydonia region of Mars that looked a great deal like a human face, and it became a sensation.

When NASA's later spacecraft took much sharper pictures of the same spot, in 1998 and again in 2001, the "face" turned out to be an ordinary hill. It's an easy mistake to make for a species that depends so heavily on faces. For a baby or a hunter, noticing a face that isn't there costs very little, and missing one that is can cost a lot. So we err on the side of seeing too many, and the Man in the Moon is the result.

# The lie-detection limit
@src Bond & DePaulo, 2006

If you've ever believed you can tell when someone is lying, the research isn't encouraging. In 2006 Charles Bond and Bella DePaulo combined 206 studies of the question and found that people correctly judged truths and lies about 54% of the time. That's only a little better than a coin flip.

Police officers, judges and others who do this for a living did no better than students. The signals most of us think we're reading, such as looking away or fidgeting, turn out not to be reliable. Many honest people look nervous and many liars look calm. So when you feel certain you can see through someone, treat that certainty with some caution. It's usually safer to check the facts than to trust your gut about someone's face.

# The truth default
@src Levine, 2014

Tim Levine's research suggests that our tendency to believe what other people tell us isn't really a weakness. It's a reasonable way to live, since most people tell the truth most of the time. If we checked everything, conversations would be slow and exhausting and trust would never get a chance to grow.

So we start from an assumption of honesty, and we switch to doubt only when something strongly suggests we should, such as a clear contradiction or a big stake. The downside is that a skilled liar can take advantage of that habit. Thoughtful skepticism doesn't mean doubting everyone. It means knowing which situations call for checking, like money, safety or a big decision, and saving your doubt for those.

# The end-of-history illusion
@src Quoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson, 2013

In 2013 Jordi Quoidbach, Daniel Gilbert and Timothy Wilson surveyed about 19,000 people between the ages of 18 and 68. Some were asked how much they had changed in the past ten years, and others were asked how much they expected to change in the next ten. At every age, people said they had changed a lot in the past and expected to change little in the future.

But the people who were ten years older reported more change than the younger ones had predicted. In other words, we all seem to treat who we are right now as the finished version. It's a quiet kind of mistake, and it affects big decisions like a tattoo, a career or a long commitment. It's worth remembering that the person making the plan is not the person who will have to live with it, and that person will probably be somewhat different.
