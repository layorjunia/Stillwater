# First principles
@src Aristotle, Metaphysics

Aristotle described a first principle as "the first basis from which a thing is known," something that can't be broken down any further. The habit that grew out of this is to ask what you actually know to be true about a problem, set aside what everyone assumes, and build your answer up from there.

Most of us reason by analogy. We look at what other people do and copy it with small tweaks, and that's usually fine, because it's fast and it works. The trouble comes when you're stuck. If the usual approach has failed, it won't help to repeat it more carefully. Going back to the basics is how you find options that nobody else was looking at, because everyone else was still standing on the same assumptions. A simple version is to ask yourself, "What is this made of, and what do I know for certain about each part?"

# Inversion
@src Munger, Poor Charlie's Almanack (2005)

The mathematician Carl Jacobi used to say "invert, always invert," meaning that a hard problem can often be solved by turning it around. Instead of asking how to succeed at something, ask what would guarantee failure, and then avoid doing those things.

The investor Charlie Munger loved this approach and liked to quote a line that he picked up: "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there." It's a simple idea, and it works especially well for decisions where the ways to fail are easier to list than the ways to win. If you want a happy marriage, you can list the habits that wreck one. If you want a long life, start with what shortens it. Avoiding the obvious mistakes won't make you brilliant, but it often gets you most of the way there.

# Second-order thinking
@src Vann, 2003

The first effect of a choice is usually easy to see. The second effect, which is what happens after that, is the one people miss. In 1902, the French colonial government in Hanoi tried to fight rats by paying a bounty for every rat tail that people handed in. It sounded like a clever plan.

Some people started breeding rats in order to collect the money, and others cut off the tails and let the rats go so they could breed and be caught again. The rat problem grew. The officials had thought about the first step, which was that people would hunt rats, and not about what the incentive would do next. Before you act, try asking "and then what?" at least twice. It slows you down a little, and it saves you from a lot of surprises like the rat farms.

# Occam's razor
@src Standard philosophy reference

When two explanations account for the facts equally well, try the simpler one first. William of Ockham was a fourteenth-century English friar, though the popular wording about not multiplying "entities" beyond need came along later. The idea has been attached to his name ever since.

It's worth remembering what the razor is not. It isn't a law of nature, and the world isn't always simple. Sometimes the right explanation is complicated. It's guidance about where to begin looking. The simple explanation is usually quicker to test, and if it fails, you can move on to a more complicated one without having wasted much time. When your car won't start, you check whether the tank is empty before you suspect a rare fault in the engine computer.

# Hanlon's razor
@src Bloch, Murphy's Law Book Two (1980)

The advice is not to blame malice for what can be explained by carelessness, distraction or confusion. A friend doesn't answer your message. A colleague sends a curt email. It's very tempting to assume the worst, that you're being ignored or disrespected on purpose.

But far more often, the person is busy, tired or didn't realize how it would read. The saying appeared in print in 1980 in a book of Murphy's Law variations, where it was credited to a Robert Hanlon. It is a mild and practical rule, and it tends to save relationships a lot of unnecessary grief. It doesn't say that no one is ever malicious. It says that this is a poor first guess, and that being wrong about it is costly.

# Chesterton's fence
@src Chesterton, The Thing (1929)

In 1929 the writer G. K. Chesterton told a small parable. Imagine you come across a fence across a road, and you can't see any reason for it. A reformer says, "Let's clear it away." A wiser reformer says, "If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think. Then, when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it, I may allow you to destroy it."

The point isn't that nothing should ever change. It's that you should understand why something exists before you remove it. A rule, a habit or a strange-looking procedure may have been put there for a reason that nobody remembers, and removing it can bring the reason back in a form you didn't expect. Often the fence turns out to be pointless and you can take it down with a clear conscience. Sometimes it was holding back a bull.

# Opportunity cost
@src Bastiat, 1850

The true cost of something isn't only the price you pay, but the best alternative you gave up to get it. The French economist Frédéric Bastiat made the point in 1850 with an essay about "what is seen and what is not seen." The broken window gets noticed, and the other things that money could have bought do not.

We notice what a purchase gets us. We rarely notice what the same money or the same hour would have done somewhere else. A free afternoon spent on one thing is an afternoon not spent on another, and you can't get it back. Asking "instead of what?" is a small habit that changes a lot of decisions, because it makes the invisible option visible. It works for money, and it works even better for time, which is the one thing nobody can earn more of.

# Margin of safety
@src Graham, The Intelligent Investor (1949)

When engineers design a bridge, they don't build it to carry exactly the load they expect. They build it to carry several times that, because the future always holds surprises. Trucks get heavier, steel gets old, and numbers turn out to be a little off.

The investor Benjamin Graham borrowed the idea for his book The Intelligent Investor in 1949. He advised buying only when a price is well below what you think a thing is worth, so that you have room to be wrong. The same idea applies to ordinary life. Leave extra time for the trip, keep extra money in the account, and leave extra room in the schedule. A plan that works only when everything goes right isn't really a plan. The margin is what lets you survive the day that doesn't go well.

# Circle of competence
@src Buffett, 1996 shareholder letter

Everyone understands some things well and others hardly at all. Warren Buffett has said that what matters isn't how big your circle of competence is, but how well you know where it ends. A smaller circle with a clear edge is safer than a large one with a blurry edge.

Most expensive mistakes happen just outside the edge, where we know enough to feel confident and not enough to be careful. A good doctor may feel she can pick stocks, and a good engineer may feel sure about medicine. A useful habit is to say plainly, "That's outside what I know," and to find someone who knows it when the stakes are high. It's not an admission of weakness. It's a way of keeping your confidence tied to your actual knowledge.

# The map is not the territory
@src Korzybski, 1931

In 1931 the scholar Alfred Korzybski pointed out that a map isn't the land it describes. A subway map is very useful precisely because it leaves out almost everything: the real distances, the streets above, the hills. It shows you how to get from one station to another and nothing more.

But try to find a particular building with it, and you'll see how little it tells you. Every model, chart, theory and statistic is a map. It helps you think, and it simplifies on purpose. It goes wrong when you forget that there's a real, messier world behind it. When a number on a screen disagrees with what you see out the window, it's worth remembering which one is the territory, and which is only a drawing of it.

# The Pareto principle
@src Pareto, 1896; Juran

Around 1896 the economist Vilfredo Pareto noticed that roughly 80% of the land in Italy was owned by about 20% of the people. The same lopsided pattern has since been noticed in many other places.

The 80/20 split isn't a law, and the real numbers vary from one case to the next. Sometimes it's 90/10, and sometimes it's closer to 60/40. The lasting lesson is that outcomes are often unevenly spread. A handful of customers, bugs or tasks can account for most of the results, so it helps to find which ones. If you can tell which twenty percent matters most, you can put your effort there, and stop feeling guilty about the rest.

# The Lindy effect
@src Taleb, Antifragile (2012)
@conf mixed

The idea, popularized by Nassim Taleb, is that for things that don't age the way living creatures do, such as books, ideas and technologies, the longer they've survived, the longer they're likely to last. A book that has stayed in print for a hundred years is a better bet to still be read in another hundred than this week's bestseller.

Time acts as a filter. Whatever is still around has been tested by many generations, and has had many chances to be dropped. It's a rough rule, though, not a precise one. It doesn't apply to people, who really do wear out, or to things that physically decay. And a very old thing can still be replaced by something better. But as a first guess about which of two ideas will still matter in fifty years, the older one usually has the edge.

# Fermi estimation
@src Standard physics-teaching reference

The physicist Enrico Fermi was known for getting reasonable answers to questions that seem impossible. A classic example is: how many piano tuners work in Chicago? You don't look it up. Instead, you guess the population of the city, the share of households that have a piano, how often a piano gets tuned, and how many tunings one person can do in a day.

Each guess may be off, but the errors tend to partly cancel, because some are too high and some are too low. The answer usually lands within a factor of two or three, which is often good enough to decide something. The real skill is breaking a big, scary question into small pieces that you can estimate. Many "I have no idea" questions turn out to be questions where you have a rough idea about each part.

# Black swan
@src Taleb, The Black Swan (2007)

For centuries Europeans took it for granted that all swans were white, because every swan they had ever seen was. Then in 1697 the Dutch explorer Willem de Vlamingh saw black swans in Western Australia. Thousands of years of observation had been overturned by a single sighting.

Nassim Taleb uses the phrase for rare, high-impact events that nobody expected and that we then explain as if they had been obvious all along. It's a warning about how much weight to put on a record of calm. A long run of ordinary days can tell you how things usually go, but not what's possible. One surprise can overturn thousands of ordinary days, and the sensible response is to prepare for what you can't predict, as well as what you can.

# Antifragility
@src Taleb, Antifragile (2012)
@conf mixed

Some things are fragile and break under stress. Others are robust and simply hold up. Nassim Taleb's word "antifragile" describes things that get stronger when they are stressed, like muscles under a workout, bones under load, or immune systems exposed to germs. A teacup wants to be left alone, and a muscle wants to be challenged.

It's a helpful way of looking at systems, including our own habits. A life that avoids every difficulty may grow brittle, while one with small, manageable stresses can grow tougher. As a full scientific theory it's more a framework than something tested in detail, and it's easy to push too far, since not all stress helps. But the main idea, that some stress is useful, rings true in many places.

# Hormesis
@src Calabrese & Baldwin, 2003
@conf mixed

Many things are harmful in large amounts and beneficial in small ones, a pattern called hormesis. Exercise is an example. A moderate workout makes you stronger, while too much breaks you down. The dose makes the difference, and the effect isn't a straight line.

Some researchers suggest that fasting and sun exposure follow the same curve, with a bit helping and a lot hurting. In toxicology, scientists still debate whether low doses of things like poisons or radiation can actually help. So the idea is well supported in some areas, such as exercise, and still argued over in others. A cautious way to hold it is to remember that "more" isn't always "better," and also that "none" isn't always "safest."

# Unintended consequences
@src Merton, 1936

In 1936 the sociologist Robert Merton published a short paper listing why actions so often have effects nobody planned. He pointed to ignorance of how things interact, mistakes in analysis, and what he called the "imperious immediacy of interest," meaning that people focus on the short term and what they want right now.

Policies, products and personal decisions all show it. A new safety rule may make people feel safer and take more risks, and a tax meant to discourage something may simply move it elsewhere. The takeaway isn't to avoid acting, since doing nothing has consequences too. It's to spend a little time on the second and third effects, and to try things small before you scale them up. That way the surprises, when they come, are cheap.

# Parkinson's law
@src Parkinson, The Economist (1955)

"Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion." C. Northcote Parkinson wrote that line in a humorous 1955 essay in The Economist. He had noticed that the British Admiralty kept adding administrators even as the navy was getting smaller.

It rings true on a smaller scale. A task that you're given a week for tends to take a week, though it might have taken an afternoon. We find ways to use the time we're given, by polishing, rechecking or putting it off until the last day. A shorter deadline sometimes gets the same job done with less fuss. If you notice that your tasks are filling all the time you give them, try setting an earlier deadline, and see what happens.

# Hofstadter's law
@src Hofstadter, Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979)

Douglas Hofstadter wrote in his 1979 book Gödel, Escher, Bach: "It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law." He was describing how long it would take computers to play chess at a high level, and the prediction was wrong in just the way the law says.

The joke is a recursion, because the law applies to itself. But it reflects a serious habit known as the planning fallacy. Our estimates are usually too hopeful, because we picture the job going smoothly and leave out the delays, the interruptions and the things that nobody thought of. A good rule is to pad your first guess, and then look at how long similar jobs have actually taken. Even then, it's wise to leave a little extra.

# Brooks's law
@src Brooks, The Mythical Man-Month (1975)

Fred Brooks managed the huge IBM System/360 software project, and in his 1975 book The Mythical Man-Month he wrote that adding people to a late software project makes it later. It sounds backward, since more hands ought to speed things up.

But new people have to be taught, which takes time from the people who already know the work. And every extra person adds more lines of communication, so more of the day goes to coordinating. As Brooks put it, nine women can't make a baby in one month. Some work simply can't be divided up. When a project is behind, it's worth asking whether the job really splits into parts that can go in parallel, or whether the wait is built in.

# The Peter principle
@src Peter, 1969; Benson, Li & Shue, 2019

In 1969 Laurence Peter, an educator, wrote a funny book arguing that people in an organization get promoted for doing well until they reach a job they can't do well, and there they stay. He meant it as a joke, but he was pointing at something that many people have noticed.

In 2019 economists studied sales data on 53,000 workers at 214 firms and found a version of it. Companies tended to promote the best salespeople into management, where selling skills matter little. Good performance in one role doesn't guarantee it in the next, because the next job may call for different strengths. It's a reason for organizations to promote on the basis of what a job needs, and for each of us to ask whether we want the new role or just the title.

# Diminishing returns
@src Turgot, 1767

The first hour of study usually teaches you a lot. The tenth hour of the same day teaches far less. Each extra unit of effort, money or time tends to add a bit less than the one before it.

The French economist Anne-Robert-Jacques Turgot described it for farming in 1767: extra labor on a given plot yields smaller and smaller extra crops. Eventually another hour in the field adds almost nothing. It's a good reason to stop polishing at some point, and to spread your effort across more than one thing. The last five percent of perfection often costs more than the first ninety-five, and it's worth asking whether it's what you really need.

# The veil of ignorance
@src Rawls, A Theory of Justice (1971)

The philosopher John Rawls proposed a thought experiment in his 1971 book A Theory of Justice. Imagine you're designing the rules of a society, but you don't know who you'll be in it. You might be rich or poor, healthy or sick, talented or not, of any race or gender.

Rawls argued that you would then choose rules that are fair to everyone, since you could end up anywhere. It's a handy test for any rule or policy, big or small. Would you still endorse it if you didn't know which side of it you'd be on? A rule that only works for the person who wrote it is probably not a fair one. The veil is a way of borrowing other people's eyes for a minute.

# The trolley problem
@src Foot, 1967; Thomson, 1976

A runaway trolley is heading toward five people on a track. You can pull a lever to divert it to a side track, where it will kill one person instead. Philippa Foot posed the puzzle in 1967. In 1976 Judith Jarvis Thomson added a version where you'd have to push a large man off a footbridge to stop the trolley.

Most people say they'd pull the lever but wouldn't push the man, even though the numbers are the same. Philosophers still argue about why. Some say it matters that pushing uses a person as a tool, and others say our feelings are leading us astray. The puzzle doesn't have an agreed answer, which is part of its value. It shows that our moral instincts aren't simply arithmetic, and that we can't always explain them.

# The Ship of Theseus
@src Plutarch, Life of Theseus

The Greek writer Plutarch told of the ship in which the hero Theseus sailed home to Athens. The Athenians kept it for centuries, and as its planks rotted, they replaced them with new ones. Eventually every plank had been replaced.

Was it still the same ship? It's a puzzle about what makes something the same thing over time. Is it the material, the shape, or the history? Your own body is a version of it, since most of your cells are replaced over the years, and you still feel like you. The puzzle doesn't have a single answer, but it invites you to think about what you really mean by "the same," and about what it is that continues.

# Sagan's baloney detection kit
@src Sagan, The Demon-Haunted World (1995)

In his 1995 book The Demon-Haunted World, Carl Sagan gave readers a toolkit for spotting bad arguments. It includes seeking independent confirmation of the facts, considering more than one hypothesis, and asking whether a claim could in principle be proven false.

One of his tips is to avoid getting too attached to a hypothesis just because it's yours. He thought the tools should be used on yourself as well as on others, since the first person you're most likely to fool is you. The kit isn't meant to make you cynical. It's meant to give you a way to be open to surprising ideas without being gullible, so that you can say, with good reason, "That might be true, and here is how we could find out."

# The devil's advocate
@src Standard history of canonization

The phrase comes from a real job. When the Catholic Church was considering someone for sainthood, it appointed an official called the advocatus diaboli, who had to argue against the case and look for flaws. The role was ended by Pope John Paul II in 1983.

The idea is still useful. Before a big decision, ask someone to argue the opposite on purpose. If the plan survives a serious attack, you can trust it more, and if it doesn't, you've saved yourself a lot of trouble. The key is that the person is assigned the role, so they can disagree without being disagreeable. It's much easier for a team to hear "I'm playing devil's advocate" than "I think you're wrong."

# Red teaming
@src Standard security and military practice

A red team is a group whose job is to attack your plan, system or idea to see where it breaks. Militaries use them in war games, and companies use them to test their security by trying to break in. They are paid to be the enemy.

The reason it works is that the people who built something are blind to its flaws, because they know how it's supposed to work and so they use it the way it was meant to be used. An outsider doesn't, and will try things the builders never thought of. It helps to hire a friendly skeptic. You can use a small version of it at home. Ask someone to find the three biggest holes in your plan, and thank them when they do.

# The checklist
@src Pronovost et al., NEJM, 2006

Experts make mistakes, not because they don't know enough but because complicated jobs have too many steps to hold in your head. In 2006 a team at Johns Hopkins introduced a simple five-step checklist for inserting central lines in Michigan intensive care units, covering things like washing hands and cleaning the skin.

Within three months, the median rate of line infections fell from 2.7 per 1,000 catheter-days to zero. The doctors already knew every item on the list. The checklist simply made sure that nobody skipped one on a busy day. It's a humbling result and a hopeful one. A short list can beat expertise alone, and pilots, surgeons and builders have all come to rely on them.

# The Eisenhower matrix
@src Eisenhower, 1954 address, quoting J. Roscoe Miller

President Eisenhower, in a 1954 speech, quoted a college president who had said, "The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent." From that line, people later built a simple grid for sorting tasks into urgent or not, and important or not.

The most valuable box is the one for things that matter but have no deadline, like your health, your relationships and your long-term work. They're the easiest to put off, because nothing pushes you to do them. Meanwhile the urgent but unimportant things, like most emails and many calls, keep shouting. A week where you've done something from the important box, even a small thing, is usually a better week than one spent entirely on the urgent.

# The decision journal
@src Duke, Thinking in Bets (2018)

Before you know how something turned out, write down what you decided, what you expected, and why. It takes a few minutes. Later, when the result is in, you can go back and separate your reasoning from your luck.

You also can't tell yourself that you knew it all along, because the page says otherwise. It works directly against hindsight bias. Over months it shows you the kinds of decisions you tend to get right and the ones where you're too sure, which is hard to learn any other way. The entries don't have to be long. A line about what you chose, how confident you were and what you thought would happen is enough to be useful later.

# Resulting
@src Duke, Thinking in Bets (2018)

The poker player Annie Duke uses the word "resulting" for judging a decision purely by how it turned out. A sensible bet can lose, and a reckless one can win. In a game with luck in it, the outcome of a single hand tells you little about how well it was played.

If you only look at results, you'll learn the wrong lessons. You'll repeat a lucky blunder and abandon a good plan after one bad break. A better question is "given what I knew then, was it a good decision?" That's harder to answer, because it asks you to forget what you know now. But it's the one that actually helps you improve, and it makes it easier to be kind to yourself when a good decision goes badly.

# Satisficing
@src Simon, 1956; Schwartz et al., 2002
@conf mixed

In 1956 the economist and psychologist Herbert Simon pointed out that people rarely find the best option. They find one that is good enough and stop. He called that satisficing, a blend of "satisfy" and "suffice."

Later, Barry Schwartz and his colleagues found that people who try to find the best, whom they called maximizers, often feel less satisfied with their choices and more regret, even when they've done well by objective measures. Not every study agrees with that finding, so it isn't settled. But "good enough" is often a wise place to stop. When the stakes are small, the time spent hunting for the perfect choice can cost more than the difference it makes.

# The 10-10-10 rule
@src Welch, 10-10-10 (2009)

When you're stuck on a decision, imagine how you'll feel about it in ten minutes, ten months and ten years. The writer Suzy Welch popularized the method in 2009. It pulls your attention out of the heat of the moment and gives you a longer view.

The urge to send an angry message may feel overwhelming in ten minutes, and in ten months it may seem trivial. The choice between two job offers might look different over ten years than it does today. It's a quick way to check your priorities, and it works because it makes you consider the same choice from three different distances. The answers don't always agree, and that disagreement is often the useful part.

# Reversible versus irreversible choices
@src Bezos, 2015 shareholder letter

In a letter to shareholders, Jeff Bezos described two kinds of decisions. Some are one-way doors, which are hard or impossible to undo. Others are two-way doors: if you walk through and don't like what's on the other side, you can go back.

Most decisions are the second kind, and they can be made quickly, with less than complete information. Save your slow, careful thinking for the one-way doors, like a marriage, a big loan or a surgery. And try not to treat every choice as though it were permanent. Many of us spend as long choosing a restaurant as choosing a house, and the restaurant can be changed next week. Sorting your choices by whether they can be undone makes it easier to spend your worry where it counts.

# Noise in judgment
@src Kahneman, Sibony & Sunstein, Noise (2021)

We usually think about bias, which is when judgments are off in the same direction. Noise is something different. It's scatter, when different people, or the same person on different days, make different calls on identical cases. Think of four rifle shots that are spread all over a target instead of clustered together.

In a study at an insurance company, underwriters were asked to price the same case. Their quotes differed by 55% on average, and the executives had expected a difference of around 10%. The cost of that scatter is large, and it's hard to see, since each person only sees their own judgment. It's a good reason to rely on shared guidelines and to get independent opinions, and not to assume that one person's call is the call.

# The wisdom of crowds
@src Galton, Nature, 1907; Surowiecki, 2004

In 1907 the statistician Francis Galton visited a fair where people guessed the weight of an ox. He collected the 787 entries and found that the middle guess, 1,207 pounds, was within a percent of the true weight of 1,198. The crowd was better than almost any individual in it.

It works under certain conditions. People have to guess independently, and they have to bring different information and different mistakes, so that the mistakes cancel each other out. If they copy each other, or a loud voice sways the room, the effect disappears. That's why it's smart to collect opinions separately before discussing them. A group that talks first often ends up with one person's guess repeated many times.

# The outside view
@src Kahneman & Lovallo, 1993; Flyvbjerg, 2003

When we plan a project, we naturally focus on its details and how things will go this time. Daniel Kahneman and Dan Lovallo called that the inside view. It's the view from within your own story, where every step looks manageable.

The outside view asks a different question: how did similar projects turn out? Bent Flyvbjerg's research on large infrastructure projects found that most go over budget and take longer than planned. So before you trust your own timeline, look up how long comparable jobs have really taken. It can feel like an insult to your plan, as though yours were an ordinary case. But the odds are that it is, and the outside view is a reliable correction.

# Superforecasting
@src Tetlock & Gardner, Superforecasting (2015)

The political scientist Philip Tetlock ran forecasting tournaments in which thousands of ordinary people predicted world events. A small group did consistently better than the rest, and better than many professional experts. They were not geniuses.

What they did differently was a matter of habit. They broke big questions into smaller ones, thought in probabilities rather than yes or no, and updated their views as new evidence came in. They also kept score, so they could learn from their misses. The skill seems to come from ways of thinking, not from special talent, and that suggests it can be learned. Most of us make predictions all the time, and few of us ever check how well we do.

# The Brier score
@src Brier, 1950

How do you grade a weather forecaster who says "70% chance of rain"? If it rains, were they right? If it doesn't, were they wrong? A single forecast can't settle it, since the forecaster said it might go either way.

The meteorologist Glenn Brier devised a score in 1950 that fixes this. Over many forecasts, a person who says 70% should be right about seven times in ten. The score rewards being well calibrated, and it punishes being very sure and wrong. It's a standard tool for judging forecasts of all kinds, from weather to elections. You can use the idea on yourself by writing down your confidence in a prediction, and checking later whether your 70% claims came true about 70% of the time.

# Sturgeon's law
@src Sturgeon, 1950s

The science fiction writer Theodore Sturgeon is said to have been told, in the 1950s, that ninety percent of science fiction is crud. His reply was that ninety percent of everything is crud. The remark has been repeated ever since as a rule of thumb.

It's a joke, and it's also a useful reminder. When you judge a whole field by its average, you'll be disappointed, because most of any field is mediocre. Judge it by its best, and you'll find what draws people to it. The same goes for books, movies, music and even your own ideas. Most of what you produce won't be great, and the way to get something good is to make a lot and keep the best.

# The law of triviality
@src Parkinson, 1957

C. Northcote Parkinson imagined a committee with a short agenda. It approves a huge nuclear reactor in a couple of minutes, since few members understand it well enough to object. Then it spends an hour arguing about the bicycle shed, because everyone has an opinion about that.

People devote most attention to the matters they feel competent about, which are often the least important. Big decisions are hard to understand, so we let them pass, and small ones are easy to understand, so we fight over them. In a meeting, it's worth asking whether the time spent matches the stakes. A rough check is to see what you've talked about the longest, and whether it was what mattered most.

# The principle of charity
@src Wilson, 1959; Davidson

When you're about to disagree with someone, first read their words in the most sensible way they allow. Philosophers use the principle so that they argue against the strongest version of a view and not a weak one. Neil Wilson named it in 1959, and Donald Davidson developed it further.

It also saves a lot of needless fights in ordinary life. Assume the person is reasonable until the evidence shows otherwise, and you'll often find they meant something different from what you first heard. Even when they didn't, you'll have a better argument, because you've answered what they said, and not a careless version of it. Charity isn't weakness. It makes you harder to dismiss, because you got the other side right.

# The Overton window
@src Mackinac Center, 1990s

Joseph Overton was a policy analyst at a think tank in Michigan. He observed that at any given time there is a range of ideas the public will accept as reasonable, and that politicians generally stay inside it. Ideas outside the window seem too extreme to be taken seriously.

The window shifts over time. Activists often work by pushing at its edges so that what was unthinkable becomes debated, and then what was debated becomes accepted. It's a helpful way of seeing why some policies are discussed and others aren't, and why opinions that once seemed fringe can become ordinary. It also helps to remember that the window describes what is acceptable to say. It doesn't tell you what is true, or what is right.

# The law of the instrument
@src Kaplan, 1964; Maslow, 1966

If the only tool you have is a hammer, it's tempting to treat everything as if it were a nail. Abraham Kaplan wrote in 1964 that "give a small boy a hammer, and he will find that everything he encounters needs pounding." Abraham Maslow later made a similar remark, and the saying is often attached to his name.

Experts do the same. The person trained in one method tends to see problems that this method can solve, and to miss the ones it can't. It's worth asking, when you're stuck, what other tools exist, and whether you picked this one because it fits or only because it's the one in your hand. Having a few different ways to look at a problem is often worth more than being very good at one.

# The streetlight effect
@src Standard reference

A man is on his hands and knees under a streetlight, looking for his keys. A passerby asks where he lost them. "Over there in the park," he says, "but the light is better here." It's an old joke, and it describes a lot of real behavior.

We do something like it all the time. Researchers measure what's easy to measure, managers track what's easy to count, and we look for answers where it's convenient. What's easy to see gets more attention than what's important. If you notice that you're searching where it's easy, ask whether the thing you want is probably somewhere else. It may be dark over there, and you may need a flashlight, but that's where the keys are.

# Cargo cult science
@src Feynman, Caltech address, 1974

In a 1974 commencement speech at Caltech, Richard Feynman described islanders in the South Pacific who, after the war, built runways and wooden control towers and waited for cargo planes, as they had seen the military do. They copied the form of what they'd seen and none of the substance, so no planes came.

He said some research is like that. It looks like science, with the experiments, the charts and the language, but lacks a kind of utter honesty, including reporting anything that might show you're wrong. The point is that following the outward steps of a method isn't the same as practicing it. What matters is the willingness to find out you're mistaken, and to say so. That applies to anyone trying to learn what's true, not only to scientists.

# You are the easiest person to fool
@src Feynman, Caltech address, 1974

In the same 1974 Caltech speech, Richard Feynman said: "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool." It's the heart of how he thought science should work.

You have to be harder on your own ideas than you'd be on anyone else's, because you want them to be true. Wanting is what makes us careless. In practice that means looking for how you might be wrong first, checking the results that disagree with you, and being glad when you find out. It's an uncomfortable habit and a freeing one, because once you stop needing to be right, you're free to get closer to what's true.
