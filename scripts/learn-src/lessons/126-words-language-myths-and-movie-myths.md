# Does the Chinese word for crisis mean opportunity?
@series talk
@src Chinese linguistics; the word weiji

Speakers and motivational writers often say that the Chinese word for crisis is made of two characters, one for danger and one for opportunity. It makes a nice thought. It is not accurate. The word is weiji. The first character, wei, does mean danger. But the second, ji, does not mean opportunity in this word.

Its sense here is closer to the point at which something changes, a turning point or the crucial moment when things could go either way. That is, in fact, the original meaning of the English word crisis, which comes from a Greek word for a decision or a turning point, used by doctors for the moment in an illness when the patient would recover or die.

The character ji can mean opportunity in other words, which is where the mix-up comes from. But in weiji it does not. The idea has been repeated by many speakers, including politicians, because it is useful for a speech. A word does not teach a lesson by breaking up into its parts, and the meaning of a word is not always the sum of its pieces.

# The lisping king who did not change a language
@series talk
@src Historical linguistics of Spanish; Peter of Castile

In Spain, the letter z, and c before e or i, is pronounced as a th sound, as in thin, in most of the country. A popular explanation says that a Spanish king had a lisp, and the courtiers imitated him, so that all Spain came to speak that way. It is a good story, but not a true one.

Only one Spanish king is known to have had a lisp, Peter of Castile, who ruled in the fourteenth century. The sound that is now heard did not arise until about two centuries after his death. It grew from a gradual change in the sounds of the language, in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, as a number of sibilant sounds merged and shifted.

In most of Latin America, and in parts of southern Spain, these letters are pronounced as s, which is another result of the same changes. The explanation by a lisping king shows how people want a simple cause for a complicated change. Languages change by drift among many speakers, and royal habits are seldom the cause.

# Sign languages are not one language
@series talk
@src Linguistics of sign languages; International Sign

Many people think that sign language is the same all over the world, a kind of universal language of the hands. It is not. Each country generally has its own native sign language, and some countries have more than one. American Sign Language and British Sign Language, for example, are quite different, and a user of one cannot understand a user of the other, though the two countries speak English.

American Sign Language is related to French Sign Language, and is a result of the school for the deaf that was founded in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1817, by Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet, with Laurent Clerc, a deaf teacher from Paris.

Sign languages are full languages, with their own grammar, and they are not versions of the spoken language of the country. There is one form that is used at international meetings, called International Sign. It is a pidgin, a simplified system that is put together by people who do not share a language, and it does not have the full complexity of a natural one. The idea that signs are universal has done a disservice to the deaf, because it suggests that they are only gestures.

# Three word origins that are wrong
@src Etymology of gringo and kangaroo; Feng Menglong, Stories to Awaken the World, 1627

The word gringo is often said to date from the Mexican-American War, from 1846 to 1848, when Mexicans supposedly shouted green go home at American soldiers in green uniforms. It did not. The word was used earlier, and it just meant a foreigner. It is probably a corruption of the Spanish word griego, which means Greek, as in our saying that it is Greek to me.

Kangaroo is said to come from a misunderstanding, when Captain James Cook asked a local man what the animal was, and was told, I don't know. It is not true. The word comes from the Guugu Yimithirr word gangurru, which names the eastern gray kangaroo.

The saying may you live in interesting times is said to be an ancient Chinese curse. There is no known Chinese equivalent. The nearest Chinese expression is a line from a 1627 collection of stories by Feng Menglong, Stories to Awaken the World, which says that it is better to be a dog in times of peace than a human in times of chaos. It is a close idea, but a different saying. Each of these tales has a cute explanation, and each fails when one looks at the sources.

# Four rules of English that are not rules
@series talk
@src Dictionaries; usage manuals; linguistic studies of AAVE

Irregardless is not a word, say many teachers. But it appears in many dictionaries, along with other nonstandard, slang and colloquial words. It has been in use for over a century. It is considered nonstandard, but it is a word, since people use it and know what it means.

It is said that you must never end a sentence with a preposition. The rule came from an attempt to make English follow Latin, where a preposition cannot come at the end. Linguists agree that ending a sentence with one is a natural part of English. Modern style guides also allow the split infinitive, as in to boldly go.

A fourth is about African American Vernacular English, in which speakers use be in sentences such as he be working. Some think that this replaces is with be in all tenses, with no change in meaning. In fact it marks a habitual aspect, an action that goes on regularly or over time. He be working means that he is usually working, which standard English does not mark with a single word. It is a rule of a grammar that has its own system, and not a mistake.

# Where 420, Xmas and crap came from
@series words
@src San Rafael High School, 1971; chi-rho symbol; Oxford English Dictionary

The number 420 is linked to marijuana. A popular story says that it comes from a police code, or from section 420 of the California penal code. It does not. That section is about obstructing access to public land. The use of 420 began in 1971 at San Rafael High School, in California, where a group of students would meet at 4:20 in the afternoon to go and smoke.

Xmas is said to be a modern way of taking Christ out of Christmas. It is not. The X stands for the Greek letter chi, the first letter of Christos, the Greek for Christ, and it has been used as a symbol since the fourth century, in the chi-rho sign. In English, the X was used as a scribe's abbreviation for Christ as early as the year 1021.

The word crap is said to come from the name of Thomas Crapper, the plumber. It does not. It comes ultimately from Medieval Latin crappa, which meant chaff or husks. Crapper was a real plumber who did much to make the flush toilet popular, but he did not give his name to the word. His name may have helped the word to survive, at a time when his firm's name was on many toilets.

# Ye olde, blue laws and the rule of thumb
@series words
@src Early printing history; thorn letter; etymology of rule of thumb

The sign for Ye Olde Shoppe is a classic of tourist places. It suggests that the old word for the was ye, and was pronounced yee. This was never the case. The word was always the, spelled with a letter called thorn, which looked like a y. Thorn was a letter of Old and Middle English that stood for the sound that is now spelled th.

When early printing presses arrived from Europe, they did not have the letter thorn, and printers used the closest shape they had, the letter y, often with a small e written above it. The result looked like ye, but people went on saying the. The ye of the signs is an echo of that printing habit.

Two other phrases have false explanations. A rule of thumb is said to come from an old English law that let a man beat his wife with a stick no thicker than his thumb. No such law is known. The term comes from the seventeenth century, when workers in various trades measured things by comparing them with the width or length of a thumb. And blue laws, which ban certain activities on Sundays, are not named for blue paper. The truth behind a phrase is often plainer than the tale.

# The film that was not the first in color
@series history
@src Histories of cinema; Kinemacolor; Lotte Reiniger

The Wizard of Oz, in 1939, is often called the first color film, because of the moment when the picture changes from black and white to color. It was not. Color film processes were used much earlier. Kinemacolor was used from 1902, and the first Technicolor process was introduced in 1917. Many silent films were also tinted or colored by hand.

Likewise, Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs, from 1937, is often called the first full-length animated film. It was not. The first is thought to have been El Apostol, a silent Argentine film of 1917, which used cutout animation and is now lost. The oldest that survives is The Adventures of Prince Achmed, a German film of 1926, also made with cutouts, by Lotte Reiniger. Snow White was the first full-length film made using cels, the sheets of clear plastic on which animators draw.

Casablanca is another. It is said that Ronald Reagan was considered for the part of Rick, played by Humphrey Bogart. An early press release mentioned Reagan, but the studio already knew that he would be in the army. The producer had always wanted Bogart. Many film legends are of the kind that make a claim of a first or an almost, and in each case the real story is more interesting.

# The movie that does not exist
@series mind
@conf mixed
@src Mandela effect; Kazaam, 1996; Sinbad on TNT, 1994

Many people clearly remember a film from the 1990s called Shazaam, in which the comedian Sinbad played a genie who helped two children. They remember the poster, and some remember seeing it on video. But no such film was ever made. It is a favorite example of the Mandela effect, a name for shared false memories, taken from the many people who believed that Nelson Mandela died in prison in the 1980s.

The film that they are thinking of may be Kazaam, which came out in 1996. It starred the basketball player Shaquille O'Neal as a genie. The two names are close. Another possible source is that in 1994, Sinbad hosted a showing of the 1977 film Sinbad and the Eye of the Tiger on the cable channel TNT, and he was dressed in a genie costume.

The effect shows how memory works. It does not replay a record, but rebuilds a story from pieces, and fills the gaps with things that seem likely. When many people make the same mistake, it may be because the same pieces are available to all of them. A strong feeling of remembering is not proof that the thing happened.
