# The girl who was found without a first language
@conf mixed
@src Curtiss, Fromkin and colleagues, UCLA, 1970s

In November 1970 the authorities in Los Angeles found a girl of thirteen who had been kept in isolation for most of her life, and who had almost no language. She was admitted to Children's Hospital on November 4, and she became known by the pseudonym Genie. A team of UCLA linguists, led by Victoria Fromkin, with her student Susan Curtiss, spent years testing and teaching her, and recording how she learned.

Genie's vocabulary grew a great deal. Her grammar did not. She learned many words, and she understood more than she could say, but her sentences stayed simple, and she did not master the rules for putting them together as children do. Some linguists saw this as support for the idea that there is a critical period for language, which ends around puberty. Others said that her abuse and neglect made the case impossible to interpret. It is a sad case, and also an inconclusive one.

# The word at the pump
@src American Foundation for the Blind; Keller, The Story of My Life, 1903

Helen Keller was born in Alabama in 1880. At nineteen months she became ill with a fever, and she lost both her sight and her hearing. For years she lived with no way to share her thoughts, except a few signs, and she had angry outbursts. In March 1887 a young teacher, Anne Sullivan, who had herself been partly blind, came to the Keller home in Tuscumbia, to teach her. She spelled words into the child's hand, but at first Helen only copied them.

On April 5, 1887, when Helen was six, Sullivan took her to the water pump in the yard. As water ran over one of Helen's hands, she spelled w-a-t-e-r into the other. Helen understood, suddenly, that the movements in her palm stood for the cool stuff that was flowing. She wanted to know the names of everything. That day she learned about thirty words. She went on to graduate from college and write books.

# Seeing with the skin
@src Bach-y-Rita et al., Nature, 1969

In 1969 the neuroscientist Paul Bach-y-Rita and his colleagues published a letter in Nature about a strange device. A television camera sent its picture to a grid of vibrating pins, twenty by twenty, which pressed against the skin of the back of a blind person. A bright spot in the picture became a buzz at the matching spot on the skin. With practice, the blind users could tell shapes, and say where objects and even people stood.

Their reports said that after some hours, users stopped feeling a pattern of buzzing on the back, and began to perceive objects in front of them, as one does with the eyes. Bach-y-Rita argued that we see with the brain, and not just with the eyes. If the brain receives information in an orderly way, it can learn to use it. The idea, called sensory substitution, has led to devices that give blind people information through the tongue or the ear.

# One worm, three hundred and two neurons
@src White, Southgate, Thomson & Brenner, 1986; Shapson-Coe et al., Science, 2024

A connectome is a complete map of the connections between the nerve cells of a brain. The first one was of a tiny roundworm, Caenorhabditis elegans, which has only 302 neurons. In the 1970s and 1980s Sydney Brenner and his colleagues sliced a worm into thousands of ultrathin sections, photographed them with an electron microscope, and traced every cell by hand. The paper appeared in 1986. It showed some thousands of connections between the cells.

The map did not explain everything about how the worm behaves, and some critics said that it told us less than hoped. In 2024 a team from Harvard and Google published a map of a piece of the human brain, a cubic millimeter of cortex, a speck smaller than a grain of rice. It contained about 57,000 cells and 150 million connections. A whole human brain has about 86 billion neurons, so a complete map is still far away.

# Hearing from a wire
@src Cochlear implant history, 1957 to 1990

In 1957 two French doctors in Paris, André Djourno and Charles Eyriès, placed a wire next to the hearing nerve of a deaf man, and sent an electrical signal into it. He heard noises, but not speech, and the device failed after a few weeks. They stopped, but their report reached an American ear surgeon, William House, in Los Angeles. In 1961 House and an engineer, John Doyle, implanted a device in a patient.

Many doubted that it would work, because the ear has thousands of delicate hair cells, and a few wires could not replace them. But in the 1970s and 1980s the devices improved, and in 1984 the American FDA approved the House and 3M single-channel implant for adults. In about 1990 it approved multichannel devices for children. Today cochlear implants let deaf people hear speech. The brain learns to make sense of the electrical signals, which sound strange at first.

# A slime mold in a maze
@src Nakagaki, Yamada & Tóth, Nature, 2000; Tero et al., Science, 2010

The slime mold Physarum polycephalum is a single cell that can grow to cover a dinner plate, and that has no brain and no nerves. It grows as a network of tubes, looking for food. In 2000 the Japanese researcher Toshiyuki Nakagaki and his colleagues put a piece of it in a small maze, with food at two ends. At first the slime spread through all the paths. After some hours it had withdrawn from the dead ends, and kept the shortest route between the foods.

In 2010 Atsushi Tero and colleagues put pieces of food on a map in the positions of cities around Tokyo, and let the slime mold connect them. Bright light, which the slime mold avoids, stood for mountains and lakes. The network it grew was comparable in efficiency, resilience and cost to the real Tokyo rail system. It is wrong to say that the slime mold thinks. But a simple rule, of strengthening the tubes that carry the most flow, can find good solutions.

# The crow that bent a wire
@src Weir, Chappell & Kacelnik, Science, 2002

New Caledonian crows are known for making tools from twigs and leaves, which they use to get insects out of holes. In 2002 the researchers Alex Weir, Jackie Chappell and Alex Kacelnik, at Oxford, were testing two crows, a male, Abel, and a female, Betty. They gave them a small bucket of food at the bottom of a tube, and a hooked wire and a straight wire to fish it out. Abel took the hooked wire, and left the straight wire.

Betty, left with the straight wire, wedged one end in some tape, and bent it into a hook, and lifted the bucket. She did it again in nine of ten trials. She had never been given wire before, and had never watched anyone bend it. Later work found that crows in the wild also bend material, so she may have been using a routine that she already knew. But Betty's skill, with a new material, stood out. She died in 2005.

# The sound that is hard to bear
@src Kumar et al., Current Biology, 2017

For some people, the sound of someone chewing, or breathing, or tapping a pen, provokes an intense reaction, of anger or disgust, which they find hard to control. It is called misophonia, which means hatred of sound. For years it was thought to be a matter of temperament. In 2017 Sukhbinder Kumar and his colleagues at Newcastle University used a brain scanner on people who had misophonia, and on people who did not.

When the people with misophonia heard their trigger sounds, mostly sounds of eating, a part of the brain called the anterior insula responded far more strongly. This is a hub of the network that decides what is important. It was more connected, too, with areas for emotion and memory. Their heart rates and skin responses rose, and the activity of the insula accounted for these effects. The findings suggest that it is a real condition of the brain, and not just a lack of patience.
