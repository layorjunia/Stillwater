# One man and a dictionary of everything
@series words
@src Johnson, A Dictionary of the English Language, 1755

In June 1746 a group of London booksellers hired Samuel Johnson to write a dictionary of English. They were unhappy with the ones on the market, and they agreed to pay him 1,500 guineas. Johnson said that he could finish in three years. It took him seven, and the book was published on April 15, 1755.

He did the work almost alone. With only a few clerks to copy out passages, he read widely, marked sentences in books that showed how words were used, and wrote the definitions himself. Instead of just listing words, he supported them with quotations from writers such as Shakespeare, Milton and Dryden. Many of his definitions are still quoted. Oats, he wrote, was a grain which in England is generally given to horses, but in Scotland supports the people.

The dictionary was considered the best in English until the Oxford English Dictionary was finished, 173 years later. The biographer Walter Jackson Bate called it one of the greatest feats of scholarship ever performed by one person working under such disadvantages. Johnson also described his own trade. A lexicographer, he wrote, was a writer of dictionaries, a harmless drudge.

# The surgeon who wrote dictionary entries in an asylum
@series words
@src Oxford English Dictionary history; William Chester Minor, 1834 to 1920

When the editor James Murray began work on the Oxford English Dictionary, he asked the public to help. Volunteers read books and sent in slips of paper, each with a word and a quotation showing how it was used. One of the best contributors was Dr. William Chester Minor, who sent in thousands. By the popular account, Murray knew him at first only by an address in Berkshire, England.

Minor was born in 1834 in Ceylon, now Sri Lanka, to missionary parents from New England. He studied medicine at Yale, and served as an army surgeon in the American Civil War. He later moved to England. In 1872, suffering from delusions, he shot and killed a man whom he believed had broken into his room. He was committed to a secure hospital for the insane, and he stayed there until 1910.

In the hospital he had a large library of old books, and used it to read systematically and send in quotations. After protests about his treatment, Winston Churchill, then the home secretary, ordered him sent back to the United States in 1910. He died in a hospital in Connecticut in 1920. The story was told in a book by Simon Winchester, The Professor and the Madman.

# The poet who helped make English respectable
@series words
@src Chaucer, c. 1343 to 1400; Hoccleve; Westminster Abbey

Geoffrey Chaucer was born in London, probably in the early 1340s, the son of a wine merchant. He was a man of public life, who served as a courtier, a diplomat, an official in the customs, and a member of Parliament for Kent. He is best known for The Canterbury Tales, a collection of stories told by pilgrims on the way to a shrine.

He wrote in English at a time when French was the language of the court, and Latin the language of learning. By showing that English could carry poetry, he helped make it a language fit for literature. A younger poet, Thomas Hoccleve, called him the first finder of our fair language. Almost two thousand English words are first recorded in manuscripts of his works.

He also wrote a scientific work, A Treatise on the Astrolabe, for his ten-year-old son Lewis, explaining how to use the instrument. When he died in 1400 he was buried in Westminster Abbey, in a place that later became known as Poets' Corner. He was the first writer to be buried there, and many have been laid there since.

# The emperor who turned away from war
@series history
@src Edicts of Ashoka; Maurya Empire

Ashoka was the third ruler of the Maurya Empire, and became emperor of Magadha in India in about 268 BC. His realm grew to cover most of the Indian subcontinent, from what is now Afghanistan to what is now Bangladesh, with its capital at Pataliputra. In about 260 BC, in the eighth year of his rule, he conquered the region of Kalinga after a brutal war.

According to his own edicts, which were carved on rocks and pillars, he was moved by the suffering of the war. He turned to a policy of dhamma, which means right conduct, and which includes kindness to others and tolerance of different beliefs. He is believed to have drawn gradually to Buddhism, and Buddhist legends say that he built many stupas and supported missionaries.

Ashoka was nearly forgotten as a historical figure, until scholars in the nineteenth century deciphered the Brahmi script of his inscriptions. Today he is regarded as one of the greatest of Indian rulers. India's national emblem is based on the lion capital that stood on one of his pillars, and the wheel on the national flag is the wheel of Ashoka.

# Twenty-eight men and a ship in the ice
@series history
@src Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition, 1914 to 1917; Shackleton

In 1914 the explorer Ernest Shackleton set out on a plan to be the first to cross the continent of Antarctica on foot. After Roald Amundsen reached the South Pole in 1911, it was the one great goal that was left. Shackleton's ship Endurance sailed into the Weddell Sea, but before it could land the men, it became trapped in the pack ice.

The ship drifted north with the ice through the Antarctic winter of 1915. In the end the ice crushed it, and it sank, leaving the crew of 28 men stranded on the ice. They camped on the floes, and later took to three small boats and reached remote Elephant Island. Shackleton and five others then sailed a lifeboat, the James Caird, about 800 miles across the stormy ocean to South Georgia, and crossed the island's mountains to reach a whaling station.

He then led the rescue of the men on Elephant Island, and they were taken off in August 1916. Every man of the Endurance crew survived. The expedition did not achieve its goal, but it is remembered as one of the great stories of leadership and endurance. The wreck of the Endurance was found in 2022 on the sea floor.

# The man who got to the Pole first
@series history
@src Amundsen, 1872 to 1928; South Pole, December 14, 1911

Roald Amundsen was born in Norway in 1872, into a family of shipowners and captains. As a young man he served on a Belgian Antarctic expedition in 1897 to 1899, and from 1903 to 1906 he led the first expedition to sail through the Northwest Passage, in a small sloop called the Gjøa.

In 1910 he left Norway on the ship Fram, and landed in Antarctica in January 1911. His party set up a base at the Bay of Whales, and laid out depots of food along the route. He left for the Pole in October, with five men, skis and sledges pulled by dogs. They reached the South Pole on December 14, 1911. The British party led by Robert Falcon Scott arrived about a month later, found the Norwegian tent, and died on the way back.

Amundsen next turned to the Arctic. On May 12, 1926, he and fifteen others in the airship Norge became the first explorers confirmed to have reached the North Pole. In June 1928 he disappeared while flying to look for the crew of another airship, the Italia. His body was never found. He is often described as the first person to have reached both poles.

# The flyer who vanished over the Pacific
@series history
@src Amelia Earhart, 1897 to 1937

Amelia Earhart was born in Atchison, Kansas, on July 24, 1897. She learned to fly in the early 1920s, when few women did. In 1928 she was the first woman to cross the Atlantic by airplane, though as a passenger, and the fame brought her publicity. In 1932 she flew the Atlantic alone and without stopping, the first woman to do so, and received the Distinguished Flying Cross.

She wrote best-selling books about flying, promoted commercial air travel, and helped to found the Ninety-Nines, an organization of women pilots. In 1937 she set out to be the first woman to fly around the world. On July 2, 1937, she and her navigator, Fred Noonan, disappeared over the Pacific Ocean in a twin-engine Lockheed Electra, on a long leg to tiny Howland Island.

The U.S. Navy and Coast Guard searched for them, but no trace of them was found. She was declared dead on January 5, 1939. Many theories have been put forward and searches have gone on for decades, but what happened is still not known. Her disappearance has made her a cultural symbol, and has overshadowed her real achievements as a pilot and as an advocate for women in aviation.

# The man who weighed the Earth
@series sci
@src Cavendish, Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society, 1798

How much does the Earth weigh? In 1797 and 1798 the English scientist Henry Cavendish found a way to answer, using a machine designed by a clergyman and geologist, John Michell, who died in 1793 before he could use it. The machine was a torsion balance, a six-foot wooden rod hung from a wire, with a small lead ball of about 1.6 pounds on each end.

Cavendish placed two very large lead balls, of about 348 pounds, close to the small ones. The gravitational pull between them was extremely faint, and it twisted the wire by only about 0.16 of a degree. He measured the twist with a telescope, from outside the room, so that his own body and the heat from it would not disturb the apparatus.

From the force he could calculate the density of the Earth, and from that its mass. He found that the Earth is about five and a half times as dense as water. It was the first time that the force of gravity between objects had been measured in a laboratory. The experiment is often said to have weighed the Earth. It also gave the first accurate value for the gravitational constant, though Cavendish did not state it that way.

# The astronomer who noticed the sky was slipping
@series sci
@src Hipparchus, about 190 to 120 BC; Ptolemy's Almagest

Hipparchus was a Greek astronomer born in Nicaea, in what is now Turkey, about 190 BC. He worked as an observer between about 162 and 127 BC, partly on the island of Rhodes. He is often called the greatest astronomical observer of antiquity.

He made the first tables of trigonometry, which let one work out the lengths of the sides of a triangle from its angles. He produced the first known star catalog in the Western world, and he built models for the motion of the Sun and Moon that were accurate enough to predict eclipses. To do it, he used records kept by the Babylonians over centuries.

His most famous discovery was precession. By comparing his own measurements of the position of stars to those of astronomers of the generation before, such as Timocharis, he found that the points on the sky where the seasons begin were slowly shifting. The Earth's axis wobbles like a spinning top, in a cycle of about 26,000 years. Hipparchus found it two thousand years before anyone could explain it. A French astronomer, Delambre, called him the father of astronomy in 1817.

# The man who fell in a well, and made money
@series people
@src Diogenes Laertius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers; Plato, Theaetetus; Aristotle, Politics

Thales of Miletus lived on the coast of what is now Turkey, about 600 BC. He was one of the Seven Sages of ancient Greece, and is often named as the first philosopher. His claim was that everything in the world is made from one basic substance, and that it is water. The point was less the answer than that he looked for a natural explanation, and not a story about the gods.

He was said to have measured the height of the pyramids, and to have predicted a solar eclipse. A theorem about triangles in a circle is named for him. According to Herodotus, he helped the army of the king of Lydia to cross a river by digging a channel to divide it. Very little of what is told of him can be checked, since our main source, Diogenes Laertius, wrote some eight hundred years later.

Two stories show the two sides of the thinker. Plato told that Thales fell into a well while he was looking up at the stars, and a servant girl laughed that he was so eager to know the sky that he could not see what lay at his feet. Aristotle told another. Thales foresaw a good olive harvest, rented all the olive presses, and made a fortune, to show that a philosopher could be rich if he wanted.
