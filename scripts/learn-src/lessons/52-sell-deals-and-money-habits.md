# The fixed pie
@src Bazerman & Neale, 1983; Thompson & Hastie, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 1990

Many people go into a negotiation assuming that whatever the other side gains, they lose. In the early 1980s Max Bazerman and Margaret Neale called this the fixed-pie bias. In 1990 Leigh Thompson and Reid Hastie tested it. Pairs of students negotiated over several issues, such as the price, the delivery time and the financing of a purchase. They were then asked what they thought the other side wanted.

Many of the students believed that their interests were directly opposed to the other's, when in fact the two sides valued the issues differently. One cared more about speed, and the other about price. Pairs that realized this could trade, giving up what mattered less to them for what mattered more, and both came out ahead. Asking questions about what the other side values is the key to making the pie bigger.

# Why bids come at the last second
@src Roth & Ockenfels, American Economic Review, 2002

On eBay, auctions run for a set time, and many bidders wait until the last few seconds to place their bids. This is called sniping. In 2002 Alvin Roth and Axel Ockenfels compared eBay with Amazon, which at the time extended an auction if bids came in at the end. On eBay, a large share of bids came in the final minutes. On Amazon, that was much less common.

Sniping makes sense on eBay. If you bid early, you may start a bidding war with other people, or show that an item is valuable. If you bid at the last moment, others have no time to respond. The study shows how the rules of an auction shape the way people bid. A small difference in the way an auction ends changes the strategy completely.

# The auction where honesty pays
@src Vickrey, Journal of Finance, 1961

In a normal sealed-bid auction, the highest bidder wins and pays what they bid. This gives people a reason to bid less than the item is worth to them, to make a profit. William Vickrey proposed a different rule in 1961. The highest bidder still wins, but pays only the amount of the second-highest bid.

Under this rule, the best strategy is to bid exactly what the item is worth to you. If you bid more, you risk winning at a price that is too high. If you bid less, you may lose an item that you could have had at a good price. Your own bid never sets the price you pay. Vickrey won the Nobel Prize in 1996 for this and related ideas. Versions of his auction are used today, for example in the way that online advertising space is sold.

# The dollar auction
@src Shubik, Journal of Conflict Resolution, 1971

The economist Martin Shubik invented a simple game in 1971. A dollar bill is auctioned off, and the highest bidder wins it. The twist is that the second-highest bidder also has to pay what they bid, and gets nothing. Any increase on a bid seems sensible, but the result is not.

When the bids reach a dollar, the second bidder, who has offered ninety-five cents, is about to lose ninety-five cents. By bidding a dollar and five cents, he will lose only a nickel, so he raises. Then the other bidder, who has offered a dollar, faces the same choice. People in classrooms often bid several dollars for a dollar bill. The game illustrates how escalation can trap people when they have already invested, and how hard it is to stop once you've started.

# Knee-deep in the big muddy
@src Staw, Organizational Behavior and Human Performance, 1976

Why do people keep putting money into a project that is failing? In 1976 Barry Staw asked business students to play the role of a manager in a company. First, they chose where to invest funds in one of two divisions. Then they were told that the investment had done poorly, and they were asked to make a second round of investment.

The students who had made the first decision themselves invested significantly more in the failing division than students who were told that someone else had made it. Having committed to a course, they sought to justify it. This is called escalation of commitment. It's a pattern seen in business, in politics and in personal life. A useful question is whether you would make the same choice if you were starting afresh today.

# One question to predict growth?
@src Reichheld, Harvard Business Review, 2003; Keiningham et al., Journal of Marketing, 2007
@conf contested

In 2003 Fred Reichheld proposed that a company could track its health with a single question. How likely are you to recommend us to a friend or colleague, on a scale from zero to ten? The score, called the net promoter score, subtracts the share who answered zero to six from those who gave nine or ten. Reichheld claimed that it was the best predictor of growth.

The idea was adopted by thousands of companies. But when researchers tested it with other data, they found that it was no better at predicting growth than other measures of satisfaction. Keiningham and his colleagues published one such study in 2007. The score is easy to collect and to understand, and that may be why it's popular. As a single magic number, it has not held up well.

# Make it easy
@src Dixon, Freeman & Toman, Harvard Business Review, 2010
@conf mixed

What makes customers loyal? Many companies try to delight them with extra service. In 2010 Matthew Dixon and his colleagues at a business research group surveyed tens of thousands of customers about their experiences with customer service. They found that going above and beyond made little difference to loyalty.

What seemed to matter more was how much effort the customer had to make. People who had to contact the company several times, repeat their story or switch channels were more likely to become disloyal. The authors proposed a measure called the customer effort score. The work comes from a consulting firm and is not a controlled experiment, so it should be taken with some care. But it fits a common sense idea. People value a problem solved with less hassle.

# When asking costs more for women
@src Bowles, Babcock & Lai, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 2007

Women are less likely to negotiate for higher pay than men. In 2007 Hannah Riley Bowles, Linda Babcock and Lei Lai studied one reason. They asked people to read about job candidates who either asked for a higher salary or accepted the first offer, and to say how much they would want to work with each one.

Male evaluators penalized women who asked, finding them less nice and more demanding, and did not penalize men for asking. Female evaluators penalized both women and men who asked. So the reluctance has a basis in how people react. The authors found that women did better when they gave a reason that fit with their relationships at work, such as that they were helping the company. The problem is the bias, and not the women.

# The boomerang effect
@src Schultz, Nolan, Cialdini, Goldstein & Griskevicius, Psychological Science, 2007

In 2007 Wesley Schultz and his colleagues delivered cards to households in California that showed how much electricity each had used, compared with the average in the neighborhood. Households that used more than average cut back in the following weeks. But households that used less than average used more, and drifted up toward the norm.

It was a boomerang effect. Telling people what others do pulls them toward the average, in both directions. In a second version, the cards had a smiling face for households that used less than average, and a frowning face for those that used more. Now the low users stayed low. The smile told them that what they did was good, which kept them from sliding. A norm message works best when it also says what is approved.

# The energy report
@src Allcott, Journal of Public Economics, 2011

After the work on social norms, a company called Opower began to send millions of households a report that compared their energy use with that of their neighbors. In 2011 the economist Hunt Allcott studied the results across several hundred thousand homes in many places. The households were randomly chosen, so the effect of the reports could be measured.

On average, the households that received the reports used about two percent less energy than the others. The effect is modest, but it's across many households, and it was achieved at low cost. It lasted for as long as the reports continued to arrive, and then faded slowly. It shows how a small nudge, applied widely, can have large effects, and it also shows that a nudge isn't a magic wand.

# Save more tomorrow
@src Thaler & Benartzi, Journal of Political Economy, 2004

Most people say that they would like to save more for retirement, but few do. In 2004 Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi designed a plan that works with human nature. Employees are asked to commit now to saving a larger share of their pay in the future, with the increase tied to future pay raises. They can opt out at any time.

In the first company to use it, the employees who joined the plan raised their savings rate from about three and a half percent to over thirteen percent in three and a half years. The plan works because it avoids the pain of cutting take-home pay now, and it uses inertia to keep people in. It's one of the best-known examples of the use of behavioral economics in a real setting.

# Locking up your own money
@src Ashraf, Karlan & Yin, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2006

Many people have trouble saving, since cash is easy to spend. In 2006 Nava Ashraf, Dean Karlan and Wesley Yin offered a savings account with a twist to customers of a bank in the Philippines. In the account, they could not withdraw their money until they reached a goal they had set, or a date that they chose. The restrictions were voluntary.

About a quarter of those offered the account took it up. After a year, those who were offered it had saved substantially more than a comparison group. The people who chose to restrict themselves seemed to know that they would spend the money otherwise. It's an example of a commitment device. Tying your own hands may be a sign of wisdom, and it works best for people who know their weaknesses.

# Playing with the house's money
@src Thaler & Johnson, Management Science, 1990

In 1990 Richard Thaler and Eric Johnson asked people to make a series of gambles. If they had won money earlier, they were more willing to take risks than if they had started from nothing. They seemed to feel that the money from the earlier win was not entirely theirs, and so it was less painful to lose.

The idea is called the house money effect, after the casino expression. It has been seen in lab studies and in real gambling. A gambler who is ahead may take riskier bets, and a person with a windfall may spend it more freely than they would earned income. Money is money, wherever it came from. But in our minds we put it in different accounts, and they have different rules.

# Why we sell winners and keep losers
@src Odean, Journal of Finance, 1998

If you own two stocks, one that has gone up and another that has gone down, which are you more likely to sell? In 1998 Terrance Odean studied the trading records of ten thousand customers of a discount brokerage firm. He found that investors were much more likely to sell stocks that had gone up than stocks that had gone down.

This is called the disposition effect. Selling a winner lets us feel the pleasure of a gain, and selling a loser makes us admit a loss. But it's not good for returns. The stocks that investors sold went on to do better than the stocks that they kept. There's also a tax reason to do the opposite, since losses can offset gains. The pattern is a good example of how feelings about regret can lead us to a worse choice.

# The more you trade, the less you make
@src Barber & Odean, Journal of Finance, 2000

In 2000 Brad Barber and Terrance Odean used records from a large brokerage firm to study how often individual investors trade, and how they did. They looked at tens of thousands of households over six years, and divided them by how often they bought and sold.

The households that traded the most earned the lowest returns, after costs. The most active group underperformed the market by several percentage points a year, while those who traded the least did best. The reasons were the costs of trading, and a tendency to be overconfident. The investors who traded most believed that they knew more than the market. The title of the paper summed it up. Trading is hazardous to your wealth.
