# Spanish that lasts for decades
@series learn
@src Bahrick, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 1984

How much of what you learned in school do you still know? In 1984 Harry Bahrick tested 733 people who had studied Spanish in high school, from just after the course to 50 years later, and who had not used it much since. Some had taken one year of Spanish, and some had taken several. He tested them on vocabulary, grammar and reading.

Knowledge fell off quickly in the first three to six years, as forgetting curves predict. Then it leveled off, and stayed about the same for decades, up to about 35 years, before it declined again in old age. Bahrick called the stable part permastore. What was in it depended on how well the material had been learned, which was measured by the number of courses and by the grades. People who had learned it well, with practice spread out over time, kept the most.

# The peak of the memory of a life
@src Rubin, Rahhal & Poon, Memory and Cognition, 1998

Ask people in their sixties to recall events from their lives, and the memories do not come evenly from all periods. More come from the years between about ten and thirty than from before or after. This is called the reminiscence bump. In 1998 David Rubin, Tamara Rahhal and Leonard Poon reviewed the evidence, and tested other kinds of memory, for facts about the world.

They found that older people also remember best the books, films and music of that period, and name them as favorites. They did better on questions about the Academy Awards and the World Series from those years. Explanations include that it is a time of many firsts, and of the formation of identity. A later study suggested that memory is better in general in adolescence and early adulthood. The songs you loved at seventeen have a special hold on you.

# The baby who kicked a mobile
@src Rovee-Collier et al., 1980

Can a baby of three months remember anything? In a series of studies, Carolyn Rovee-Collier and her colleagues tied a ribbon from the ankle of an infant, lying in a crib, to a mobile hanging above. The baby soon learned that kicking made the mobile move, and kicked more. Later, they brought the baby back and tied the ribbon to a fixed bar, instead, and watched how much the baby kicked when it saw the mobile.

If the infant had learned the connection, it kicked more at the sight of the mobile, even a day or more later. Three-month-olds forgot after a while, but a brief reminder, in which the experimenter moved the mobile without the baby's help, brought the memory back. With a reminder, infants showed no forgetting weeks later. The experiments showed that very young babies learn and remember, and that forgetting is not always loss.

# The machine that lit up for blickets
@src Gopnik & Sobel, Child Development, 2000

How do young children decide what something is? We often assume that they go by looks. In 2000 Alison Gopnik and David Sobel tested this with a machine that lit up and played music when certain objects were put on it. They called the objects that set it off blickets. Children aged two, three and four saw that a particular object made the machine go. Then they were asked which other objects were blickets.

The children did not simply pick the objects that looked like the first one. They paid attention to what the objects did to the machine. An object that made it light up was a blicket, even if it looked quite different. Gopnik has argued that young children behave like scientists, working out how the world works from evidence. Not every researcher agrees that children work in the same way as scientists.

# When children learn that others can be wrong
@src Wellman, Cross & Watson, Child Development, 2001

In a classic test, a child sees a doll, Sally, put a marble in a basket and leave. Another doll, Anne, moves the marble to a box. The child is asked where Sally will look for her marble when she returns. To answer correctly, the child must realize that Sally has a false belief, which differs from the real state of things. Most three-year-olds say that she will look in the box. Older children say the basket.

In 2001 Henry Wellman, David Cross and Julanne Watson pooled the results of 178 studies of tasks like this. They found a steady change over the preschool years. Children moved from below chance to above chance, in many countries. A model with age and the features of the task explained more than half of the differences between studies. It is a milestone in understanding that other people have minds of their own.

# Easy, difficult and slow to warm up
@src Thomas & Chess, New York Longitudinal Study, from 1956

In 1956 the psychiatrists Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess began to follow more than a hundred children in New York from infancy. They interviewed their parents about how the babies behaved, such as how regular their sleep was, and how they reacted to new things. They found that babies differ from the first weeks, and that the differences could be grouped.

About 40 percent were easy babies, who were regular, cheerful and adapted quickly. About 10 percent were difficult, with irregular habits, strong reactions and slow adjustment. About 15 percent were slow to warm up, who withdrew from the new but then adapted. The rest did not fit one group. Thomas and Chess stressed goodness of fit. A temperament is not good or bad in itself, but depends on whether the parents and the child's surroundings suit it. Parents are not the only cause of how their children turn out.

# Twins raised in different homes
@conf mixed
@src Bouchard et al., Science, 1990

How much do genes and upbringing contribute to who we are? Identical twins who are separated at birth, and brought up in different families, offer a natural test. In the Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart, Thomas Bouchard and his colleagues brought more than a hundred sets of twins or triplets who had been raised apart to Minneapolis, for a week of tests of intelligence, personality, interests and health.

They reported that identical twins raised apart were about as alike in personality and interests as identical twins raised together. They estimated that about 70 percent of the variation in IQ was associated with genes. Critics argue that the twins were often in touch, that families with adopted children are similar, and that the figures depend on the population. A heritability figure describes a group at a certain time. It does not say how much of one person's traits is caused by genes.

# What happened to the children of the orphanages
@src Nelson, Zeanah, Fox et al., Science, 2007

After the fall of the dictator Nicolae Ceausescu, in 1989, Romania had many thousands of children living in large state institutions, with little attention, in cribs. In 2000 a team of American researchers, Charles Nelson, Charles Zeanah and Nathan Fox, began the Bucharest Early Intervention Project. They assessed 136 institutionalized children, with an average age of 22 months. By lottery, half were placed in foster families, which the project had created, and half stayed in care.

By the age of four and a half, the children who had stayed in the institutions scored far lower on tests of intelligence than children who had never been institutionalized, and lower than those placed in foster care. The earlier a child was moved, the better the outcome. Later follow-ups found benefits in mental health and attachment as well. The study is often cited as evidence of a sensitive period in early childhood, and of the importance of a caring family.

# The nested worlds of a child
@src Bronfenbrenner, The Ecology of Human Development, 1979

In 1979 the psychologist Urie Bronfenbrenner argued that a child cannot be understood apart from the places in which the child grows up. He pictured them as nested circles. The innermost is the microsystem, the settings where the child is directly, such as the home, the school and the playground. Around it is the mesosystem, the links between those settings, such as how the parents and teachers get along.

Next is the exosystem, which holds settings that the child is not in, but that affect the child, such as a parent's workplace. A change in a parent's hours can change a child's day. The outermost is the macrosystem, which is the culture, the laws, and the economy. His ecological model shifted attention from the child alone to the whole web around the child. It is a reason why a program for children often works best when it also reaches the family and the community.

# What you remember from a list
@src Glanzer & Cunitz, Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 1966

Read a list of words to people, and ask them to write down all that they remember, in any order. They do best on the words at the end of the list, which is called the recency effect, and well on the first few words, which is called the primacy effect. The words in the middle are the worst remembered. The pattern is called the serial position curve, and it has been found over and over.

In 1966 Murray Glanzer and Anita Cunitz did an experiment to see whether the two ends have the same cause. They had people count backward for thirty seconds before they recalled the list. The recency effect vanished, since the last words had faded from the short-term store. The primacy effect stayed, because the first words had been rehearsed enough to enter long-term memory. It was an early piece of evidence for two kinds of memory.
