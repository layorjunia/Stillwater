# What a long study found about a good life
@src Waldinger & Schulz, The Good Life, 2023

In 1938 researchers at Harvard began following 268 male undergraduates, and later added a group of 456 boys from poor neighborhoods in Boston. Over more than eighty years, they've tracked these men and later their wives and children, checking their health, work and relationships every few years. It's one of the longest studies of adult life ever done.

Robert Waldinger, who has directed the study, summarizes its biggest lesson in a sentence: good relationships keep us happier and healthier. The men who were most satisfied with their relationships at age 50 were the healthiest at 80, more than those with low cholesterol. Close ties seemed to protect both the body and the brain. What counted wasn't the number of friends or whether one was married, but the quality of the closeness. Relationships, it seems, are something worth working on, as with exercise.

# The recipe for friends
@src Hall, Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, 2018

How much time does it take to make a friend? In 2018 Jeffrey Hall at the University of Kansas surveyed adults who'd recently moved, and tracked how their relationships developed. He estimated that it takes about 50 hours of time together to go from acquaintance to casual friend, about 90 hours to become a friend, and more than 200 hours to become a close friend.

What counted wasn't just hours but the kind of time. Joking around, having meaningful conversations and spending time doing things together raised the closeness more than being in the same room with a task. The numbers are estimates from one study, and people vary. But the message is plain and a bit comforting. Friendship needs time, so it makes sense to keep seeing people, and not to conclude too soon that it isn't happening.

# The number of people you can keep up with
@src Dunbar, 1992; Zhou et al., 2005
@conf mixed

In 1992 the anthropologist Robin Dunbar noticed that, among primates, the size of a species' social group was related to the size of its neocortex. Extending the relationship to humans, he predicted that we can maintain stable relationships with about 150 people. The figure became known as Dunbar's number.

Studies of Christmas card lists, village sizes and phone contacts have found numbers in that range, along with a pattern of circles: about five very close people, 15 good friends, 50 friends and 150 acquaintances, each circle about three times the size of the one inside. It isn't a hard limit, though. Some researchers argue that the 150 figure is too uncertain to be treated as a rule, and that it varies between people. Even so, the idea captures something true, that we have only so much time and attention for relationships.

# The people you can call at three in the morning
@src Marsden, American Sociological Review, 1987; McPherson, Smith-Lovin & Brashears, 2006
@conf mixed

In the General Social Survey in 1985, Americans were asked a simple question: "Who do you discuss important matters with?" The most common answer was three people. In 2004 the researchers repeated the question, and the most common answer was none. The average number had fallen by about a third, and the proportion who named nobody had risen.

The result made headlines as evidence that Americans were becoming more isolated. It was criticized: some argued that the wording of the question in the 2004 survey made the results look different, and later surveys have found less change. But other research does show rising loneliness and fewer close ties, in many countries. It's a reason to take stock of who you'd turn to, and whom you can be that person for.

# Love has several parts
@src Sternberg, Psychological Review, 1986
@conf mixed

In 1986 Robert Sternberg proposed that love is made of three ingredients: intimacy, which is closeness and sharing; passion, which is physical attraction and excitement; and commitment, the decision to stay. Different combinations make different kinds of love. Intimacy and commitment without passion is companionate love, as in a long marriage. Passion alone is infatuation. All three together is what he called consummate love.

The model is easy to remember, and many people find it helpful for thinking about their relationships. As science, it's been less successful. The three parts can be measured, but studies don't always find them as separate as the theory says. And the theory describes what love is made of, but doesn't explain how it starts. It's best seen as a useful way of asking what's missing, and whether it can be built.

# What the brain does in love
@src Aron et al., Journal of Neurophysiology, 2005

In 2005 Arthur Aron, Helen Fisher and their colleagues scanned the brains of 17 people who were intensely in love, having recently fallen. While they looked at a photograph of their beloved, and compared with a photo of a friend, the scans showed activity in regions rich in dopamine, such as the ventral tegmental area, which is part of the brain's reward system.

It's the same system that is active with motivation and craving for things such as food or drugs. Fisher has argued that romantic love is a drive more than an emotion, as powerful as hunger. It's a small study, and brain scans can't tell us what love is. But it fits what people describe: an urge to be with the person, a focus on them, and a sense that nothing else matters, which mellows into something calmer over time.

# What people say they want
@src Eastwick & Finkel, JPSP, 2008

In 2008 Paul Eastwick and Eli Finkel asked people about their ideal partner, such as how important attractiveness and earning power were to them. Then they had the same people take part in speed dating events. The traits that men and women said mattered, for example that women valued earnings and men valued looks, didn't predict whom they were attracted to at the event.

Stated preferences, in other words, didn't predict real attraction when people met face to face. What does seem to predict it is the particular chemistry between two people, which can't be foreseen from a list. We're poor at saying what we want in a partner, and better at recognizing it when we meet it. If you've been turned down by a checklist, you may have missed good matches.

# The ratio that predicts
@src Gottman & Levenson, 1992
@conf mixed

John Gottman and Robert Levenson watched couples talk about a conflict, and counted their positive and negative moments, such as smiles, humor and affection against criticism and sarcasm. In 1992 they reported that couples who stayed together tended to have about five positive interactions for every negative one during conflict. Couples heading for divorce were much closer to one to one.

It's called the magic ratio, 5 to 1. It's a rule of thumb, based on observation of a small number of couples, and it's been criticized as too neat, since the effect of any single negative moment depends on the context. But it points to something that most people recognize. In a relationship, a small amount of criticism needs to be balanced by a lot of warmth, and the warmth has to be real.

# The fun you have together
@src Aron et al., JPSP, 2000

In 2000 Arthur Aron and his colleagues studied couples who'd been together for years and found that many of them felt that their marriage had gone flat. They tried an experiment. Some couples were asked to spend 90 minutes a week for ten weeks doing activities that were exciting, such as skiing, dancing or hiking. Other couples did something pleasant but ordinary, like going to dinner or the cinema, and others did nothing different.

The couples who did exciting things together reported greater marital satisfaction than the others. Aron's explanation, called self-expansion theory, is that people are attracted to relationships that help them grow and feel new. Doing novel things together can bring back that feeling. It suggests that to keep a relationship alive, shared newness may matter more than shared comfort.

# Why wait for the apology?
@src McCullough et al., JPSP, 1998

Forgiving sounds like a favor you do for another person, but psychologists treat it as something that also helps the one who forgives. In 1998 Michael McCullough and his colleagues defined forgiveness as a set of changes in motivation: less desire to take revenge, less avoidance, and more goodwill. It doesn't require forgetting or excusing what was done, or reconciling.

In studies, people who were able to forgive had lower blood pressure, less stress and better mental health than those who held grudges. In a training approach developed by Everett Worthington, people were taught to recall the hurt, to empathize with the person who caused it, and to make a deliberate decision. Forgiveness may be easier when the other person apologizes, but it doesn't depend on that.

# Raising children with warmth and rules
@src Baumrind, Child Development, 1966

In 1966 Diana Baumrind described three styles of parenting, based on her observations of families in California. Authoritarian parents set strict rules and expect obedience, with little warmth. Permissive parents are loving but set few limits. Authoritative parents set clear rules and explain them, and are also responsive and warm. Later researchers added a fourth, neglectful style.

Children raised in authoritative homes tended to do best, with higher confidence and better school results and fewer problems. The pattern has been found in many studies, though it varies across cultures, and parenting style is only one influence among many, including the child's temperament. The core idea is a useful one, though: children need both warmth and structure, and they do best when they have both.

# Does spanking work?
@src Gershoff & Grogan-Kaylor, Journal of Family Psychology, 2016

Many parents were raised with spanking, and many believe that it works. In 2016 Elizabeth Gershoff and Andrew Grogan-Kaylor analyzed 75 studies, covering more than 160,000 children, and found that spanking was associated with a greater likelihood of a number of poor outcomes, such as aggression, antisocial behavior and mental health problems. It wasn't linked with any better compliance in the long run.

Most of these studies can't prove cause and effect, because parents can't be assigned to spank at random. And aggressive children might provoke more spanking. But the pattern holds up when researchers control for the child's earlier behavior, and it's stronger the more often it is done. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises against it. Evidence supports other methods, such as clear rules, praise for good behavior, calm timeouts and consistency.

# The truth about birth order
@src Damian & Roberts, Journal of Research in Personality, 2015

Many people believe that firstborns are responsible, middle children are peacemakers and youngest children are rebels. In 2015 Rodica Damian and Brent Roberts analyzed data from more than 370,000 students in the US, and found that birth order had no meaningful effect on personality traits such as extroversion, agreeableness or conscientiousness. There was a very small effect on measured intelligence, in favor of the firstborn.

The researchers tested the same families to see if the effects were due to differences between families, and used comparisons between siblings in the same family. The result was the same. The belief persists because we notice cases that fit the stereotype, and because we know our siblings so well. Family dynamics matter, but where you came in the lineup isn't a dependable predictor.

# Do we change as we grow older?
@src Roberts, Walton & Viechtbauer, Psychological Bulletin, 2006

Is personality fixed, or does it keep developing? In 2006 Brent Roberts and his colleagues combined 92 long-term studies and found that personality does change across the life span, mostly in a good direction. People become more conscientious, more agreeable and emotionally steadier as they age, especially from their twenties to their fifties. They call it the maturity principle.

Personality is also stable in an important sense. People tend to keep their relative position, so that someone who is more anxious than their peers at 20 is likely to be so at 60. But the whole group shifts toward being calmer and more responsible. Changes can also be deliberate. People who work on a trait, such as being more organized, often see a change over a period of months.

# The risk of being watched by friends
@src Gardner & Steinberg, Developmental Psychology, 2005

In 2005 Margo Gardner and Laurence Steinberg had teenagers, young adults and adults play a computer driving game in which they could take risks, such as running yellow lights, and risk a crash. Some played alone and some with two friends of the same age watching. For adults, having friends watching made no difference. For teenagers, it made a big difference: they took significantly more risks.

The researchers think that in adolescence, the brain's reward system is especially sensitive to what peers think, and the systems of self-control are still developing. It helps to explain why many accidents involving teenagers happen when they're with friends. It's not that teens are unable to think about risk. It's that the presence of peers raises the reward. Parents and teachers can use this by reducing opportunities for risky situations.

# The happiness curve
@src Blanchflower & Oswald, 2008
@conf mixed

In 2008 the economists David Blanchflower and Andrew Oswald analyzed surveys of over a million people in 72 countries, and found a surprising pattern. Life satisfaction tends to dip in middle age, with the lowest point in the late forties, and then rise again into old age. It forms a U-shape. The pattern appeared even after controlling for income, marital status and other things.

The finding has been debated. Some studies find the same curve, and some find a flatter line, and the dip is small compared with differences between individuals. Researchers suggest possible explanations, such as the stress of middle years, or an increased ability to accept what we can't change as we get older. If you're in your forties and feeling low, it's worth knowing that on average, things tend to get better.

# Old age and the glass half full
@src Carstensen, Science, 2006; Mather & Carstensen, 2005

In the 1990s Laura Carstensen noticed that older people don't seem to be as unhappy as expected. In experiments, she and her colleagues found that older adults pay more attention to positive information than young people do. When shown pictures of faces and asked about them later, they remembered the happy ones better than the angry ones, while the young remembered both equally.

She explained it with socioemotional selectivity theory. When we see our time as limited, we focus on things that are emotionally meaningful, and spend time with those we love, and pay less heed to worries and conflicts. The effect isn't that older people are naive. It's a shift in priorities. People of any age who are asked to imagine that they've only a short time left show the same shift.

# Regrets, short and long
@src Gilovich & Medvec, Psychological Review, 1995

What do people regret most? In 1995 Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec reviewed studies in which people were asked about their regrets. In the short run, people regret what they did: the action that went wrong. But when looking back over a lifetime, they tend to regret the things that they didn't do, such as the course not taken, the person not asked out or the chance that they let go.

One explanation is that we can do something about actions that went wrong. We rationalize, learn from them or apologize, but the missed opportunities leave us wondering what might have been. It's a reason to take chances when the downside is limited. A good question to ask when you're hesitating is whether, in ten years, you'll regret not having tried.

# Does social media make us unhappy?
@src Orben & Przybylski, 2019
@conf mixed

In 2019 Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski analyzed three large datasets, covering more than 350,000 adolescents in the UK and US, to test whether the amount of time spent with digital technology was linked to well-being. They found a very small negative association. It explained about 0.4 percent of the variation in teenagers' well-being, which was comparable to the effect of regularly eating potatoes, or of wearing glasses.

The study was influential, and also criticized. Critics say that averaging across all uses hides important differences, and that some groups, such as young girls, may be more affected. Other studies find stronger links with specific habits, such as heavy use at night or passive scrolling. So the question isn't settled. The evidence suggests that it's the quality of use, and who is using it, that matter more than the hours.

# Sensitive people
@src Aron & Aron, JPSP, 1997
@conf mixed

In 1997 the psychologists Elaine Aron and Arthur Aron proposed that some people have a trait that they called sensory processing sensitivity: they notice subtle details, are more affected by noise and crowds, think deeply, and feel emotions strongly. Elaine Aron called them highly sensitive people, and estimated that about 15 to 20 percent of people are like this.

Researchers have found that people who score high on the scale show more activity in brain areas for attention and empathy when looking at emotional pictures. The trait overlaps with others, such as introversion and neuroticism, and it isn't an official diagnosis. Some critics say that the evidence is limited. But the concept is helpful for many people, giving a name to a way of being that isn't a flaw. It suggests designing your day with enough quiet and rest.

# Introverts need not be fixed
@src Eysenck, 1967; Geen, 1984; Cain, Quiet, 2012
@conf mixed

In the 1960s the psychologist Hans Eysenck proposed a theory of introversion and extroversion based on arousal. He thought that introverts have brains that are more easily stimulated, so they prefer a quieter setting, while extroverts need more stimulation to feel their best. The evidence for the details is mixed, but the general idea fits many people's experience.

In a classic experiment, introverts and extroverts did tasks in noisy or quiet rooms, and the introverts did better in the quiet and worse in the noise, in line with the theory. Susan Cain's 2012 best seller Quiet argued that society undervalues introverts' strengths, such as focus and listening. Being introverted isn't the same as being shy. It's about where you recover your energy, and it doesn't need to be fixed.

# Is the divorce rate really half?
@src Kennedy & Ruggles, Demography, 2014

You may have heard that half of all marriages end in divorce. The statistic, which was widely quoted in the 1980s, was based on projections from a peak in the rate of divorce at that time. In reality, the divorce rate in the United States has been declining since then, and for couples who married after the 1980s the share who divorce is lower than half.

Sheela Kennedy and Steven Ruggles showed in 2014 that the picture is more complicated. Divorce has been rising for people over 50, but falling for younger people, who marry later and are more educated. The risk is also strongly affected by age at marriage, education and income. The "half" figure is a myth, or at least out of date, and the odds aren't the same for everyone.

# What helps teenagers
@src Resnick et al., JAMA, 1997

In the 1990s the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent Health, known as Add Health, surveyed more than 12,000 American teenagers about their lives, and tracked them as they grew up. In 1997 Michael Resnick and his colleagues reported on what protected them from risks such as drug use, violence, early sex and suicide attempts.

The strongest protector was a feeling of connection, especially with parents and with school. Teens who felt that their parents cared about them, and that their teachers treated them fairly, were less likely to engage in risky behavior, even when the risks in their lives were high. Rules and monitoring helped too, but the warmth of the relationship came first. It's one of the most replicated findings in adolescent research.

# Why we change our minds about what we want
@src Gilbert & Ebert, JPSP, 2002

In 2002 Daniel Gilbert and Jane Ebert ran an experiment with photography students who were allowed to develop two prints, and then choose one to keep. Some of the students were told that they could change their minds in the next few days, and others that the decision was final. Those with the final decision liked the photograph that they'd chosen better a few days later than those who could change their minds.

When a choice can't be reversed, the mind works to make it feel good. When it can be reversed, the mind keeps looking, and the satisfaction is lower. The students didn't know it: most thought that they'd prefer the option to change their mind. It suggests that the freedom to take things back can make us less happy with what we have, and that a firm commitment can be a gift to ourselves.
