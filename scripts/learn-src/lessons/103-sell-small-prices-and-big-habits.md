# Pennies a day
@conf mixed
@src Gourville, Journal of Consumer Research, 1998

A charity asks for a donation of 365 dollars a year. Another asks for one dollar a day. The amount is the same, but they do not feel the same. In 1998 the marketing researcher John Gourville tested this in a series of experiments that he called the pennies-a-day strategy. He changed the way that a cost was presented, from one large payment into a series of small ongoing ones, and he asked people to evaluate the request.

The reframing made people more willing to say yes. His explanation was that the framing changes what we compare the price with. A daily cost reminds us of other small daily costs, such as a cup of coffee, and a large cost reminds us of other large ones, such as a vacation. A small unit is easier to accept. It is a trick that can be used for good, as when it helps people save, and in a bad way, when it makes a big commitment look trivial.

# Tightwads and spendthrifts
@src Rick, Cryder & Loewenstein, Journal of Consumer Research, 2008

Some people hate to part with money, even when they can afford to and would like to. Others spend freely, even when they should not. In 2008 Scott Rick, Cynthia Cryder and George Loewenstein built a short questionnaire to measure the difference, which they called the spendthrift-tightwad scale. It asks, for example, how much people suffer when they spend. They tried it on thousands of consumers, over 18,000 in all.

They found that tightwads were more common than spendthrifts, by about three to two, and that most people fell between. The tightwads had far less debt and more in savings. They also spent less than they said they would like to, because of what the authors called the pain of paying, an anticipated sting at the time of purchase. Cues that dull the pain, such as paying by card, can make a tightwad spend like a spendthrift. The scale shows that people vary a lot in how they feel about spending.

# The credit card premium
@src Prelec & Simester, Marketing Letters, 2001

Does it matter how you pay? In 2001 Drazen Prelec and Duncan Simester held an auction among business school students at MIT for a pair of tickets to a Boston Celtics game that had been sold out. Half of the bidders were told that they would pay in cash, and the others that they would pay by credit card. Each submitted a sealed bid, and the highest bidder won.

People who expected to pay by card bid far more. The average bid was about twice as high as for those who would pay in cash, and the highest bids from card users were more than twice as high. The authors did not settle why, but one explanation is that paying by card makes the cost feel less real. The effect is not found every time. But it is a reason to treat a card as a tool that changes how much we are willing to pay.

# Losses disguised as wins
@src Dixon, Harrigan, Sandhu, Collins & Fugelsang, Addiction, 2010

A modern slot machine lets a player bet on many lines at once. Suppose that you bet on twenty lines, at a cost of twenty cents, and win back ten cents. You have lost ten cents, but the machine flashes lights and plays a jingle, as if you had won. Researchers call this a loss disguised as a win. In 2010 Mike Dixon and his colleagues tested how the body reacts to it.

They measured the skin conductance, a sign of arousal, of 40 people with little experience of gambling, as they played a multi-line machine. The response after a loss disguised as a win was as strong as after a real win, and both were stronger than after a plain loss, when the machine stayed quiet. The authors suggested that players may count such outcomes as wins. It helps to explain why people can think they are doing better than they are, and it is a feature that the anthropologist Natasha Dow Schüll criticized in her study of machine design.

# The hot dog that could not rise
@src Costco, 1985 to today

In the mid-1980s the warehouse chain Costco began to sell a quarter-pound hot dog and a soda for a dollar fifty. Four decades later the price is still a dollar fifty, though the cost of beef, bread and everything else has gone up. In 2013 the chief executive, Craig Jelinek, told the co-founder Jim Sinegal that the company was losing money on it, and suggested a small rise. Sinegal answered, in blunt terms, that he must not touch it.

Jelinek kept the price. He even switched the soda from Coca-Cola to Pepsi products, which cost less, in order to hold the price. The hot dog is a loss leader, which draws members, who pay a yearly fee, and builds trust. Much of the chain's profit comes from the membership fees, and not from marking up goods. It is a case of a price that has become a promise, which the company keeps because the promise is worth more than the loss.

# Selling in a living room
@src Brownie Wise and Tupperware, 1951 to 1958

In the late 1940s Earl Tupper made a line of airtight plastic food containers, which he sold in stores, where they did not do well. Customers did not understand how the lids worked. A saleswoman in Florida, Brownie Wise, had been selling the containers at parties in people's homes, where she could show how to seal them. Sales were remarkable. In 1951 Tupper hired her to run Tupperware Home Parties, and took the containers out of the stores.

A hostess invited her friends, and a demonstrator showed the product, gave out small gifts, and took orders. Wise became well known, and by the mid-1950s she was on the cover of Business Week. In 1958 Tupper dismissed her. The home party is a clear example of how sales can use friendship. People bought from someone whom they knew, and from whom they had accepted hospitality and gifts, in front of friends who were also buying.

# Dark patterns at scale
@src Mathur et al., Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, 2019

A dark pattern is a feature of a website that is designed to steer people into doing something that they may not want, such as a countdown timer that is not real, or a claim that stock is low when it is not. In 2019 a group of researchers at Princeton and the University of Chicago set out to measure how common they are. They wrote a program to visit about 11,000 shopping websites, and collected about 53,000 product pages.

They found 1,818 instances of dark patterns, of 15 types, on 1,254 sites, which is about 11 percent of the sites. Some of them were plainly deceptive, with 234 instances on 183 sites. They also found that 22 outside companies supplied the tools for dark patterns, which they sold to shops as plug-ins. The study gave regulators and consumers a count, and not just examples. It suggests that such pressure tactics are not the work of a few shops.

# Only one room left
@src Competition and Markets Authority, 2019

When you look for a hotel on a booking website, you may see a message that only one room is left, or that several people are looking at the same room right now. In 2017 the British regulator, the Competition and Markets Authority, began to investigate whether such claims, and the discount claims on the sites, were misleading. It looked at six sites, Expedia, Booking.com, Agoda, Hotels.com, ebookers and trivago.

The concern was that claims about how many rooms were left, and how many people were viewing, could give a false impression of how popular a room was, and rush people into a decision. In February 2019 the sites agreed to change their practices, including how they present discounts, and the changes were due by September of that year. The regulator also published principles for the whole industry, and 25 other sites signed up. The case shows that a message created to make us hurry can be tested against the facts.
