# A language you can whistle
@src Carreiras, Lopez, Rivero & Corina, Nature, 2005

On La Gomera, one of the Canary Islands, shepherds have long talked across deep valleys by whistling. The whistled speech is called Silbo Gomero, and it is a version of Spanish. The whistler turns the sounds of the words into pitch and rhythm, and a skilled listener can understand a message from far across a ravine. In 2005 Manuel Carreiras and his colleagues put five whistlers and five Spanish speakers who did not know Silbo into a brain scanner.

Both groups showed similar activity when they heard ordinary Spanish. When they heard whistled sentences, only the whistlers used the regions of the left temporal lobe that handle speech. The others used areas that are not tied to language. The brain treated the whistles as words, when the person knew how. One of the researchers pointed out that Silbo is not a full language of its own, since children are not born whistling it. It is a way of carrying Spanish on a different sound.

# Jocks and burnouts
@src Eckert, Jocks and Burnouts, 1989; Linguistic Variation as Social Practice, 2000

Do people talk like their class, or like their friends? In the early 1980s the linguist Penelope Eckert spent two years at a high school in the suburbs of Detroit, which she called Belten High, and shorter periods at four other schools. She found that the students were divided into two main groups. The jocks were centered on the school and its activities. The burnouts were centered on the local area and the city.

She measured the vowels that the students used, which in the Detroit area were changing in a pattern known as the Northern Cities Shift. The pronunciations tended to follow the social group, and not only the parents' income. The burnouts used the newest forms more than the jocks did. A way of speaking can be a badge, which shows who you spend time with and what you stand for. Language change often moves through groups like these, and not through whole towns at once.

# Do sounds carry meaning?
@src Blasi et al., PNAS, 2016
@conf mixed

Most linguists have long held that the sound of a word has nothing to do with its meaning. The word dog does not sound like a dog, and other languages use other sounds. In 2016 Damián Blasi and his colleagues checked this with word lists that covered nearly two thirds of the world's languages. They looked at a hundred basic words, such as nose, tongue, small and full, and asked whether certain sounds were used for them more often than chance would predict.

Some were. Words for nose tend to have an n sound, words for tongue an l, words for small an i, and words for full a p or b. The patterns recurred in different continents and language families, and they seemed to have arisen independently. Some researchers point out that earlier work had also found links between sound and meaning. It does not mean that the sounds are fixed, only that the link between sound and meaning is not always arbitrary.

# Subject, object, verb
@src Dryer, World Atlas of Language Structures, chapter 81, 2013

In the sentence Clover ate a mouse, the subject comes first, then the verb, then the object. English does it that way, and so do Spanish, Russian and Mandarin. But in the World Atlas of Language Structures, which surveys 1,377 languages, the most common order is a different one. The subject comes first, then the object, and the verb comes last. About 565 languages, or 41 percent, do it that way, including Japanese, Korean, Turkish and Hindi.

The order that English uses comes second, with 488 languages, or 35 percent. Languages in which the verb comes first, such as Welsh, make up about 7 percent, and the other orders are rare. A few languages put the object first, and the invented language Klingon was designed to put the object before the verb and the subject last. The speakers of each language find their own order natural, but it is only one of the ways in which the same idea can be built.

# A judge in Calcutta notices a pattern
@src Jones, The Third Anniversary Discourse, 1786

In 1786 William Jones, a British judge who worked in Calcutta and who had learned Sanskrit, gave a talk to the Asiatic Society that he had founded. He said that Sanskrit, however old it was, had a structure that was wonderful, and that it was more perfect than Greek and richer than Latin. Then he made a larger point. It had a stronger likeness to both of them, in the roots of the verbs and in the forms of the grammar, than could have been produced by accident.

He suggested that the three had sprung from a common source which perhaps no longer existed. He also thought that Gothic, Celtic and Persian might belong to the same family. It was not the first time the resemblance had been noticed, but his clear statement is often seen as the start of the study of Indo-European languages. From that idea came the search for the reconstructed ancestor, which is now called Proto-Indo-European, and for the people who spoke it.

# Where did Indo-European begin?
@conf contested
@src Bouckaert et al., Science, 2012

The Indo-European languages, from English and Hindi to Russian and Persian, share an ancestor, but where and when it was spoken is disputed. One view puts it on the steppe north of the Black Sea, about 6,000 years ago. Another puts it in Anatolia, in what is now Turkey, with farmers who spread out about 8,000 years ago. In 2012 Remco Bouckaert and his colleagues took basic vocabulary from 103 ancient and modern languages and used statistical methods developed to trace the spread of viruses.

They found strong support for Anatolia, with a date between about 7,800 and 9,800 years ago. Critics were not convinced. They questioned the statistical methods, and pointed to words for wheeled vehicles, which seem to have been shared at a later time and which fit the steppe story. Later studies of ancient DNA have mostly favored the steppe story, though the question is still discussed. It is an example of a question where different kinds of evidence can pull in different directions.

# How many languages are left?
@src Ethnologue, 2024

Ethnologue, a catalog of the world's languages, counts about 7,160 that are in use today. The number changes from year to year, because it is hard to decide where one language ends and a dialect begins. They are not spread evenly. Papua New Guinea alone has more than eight hundred, and many of them are spoken by only a few thousand people.

About 40 percent of all languages are classed as endangered, which is more than three thousand of them, and many have fewer than a thousand speakers left. A language is usually lost not in one stroke, but when children stop learning it, because the parents think that another language will give them better chances. When the last fluent speaker dies, the stories, names and ways of describing the world that were carried in the language go with it. Some communities are working to revive theirs, with classes and recordings.

# Time that runs from east to west
@src Boroditsky & Gaby, Psychological Science, 2010

In English, we speak of the future as ahead, and we lay out time from left to right, as on a calendar. In Kuuk Thaayorre, a language spoken in the community of Pormpuraaw in northern Australia, there are few words for left and right. People use compass directions for everything, even small distances, and they greet each other by asking where they are going, in terms such as north-northeast.

In 2010 Lera Boroditsky and Alice Gaby gave sets of picture cards to fourteen people from Pormpuraaw and fourteen students at Stanford. Each set showed a progression, such as a person growing older, and was shuffled. The participants were asked to lay out the cards in the order of time. The students put them from left to right. The Kuuk Thaayorre speakers put them from east to west, no matter which way they were facing. Their language, it seems, taught them to keep track of direction at all times.

# The vowels of baby talk
@series learn
@conf mixed
@src Kuhl et al., Science, 1997

When adults speak to babies they change their voices. The pitch rises, the pace slows and the vowels are drawn out. It is often called baby talk, or parentese. In 1997 Patricia Kuhl and her colleagues recorded thirty mothers who spoke English, Russian and Swedish, first to another adult and then to their baby. They analyzed more than two thousand words.

In all three languages, the mothers stretched their vowels more when they spoke to the baby. The vowels were pushed farther apart from one another, so that they were easier to tell apart. Kuhl suggested that it helps infants to learn the sounds of their language. Some later work found that the vowels in baby talk overlap more than this suggests, and the benefit is debated. But it is a striking fact that parents on different continents do the same thing, without being taught.

# Metaphors we live by
@src Lakoff & Johnson, Metaphors We Live By, 1980
@conf mixed

We say that someone attacked a weak point in an argument, that a claim was defended, that a position was indefensible, and that a debate was won or lost. In 1980 George Lakoff and Mark Johnson pointed out how much of everyday talk about arguments uses the language of war. They said that this was not decoration. It showed that we think about arguments as a kind of fight, and so we act in that way.

They gave other examples. We spend, waste and save time, as if it were money. We speak of ideas as being on a path, and of life as a journey. Their book argued that abstract thought is built on metaphors drawn from bodily and physical experience. The evidence in it was mostly from the patterns of language, and not from experiments, and some linguists disagree. Later experiments have shown that a metaphor can change how people reason.

# From pidgin to a language
@conf mixed
@src Bickerton, Roots of Language, 1981

When people who have no common language have to work together, they invent a simple way to talk, which is called a pidgin. A pidgin has a small vocabulary and little grammar. It happened on sugar plantations in Hawaii, where workers came from China, Japan, Portugal and the Philippines, and their children grew up hearing it. The linguist Derek Bickerton studied what happened next.

The children did not just copy the pidgin. They made a fuller language of it, with a consistent grammar, which is called a creole. Bickerton argued that children are born with a built-in ability to make grammar, and that this explains how creoles arise so quickly. Other linguists think that the change was more gradual, and that other languages had an influence. Tok Pisin, which began as a pidgin and is now an official language of Papua New Guinea, shows how a language like that can grow.
