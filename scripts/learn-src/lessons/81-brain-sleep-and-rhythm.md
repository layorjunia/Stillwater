# Two sleeps a night
@src Ekirch, American Historical Review, 2001; Wehr, J. Sleep Research, 1992

The idea that a person should sleep for eight hours in a row may be recent. In 2001 the historian Roger Ekirch published a study of hundreds of references, in diaries, court records and literature, from the Middle Ages up to the early modern period. They mention first sleep and second sleep. People went to bed soon after dusk, slept for a few hours, woke around midnight, and stayed awake for an hour or two, before sleeping again until dawn.

In the interval, they might pray, talk, make love, or visit neighbors. In 1992 the psychiatrist Thomas Wehr tested whether this is natural. He kept volunteers in darkness for fourteen hours a night, for a month. After a few weeks, their sleep settled into two parts, with a quiet, wakeful period in between. It suggests that waking in the night may not always be a disorder. Many people worry about it more than is needed.

# Why teenagers can't sleep early
@src Carskadon, 2011; Crowley et al., Journal of Adolescence, 2018

Parents of teenagers often complain that they won't go to bed, and then can't get up. It's not just laziness. In the 1990s Mary Carskadon at Brown University found that the body clocks of adolescents shift later at puberty. Their brains release melatonin, the hormone that signals night, later than those of younger children. They feel sleepy later, and wake later.

At the same time, they still need about eight to ten hours of sleep. Early school start times, such as seven thirty, clash with the biology. A teenager who wakes at six may be, in biological terms, in the middle of the night. Studies of schools that moved the start time later found that students slept more, were less depressed, and had fewer car accidents. The pattern is found in many mammals. It reverses in the early twenties.

# Owls and larks
@src Roenneberg et al., Sleep Medicine Reviews, 2007; Horne & Östberg, 1976

Some people are at their best in the morning, and others in the evening. In the 1970s the psychologists Jim Horne and Olov Östberg created a questionnaire to measure it. The tendency is called chronotype. People are sometimes called larks, if they like the morning, and owls, if they like the night. In 2007 Till Roenneberg and his colleagues collected data on the sleep habits of more than fifty thousand people in Europe.

They found that chronotypes are spread across a continuous range, not in two groups. Most people are somewhere in the middle. Genes explain a part of the difference, and so does age. It also varies with light exposure. Those who live in cities, with artificial light, tend to be later. A mismatch between a person's body clock and the working day is called social jet lag, and it's associated with poorer health. Forcing an owl to act like a lark is difficult.

# What a short sleeper's gene does
@src He et al., Science, 2009; Shi et al., Neuron, 2019

A very few people get along with only four to six hours of sleep, without feeling tired, and without any apparent harm. They are called natural short sleepers. In 2009 Ying-Hui Fu and Louis Ptáček, at the University of California, San Francisco, studied a family in which a mother and daughter slept about six hours a night, much less than the usual eight. They found a mutation in a gene called DEC2.

When the researchers put the mutation into mice, the mice also slept less. In 2019 the same team found a mutation in another gene, ADRB1, in another family of short sleepers. People with such mutations are rare. It is not possible to train yourself to be a short sleeper. Most people who say they can manage on five hours are probably running a sleep debt, and don't notice the effects.

# The sleepless fly
@src Shaw, Cirelli, Greenspan & Tononi, Science, 2000

Do all animals sleep? In 2000 Paul Shaw and his colleagues showed that fruit flies do. They watched flies in tubes, and found that they had long periods of rest each night, in which they were less responsive to stimuli, and had a typical posture. If the flies were prevented from resting, by shaking the tubes, they rested more afterward, as if they had a sleep debt.

The flies were also affected by caffeine, which kept them awake, and by sleeping pills. The discovery meant that the genes that control sleep could be studied in an animal that is easy to breed and manipulate. Many of the genes that control sleep in flies, such as the clock gene period, also play a role in humans. Sleep is found in animals from jellyfish to humans, which suggests an ancient and essential function.

# Dolphins that sleep with half a brain
@src Mukhametov, Journal of Comparative Physiology, 1984; Rattenborg et al., Nature, 1999

A dolphin must come to the surface to breathe, and so it can't fall into a deep sleep, as a human can. In the 1970s Lev Mukhametov, a Soviet scientist, recorded the brain waves of bottlenose dolphins. He found that they sleep with one half of the brain at a time. While one hemisphere shows the slow waves of sleep, the other is awake, and the dolphin swims slowly, with one eye open.

This is called unihemispheric sleep. After a while, the two halves switch. Some birds do it too. In 1999 Niels Rattenborg and his colleagues showed that ducks at the edge of a group keep the eye that faces outward open, and the hemisphere of the brain opposite is awake, keeping watch for predators. It's a way of staying alert while resting. Humans have a mild form. In a new place, the left half of the brain sleeps more lightly on the first night.

# The first-night effect
@src Tamaki et al., Current Biology, 2016

Many people sleep badly on the first night in a hotel or a new bed. In 2016 Masako Tamaki and her colleagues at Brown University tested why. They monitored the brains of volunteers who spent two nights in the sleep laboratory. On the first night, there was a difference between the two halves of the brain. The left half had more activity in a network of regions, when the deep sleep was at its deepest.

The left hemisphere seemed to stay on watch, and was more responsive to sounds. On the second night, the difference had gone. In another experiment, odd sounds were played to one ear. They woke people more easily on the first night, when played to the right ear, which sends signals to the left half of the brain. It seems that the brain is on guard in a strange place. A familiar pillow, or a second night, helps.

# The brain's cleaning shift
@src Xie et al., Science, 2013

In 2013 Maiken Nedergaard and her colleagues at the University of Rochester published a study that made headlines. They injected a dye into the brains of mice, and watched how it spread. When the mice were awake, the dye moved slowly. When they were asleep, or anesthetized, the dye flowed through the brain much faster. The spaces between the brain cells expanded by about sixty percent during sleep.

The flow allows cerebrospinal fluid to wash through the brain and clear away waste, including a protein called beta-amyloid, which builds up in Alzheimer's disease. Nedergaard called it the glymphatic system, because it depends on glial cells. The idea suggests a reason why we need sleep, and why poor sleep may be linked to dementia. It's still being tested, and some scientists have questioned how strong the effect is in humans.

# Napping and the afternoon dip
@src Mednick, Nakayama & Stickgold, Nature Neuroscience, 2003; Brooks & Lack, Sleep, 2006

Many people feel sleepy in the early afternoon, even if they have had a good night's sleep and a light lunch. It's partly a dip in the body clock, which has a small trough in alertness at about two or three in the afternoon, as well as a major one in the middle of the night. It occurs even in people who don't eat lunch. In many cultures, a nap in the afternoon is common, such as the Spanish siesta.

In 2003 Sara Mednick and her colleagues at Harvard tested naps. People learned a visual task in the morning, and then were tested in the afternoon and evening. Those who took a nap of an hour or so, which contained both slow-wave and dream sleep, improved a lot, while those who stayed awake got worse. Short naps of about ten to twenty minutes can boost alertness without grogginess. Long naps can leave you groggy.

# The cost of lost sleep for the heart
@src Cappuccio et al., European Heart Journal, 2011; Jike et al., 2018

Sleeping too little is linked to many health problems. In 2011 Francesco Cappuccio and his colleagues combined the results of fifteen studies of sleep and cardiovascular disease, covering more than four hundred thousand people. They found that those who slept less than about six hours a night had a higher risk of heart disease and stroke than those who slept seven or eight.

Those who slept for a very long time, more than nine hours, had a higher risk as well, which may be because illness itself makes people sleep longer. The studies were observational. It's hard to be sure that sleep loss is the cause, and other factors, such as stress and depression, could play a part. But experiments have also shown that cutting sleep raises blood pressure, and increases inflammation. A natural experiment suggests an effect. In the spring, when the clocks go forward, there is a rise in heart attacks.

# Why we yawn
@src Gallup & Gallup, Evolutionary Psychology, 2007; Provine, 2005
@conf mixed

Yawning is common to almost all vertebrates, and we begin to do it in the womb. Yet no one is sure of its function. The old idea was that it takes in more oxygen, and removes carbon dioxide. But in a famous experiment, Robert Provine found that breathing air that was rich in oxygen, or in carbon dioxide, did not change how much people yawned.

Another idea was proposed by Andrew Gallup and Gordon Gallup in 2007. It's that yawning cools the brain. In their studies, people yawned less when they held a cold pack to the forehead, and more in warm conditions. A yawn draws in cool air, and increases blood flow to the head. The idea is plausible, and it's supported by some animal studies, though others have not repeated the result. It could explain why we yawn when tired, since the brain is warmer. The question is not yet settled.
