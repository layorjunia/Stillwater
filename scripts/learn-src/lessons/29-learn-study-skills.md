# What students do that doesn't work
@src Dunlosky et al., Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2013

In 2013 John Dunlosky and his colleagues reviewed the research on ten popular study techniques and rated how useful each one is. Several of the habits that students rely on most came out near the bottom. Highlighting and underlining, rereading, summarizing, and drawing mental pictures of a text were all rated as having low or only modest usefulness for learning.

None of these is harmful, and some can help in particular settings. The problem is that they feel productive. A page marked in yellow looks like work. A chapter read twice feels familiar. But familiarity is not the same as being able to recall something next week. The review's message was not to stop studying, but to swap in methods that make your memory work harder.

# The two methods that rated highest
@src Dunlosky et al., Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2013

Of the ten techniques in the Dunlosky review, only two earned the top rating of high utility. One was practice testing, which means trying to recall what you've learned without looking, using flashcards, practice questions or a blank page. The other was distributed practice, which means spreading your study over several sessions instead of putting it all into one.

Both methods work across a wide range of ages, subjects and kinds of material. They also share a feature. They feel harder than the alternatives, and they often make you feel less sure of yourself in the moment. That feeling is the sign that they are working. If studying feels smooth, it's worth asking whether you're really learning or just recognizing what you've seen before.

# Rereading feels like learning
@src Callender & McDaniel, Contemporary Educational Psychology, 2009

You read a chapter, you read it again, and the second time it all seems clear. That clarity is the problem. In 2009 Aimee Callender and Mark McDaniel tested whether rereading a textbook passage helps students learn it. Across several experiments with real textbook material, reading the passage a second time did not reliably improve later test performance over reading it once.

Rereading works on your sense of fluency. The words go by easily, because you've just seen them, and you take the ease as a sign that you know the material. But recognizing a sentence as familiar is a far lower bar than producing the idea on your own. Closing the book and trying to say what you remember is less pleasant, and it teaches you much more.

# Retrieval beats concept mapping
@src Karpicke & Blunt, Science, 2011

In 2011 Jeffrey Karpicke and Janell Blunt compared several ways of studying a science text. Students could read it once, read it several times, make a concept map while looking at the text, or read it and then write down everything they could remember. A week later the students who had practiced retrieving the text did best, including on questions that asked them to draw inferences.

Concept mapping is a well respected technique, and it asks you to think hard about how ideas connect. Even so, it did not beat the plain act of recalling. The students themselves guessed that repeated reading would work best, which fits a pattern in this research. We tend to prefer the study methods that feel good and to undervalue the ones that actually build memory.

# Restudying wins today and loses next week
@src Roediger & Karpicke, Psychological Science, 2006

In 2006 Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students read short prose passages and then either read them again or take a recall test on them. When the final test came five minutes later, the students who had reread did better. When the final test came two days or a week later, the students who had been tested did better.

This crossover explains why cramming feels so effective. If your goal is to do well in the next few minutes, rereading is a fine tool. If your goal is to remember something next month, it fades quickly, while retrieval practice slows the forgetting. The method you choose should match the time you need the knowledge to last. For most things we care about, it is much longer than a day.

# Dropping a card too soon
@src Karpicke & Roediger, Science, 2008
@conf mixed

Imagine you are studying vocabulary with flashcards. When you get a card right, you set it aside to save time. In 2008 Jeffrey Karpicke and Henry Roediger tested this habit with students learning Swahili and English word pairs. In one condition, words were dropped from testing after being recalled once. In another, they stayed in, and students kept trying to recall them.

Students who kept being tested remembered about twice as many words a week later. Extra study time, with the words dropped from testing, did little. A later study argued that part of this result came from the way the practice was spaced, and that the picture is more complicated. Still, the common advice holds up. One correct answer doesn't mean a memory is secure. Coming back to it a few more times is where the learning happens.

# A test that teaches wrong answers
@src Roediger & Marsh, Journal of Experimental Psychology: LMC, 2005

Multiple-choice questions show the right answer along with several wrong ones, and that has a cost. In 2005 Henry Roediger and Elizabeth Marsh had students read short passages, then take a multiple-choice test on them, and later answer the same questions without any choices. The more wrong options students had seen, the more often they later gave one of those wrong options as an answer.

Later work found that the effect shrinks when students get the correct answers soon afterward. That points to a simple rule for using practice tests. Check your answers, and do it soon. A wrong answer that you've seen but never corrected can feel familiar, and familiarity can feel like truth. It also explains why a question with wrong choices can plant an idea you didn't have before.

# The best gap between study sessions
@src Cepeda et al., Psychological Science, 2008

Spacing your study helps, but how far apart should the sessions be? In 2008 Nicholas Cepeda and his colleagues tried to answer this with more than a thousand people who learned facts and were tested after different delays. They varied both the gap between study sessions and the time until the final test, and mapped what worked best.

The best gap depended on how long you needed to remember. If the test was a week away, a gap of a day or so was best. If it was months away, a gap of weeks was better. As a rough guide, the ideal gap was about a tenth to a fifth of the time that you want to remember the material. Too short a gap is wasteful, but a gap that is too long is less costly than one that is too short.

# Cramming works until it doesn't
@src Cepeda et al., Psychological Bulletin, 2006

In 2006 Nicholas Cepeda and his colleagues gathered hundreds of experiments on distributed practice and analyzed them together. The pattern was clear. When the same amount of study was spread over several sessions, people remembered more later than when the study was crammed into one. This held for many ages and kinds of material, and it is one of the sturdiest findings in the psychology of learning.

Cramming isn't useless. It can get you through an exam the next morning, and sometimes that is all you need. But what you learn that way tends to disappear quickly, and it leaves little to build on. If the material matters to you beyond a test, it's far better to break it into short sessions and let a few days pass in between, even if each session is only a few minutes.

# Hearing words while you read
@src Salamé & Baddeley, 1982; Perham & Currie, 2014

In 1982 Pierre Salamé and Alan Baddeley found that background speech makes it harder to remember lists of items you've just read, even when you can't understand the language. Speech seems to intrude on the part of working memory that holds words in order. Steady noise without speech, and instrumental music, had much weaker effects.

Later work looked at music with lyrics. In 2014 Nick Perham and Alice Currie tested whether listening to favorite songs helps reading comprehension. It didn't, and songs with lyrics made comprehension worse than silence. Plenty of people feel that music helps them concentrate. It may help your mood or block out something worse, but when the task is understanding sentences, words in the background compete with the words on the page.

# Paper versus screen
@src Delgado et al., Educational Research Review, 2018

Do you understand more when you read on paper or on a screen? In 2018 Pablo Delgado and his colleagues combined the results of dozens of studies, covering well over a hundred thousand readers. They found that reading on paper led to better comprehension than reading on screens, and the gap was largest for informational texts and when readers were under time pressure.

The reasons are still debated. Screens may encourage skimming. People may also be more overconfident about what they understood on a screen. And paper gives a stable sense of where something is on the page and how far along you are. None of this says that screens can't be used well. It does suggest that for long or difficult material, slowing down on a screen, or printing it, can be worth it.

# The classmate's laptop
@src Sana, Weston & Cepeda, Computers & Education, 2013

In 2013 Faria Sana, Tina Weston and Nicholas Cepeda ran a lecture experiment. Students listened to a talk with a laptop and were asked to do unrelated tasks on it, such as searching the web for other things, during the lecture. Those who multitasked scored lower on a test about the talk than students who hadn't used a laptop for anything else.

The surprise came from the students sitting nearby. Some were placed where they could see a multitasking classmate's screen, and these students scored lower too, though they hadn't been asked to do anything but listen. Distraction spreads. Moving, flickering content is hard to ignore, and the person next to you may be costing you more than they cost themselves. It's a reason to think about where you sit.

# The phone on the desk
@src Ward et al., Journal of the Association for Consumer Research, 2017
@conf mixed

In 2017 Adrian Ward and his colleagues reported that people who had their phone on the desk, even silenced and untouched, did worse on tests of working memory and problem solving than people whose phone was in another room. The idea was that part of your attention is used up on not looking at the phone. The paper was widely covered under the name brain drain.

Later researchers tried to repeat the finding, and several did not find the same effect. The question is still open, and the effect may be smaller, or may depend on how attached to the phone a person is. It remains a sensible, low cost experiment to run on yourself. If you have something hard to do, put the phone out of sight, and notice whether it makes any difference to you.

# Sleeping on it
@src Wagner et al., Nature, 2004

In 2004 Ullrich Wagner and his colleagues gave people a number task with a hidden shortcut. People who worked through it could solve it the long way, but a rule that was never mentioned could make it far quicker. After training, some people slept for the night, and others stayed awake for the same length of time. Then everyone tried again.

More than half of those who had slept found the hidden rule, compared with under a quarter of those who had stayed awake. Sleep didn't help people who hadn't done the training first. It seems that during sleep, the brain reorganizes recent experience in a way that can reveal a pattern. The old advice to sleep on a problem has some support, as long as you've worked on the problem before you go to bed.

# Practicing in your head
@src Pascual-Leone et al., Journal of Neurophysiology, 1995

In 1995 Alvaro Pascual-Leone and his colleagues taught people a short five-finger piano exercise. One group practiced at the keyboard for a couple of hours a day for five days. Another group only imagined playing, in the same schedule, without moving their fingers. The researchers mapped the motor cortex with magnetic stimulation each day.

Both groups showed changes in the part of the cortex that controlled the fingers. The group that practiced physically improved more in actual playing. But once the mental-practice group had a single session at the keyboard, they caught up quickly. Later studies and reviews agree that mental rehearsal helps, though it works best as an addition to real practice and not as a replacement for it.

# Watching is not doing
@src Kardas & O'Brien, Psychological Science, 2018

Have you ever watched a how-to video and felt that you could do it? In 2018 Michael Kardas and Ed O'Brien tested this across six experiments. People watched videos of tasks such as throwing darts, doing the moonwalk and juggling. The more times they watched, the more confident they became that they could do it themselves.

But performance did not improve with more viewing. People who watched a dart video twenty times were no better at throwing darts than people who watched it once, though they predicted they would be. Watching lets you see the steps, but it doesn't teach you how they feel. The effect shrank when people picked up the props themselves. The lesson for learning any skill is to move from watching to trying as soon as you can.

# Feeling like you learned less
@src Deslauriers et al., PNAS, 2019

In 2019 Louis Deslauriers and his colleagues ran a crossover experiment in a large physics course at Harvard. For one class, half the students got a polished lecture from an expert, and half took part in an active session where they worked on problems in groups. For the next class, the groups switched. All of them took a short test afterward and rated how much they felt they had learned.

The active students scored higher on the test, but they felt they had learned less. The lecture students rated the teaching more highly and said they'd like all their classes taught that way. The effort of working things out felt like a sign of poor learning, and smooth explanation felt like a sign of good learning. It's a reminder not to trust the feeling of ease when you judge your own learning.

# Doing it yourself
@src Cohen, Scandinavian Journal of Psychology, 1981; Engelkamp & Zimmer, 1989

Suppose that you were asked to remember a list such as "break the match" and "lift the cup." Half the time you only hear the phrase, and half the time you actually do the action. Researchers found, beginning in the early 1980s, that people recall the phrases they acted out better than the phrases they only heard or read.

This is called the enactment effect, or the subject-performed task effect. It seems to work because movement adds another trace to memory, made of how it felt to do it, as well as how it sounded. It has been seen in many studies, including with older adults. For learning, it suggests that when the material allows it, such as a procedure or the names of objects in another language, doing something physically can give a boost over just reading about it.

# Drawing to remember
@src Wammes, Meade & Fernandes, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 2016

In 2016 Jeffrey Wammes, Melissa Meade and Myra Fernandes asked people to learn a list of common words, such as "apple" or "kite." For each word, they either wrote it out several times or drew a picture of it. When they were later asked to recall the words, they remembered the drawn words much better than the written ones.

The authors called it the drawing effect. Drawing seems to combine several kinds of memory at once, including the meaning of the word, the movement of the hand and the look of the picture. The drawings did not have to be good, and the benefit didn't depend on artistic skill. If you're trying to learn vocabulary or labeled parts, a quick sketch in the margin may help.

# Choking: when thinking gets in the way
@src Beilock & Carr, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2001

Why do skilled people sometimes fail when it matters most? In 2001 Sian Beilock and Thomas Carr studied golfers putting under pressure. When experienced golfers were told to pay attention to the steps of their swing, they did worse. Beginners, who haven't yet made the skill automatic, seem to need that kind of attention.

The researchers' explanation is that expert skills become automatic. When pressure makes you monitor each step, you break up a well-practiced routine. The remedy tested in later studies was to give attention something else to do, such as a rhythm or a single simple cue. It has a lesson for any performance, from speaking to playing music. After practice, trust the automatic part, and don't give it a running commentary.

# Writing your worries before an exam
@src Ramirez & Beilock, Science, 2011
@conf mixed

In 2011 Gerardo Ramirez and Sian Beilock asked students to spend ten minutes writing about their feelings about an upcoming exam just before they took it. In several experiments, students who wrote about their worries did better than those who didn't, and the benefit was greatest among students who tended to feel anxious about tests.

The explanation was that worries use up working memory, and writing them down offloads some of the load. Other labs have had mixed results when they tried to repeat it, so the effect may be smaller or work only in some conditions. It costs ten minutes and carries little risk. If you get nervous before tests or big talks, it's reasonable to try it and see if it helps you.

# Babies know when they don't know
@src Goupil, Romand-Monnier & Kouider, PNAS, 2016

Metacognition, knowing what you know, seems like an adult skill. In 2016 Louise Goupil, Margaux Romand-Monnier and Sid Kouider tested toddlers of twenty months. They hid a toy in one of two boxes and let the children try to find it, sometimes after a delay that made them unsure where it was. The children could also ask a parent for help.

When the children had been less sure, they asked for help more often, and when they were sure they searched on their own. They also tended to be right more often when they had been confident. So even very young children seem to track how reliable their own memory is. The ability grows and sharpens over the school years, and it is a skill that can be trained.

# Monkeys know when they remember
@src Hampton, PNAS, 2001

In 2001 Robert Hampton tested whether rhesus monkeys can tell when they remember something. Monkeys saw an image, then waited through a delay. On some trials, they could choose to take a memory test or decline it for a smaller reward. On other trials, they were forced to take the test.

The monkeys were more accurate when they chose the test than when they were forced to take it. They tended to decline when the delay was long or the memory was weaker. This suggests that they were monitoring something about their own memory, and not only reacting to the cues in the room. Similar work with rats has been more disputed. The finding supports the idea that a basic form of metacognition is older than humans.

# Teaching how to think about thinking
@src Education Endowment Foundation, Teaching and Learning Toolkit

Schools have tried teaching metacognition directly. That means showing students how to plan a task, check their progress and reflect on what worked afterward. The Education Endowment Foundation, a British charity that reviews the evidence, has long rated this among the most promising approaches in schools, adding several months of progress on average, and at very low cost.

The same review stresses a catch. It's hard to get that benefit in practice. The approaches that work are taught inside a particular subject and not as a general course on study skills, and students need practice with real tasks. Telling people to reflect doesn't work as well as modeling how an expert plans and checks their own work out loud. It's a good guide for teaching yourself.

# Brain games and real life
@src Owen et al., Nature, 2010; Simons et al., Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2016

In 2010 Adrian Owen and his colleagues recruited more than 11,000 people to follow online brain-training programs for several weeks. People improved at the tasks they practiced, as you'd expect. But the improvement did not carry over to other mental tasks, even ones that were closely related.

In 2016 Daniel Simons and his colleagues reviewed the evidence behind the claims of the brain-training industry. They concluded that there was good evidence that practice improves performance on the trained tasks, but little evidence that it improves everyday abilities. This doesn't mean that your brain can't change. It means that getting better at a puzzle mostly makes you better at that puzzle. To improve at something real, practice the real thing.

# The Dunning-Kruger effect, as usually told
@src Kruger & Dunning, JPSP, 1999; Gignac & Zajenkowski, Intelligence, 2020
@conf mixed

In 1999 Justin Kruger and David Dunning found that people who scored lowest on tests of humor, grammar and logic tended to estimate that they had done much better than they had. The popular summary is that unskilled people are too ignorant to know they are ignorant, and that experts are the humble ones.

The truth is more mixed. Some researchers have argued that a lot of the pattern comes from statistics. Everyone's self-ratings are noisy, and when you sort people by score, the lowest group will tend to guess too high and the highest group too low, even if people had no bias at all. Other work finds that the lowest scorers really do overestimate more than can be explained by chance. So it's not a law that fools are confident. Everyone should check their own judgments against real feedback.
