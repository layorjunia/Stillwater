# The powerful forget the other side
@conf mixed
@src Galinsky, Magee, Inesi & Gruenfeld, Psychological Science, 2006

Draw the letter E on your own forehead, as fast as you can. Some people draw it so that it reads correctly to themselves, and others draw it so that it reads correctly to someone facing them. In 2006 Adam Galinsky, Joe Magee, Marlone Inesi and Deborah Gruenfeld first made volunteers feel powerful or powerless, by asking them to recall a time when they had power over others, or when others had power over them. Then they asked them to draw the E.

People who had been made to feel powerful were almost three times as likely to draw the E so that it faced themselves, and would look backward to anyone looking at them. In other experiments the powerful were less likely to allow that other people did not share their knowledge, and less accurate at reading the emotions on faces. The effects arose from a brief feeling of power. Studies of priming have been hard to repeat, so this is suggestive, but it is a good reason for anyone in charge to ask how things look from the other side.

# Can an angry woman get ahead?
@series talk
@src Brescoll & Uhlmann, Psychological Science, 2008

Is anger judged in the same way in a man and in a woman? In 2008 Victoria Brescoll and Eric Uhlmann asked people to evaluate men and women who showed anger or sadness at work. The raters were asked how much status each person deserved. They also looked at whether it mattered if the person was described as a trainee or as a chief executive.

Angry men were given more status than sad men. Angry women were given less status than angry men, and even a woman who was a chief executive was given less status if she showed anger than if she did not. People tended to explain a woman's anger by her character, as if she were out of control, and a man's anger by the situation. When a clear outside reason for the anger was given, the difference mostly went away. The study is about how others judge, and not about how anyone should feel.

# Why powerful women talk less
@series talk
@src Brescoll, Administrative Science Quarterly, 2011

Do people who have power talk more? In 2011 Victoria Brescoll measured how long each United States senator spoke on the Senate floor, during the sessions of 2005 and 2007. She compared the time with a score for each senator's power, which was given by a non-partisan firm. She also ran an experiment in which people read about a chief executive who spoke more than others.

For men, more power meant more talking. For women, it did not, since powerful women did not talk more than less powerful ones. In the experiment, a female executive who talked a lot was rated as less competent and less suitable for leadership than a male one who talked as much. The author suggested that powerful women hold back because they expect a penalty, and that they are right to expect it. It is a case in which a rule about speaking up applies differently to different people.

# Women who succeed at men's jobs
@series mind
@src Heilman & Okimoto, Journal of Applied Psychology, 2007

When a woman succeeds in a job that is seen as a man's job, she is often liked less. In 2007 Madeline Heilman and Tyler Okimoto set out to find out why. In three experiments, people read about a manager who had succeeded in a job of that kind. Some read about a man, and some about a woman. Then they rated how likable and how hostile the manager seemed.

Their idea was that success in such a job suggests a lack of warmth and concern for others, which they called an implied communality deficit. When the evaluators were also given information that showed that the woman was caring, such as that she was a mother, or when she acted in a communal way, the penalty was reduced. It suggests that the dislike came from broken expectations, and not from the success itself. It is still true that expectations like that are unfair.

# What an unfair offer does to the brain
@series brain
@src Sanfey, Rilling, Aronson, Nystrom & Cohen, Science, 2003

In the ultimatum game, one player proposes how to split a sum of money, and another can accept, or reject, in which case both get nothing. A purely selfish responder would accept anything, since something is better than nothing. But people often reject offers that they see as unfair. In 2003 Alan Sanfey, James Rilling and their colleagues scanned the brains of responders as they received offers from other people, and from computers.

Unfair offers made the anterior insula active, a region that is linked with negative feelings, and the activity was stronger as the offer got less fair. It was especially strong before the responder rejected an offer. A region of the prefrontal cortex, which is tied to goals and reasoning, responded as well. The authors concluded that emotion plays a real part in economic choices. Later work has asked whether the insula signal means arousal, or the sense that a social rule was broken.

# When a stereotype is named out loud
@series sell
@conf mixed
@src Kray, Thompson & Galinsky, J. Personality and Social Psychology, 2001

Do stereotypes change how people negotiate? In 2001 Laura Kray, Leigh Thompson and Adam Galinsky paired men and women in a negotiation exercise. Before it, some were told that the task was a very good measure of negotiating ability, which brings to mind the stereotype that men negotiate better. For others, the stereotype was not raised.

When the stereotype was raised in a subtle way, women did worse than men. But when the men were told outright that men tend to do better than women, the women did better than the men. The authors called this reactance, which is the urge to prove a stereotype wrong. Later work found that the effect depends on how the stereotype is raised, and on what it says. It suggests that the way a task is framed can change how people perform, in either direction.

# When control is lost, patterns appear
@conf mixed
@src Whitson & Galinsky, Science, 2008

Do we see patterns more when we feel that things are out of our control? In 2008 Jennifer Whitson and Adam Galinsky asked some people to recall a time when they had no control over what happened, and others to recall a time when they were in control. Then they gave them tasks. In one, they showed grainy pictures that were mostly visual noise, some of which hid a faint object.

People who had recalled losing control were more likely to see shapes in the noise. They were also more likely to see links in made-up stock market data, to believe in conspiracies and to hold superstitions. When they were first given a chance to think about their core values, the effect shrank. The samples in each study were small, but the idea is useful. When we feel helpless, the mind looks harder for order, even when there is none.

# Thirty-six samples, thirteen effects
@series think
@src Klein et al., Social Psychology, 2014

In 2014 a large group of researchers, led by Richard Klein, tested thirteen well-known findings in psychology, each in many places. The project, called Many Labs, used thirty-six samples, in laboratories and online, with a total of 6,344 people. They used the same procedures and materials for each one, and shared the data publicly.

Ten of the thirteen effects held up. One, in which imagining contact reduces prejudice, had weak support. Two did not replicate. In one of them, seeing the American flag was supposed to make people more conservative, and in the other, seeing money was supposed to make people defend the system more. The place and the type of sample made little difference. What mattered most was the effect itself. It shows that some findings are sturdy and some are not, and that you can find out by repeating them.

# The honesty pledge and the invented data
@series think
@conf contested
@src Shu et al., PNAS, 2012 (retracted 2021); Kristal et al., PNAS, 2020

In 2012 a team of five researchers reported a neat trick for making people more honest. When people signed a statement of honesty at the top of a form, before filling it in, they cheated less than when they signed at the bottom. One of the studies was a field test with a car insurance company, in which more than thirteen thousand customers reported the mileage of their cars. The paper appeared in a leading journal, and it was widely cited.

In 2020 the authors reported that they could not repeat the laboratory experiments. In 2021 other researchers, who run a blog called Data Colada, analyzed the posted data and showed that the insurance data had been fabricated, and the journal retracted the paper. Who altered the data has not been established. The episode is a reminder that a striking result with a simple cure needs to be checked more than once, and that checking is a job for the whole field.
