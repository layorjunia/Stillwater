# The ad that called its own product a lemon
@src Doyle Dane Bernbach, 1959 and 1960; Advertising Age, 1999

In the 1950s, American car ads were loud and glossy, and promised bigger and better everything. In 1959 the agency Doyle Dane Bernbach launched a campaign for the Volkswagen Beetle, a small, odd-looking German car. The ad had a photograph of the car, with a large amount of blank space, and a headline of two words: Think small. It went against everything that was being done.

Another ad in the campaign, called Lemon, showed a picture of a Beetle, and explained that a car had been rejected because of a small blemish on the glove compartment, which an inspector had spotted. The idea was that VW was so careful that it threw out a car for a trivial flaw. The ads were funny and honest, and respectful of readers. In 1999 Advertising Age named the Volkswagen campaign the best of the century.

# Torches of Freedom
@src Bernays, Propaganda, 1928; Brandt, The Cigarette Century, 2007

In the 1920s it was considered improper for women to smoke in public, and cigarette companies were missing half the market. The American Tobacco Company hired Edward Bernays, a pioneer of public relations, and a nephew of Sigmund Freud. In March 1929 he arranged for a group of young women to march in the Easter Parade on Fifth Avenue in New York, and to light cigarettes together at a signal.

He had tipped off the press that the women would be lighting torches of freedom, as a protest for the equality of the sexes. The photographs appeared in newspapers across the country. Bernays understood that people respond to a story, and not to a sales pitch, and that a product can be tied to a cause. Smoking rates among women rose in the following years. It's often seen as the birth of modern public relations, and as a cautionary tale.

# The subliminal popcorn hoax
@src Rogers, Public Relations Quarterly, 1992

In 1957 a market researcher named James Vicary announced that he had flashed the messages Eat Popcorn and Drink Coca-Cola on a movie screen for a fraction of a second, too fast for viewers to notice. He claimed that sales of popcorn rose by almost sixty percent, and sales of Coke by eighteen percent. The story created a panic about mind control, and it led to proposals for laws against subliminal advertising.

In 1962 Vicary admitted that he had made it up, since he had not had enough data. No one had been able to reproduce his results. Later research has shown that very brief messages can have small effects in the lab, but there's no evidence that they can make you do something that you don't want to do. The hoax shows how readily people believe in the power of hidden persuasion.

# A hidden brand, for the thirsty
@src Karremans, Stroebe & Claus, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 2006
@conf mixed

Can a hidden message ever influence a choice? In 2006 Johan Karremans and his colleagues in the Netherlands tested it. Students did a computer task, in which the brand name Lipton Ice was flashed on screen for a few hundredths of a second, too fast to read. For others, a meaningless string of letters was flashed instead. Afterward, they were asked which drink they would prefer.

The students who had been primed with the brand were more likely to choose Lipton Ice, but only if they were thirsty. It suggests that a brief message may tip a choice that someone already wants to make, but doesn't create a new desire. Other labs have had mixed results, and the effects are small. It's an answer to the question of whether Vicary's hoax had any truth. It's a little, in narrow conditions.

# The thirty-five dollar swoosh
@src Knight, Shoe Dog, 2016

In 1971 Phil Knight, who ran a small company that sold running shoes, needed a logo. He asked Carolyn Davidson, a graphic design student at Portland State University, who had done some work for him. She came up with several designs. Knight reportedly said that he didn't love it, but that he thought it would grow on him. She was paid thirty-five dollars for her time.

The design was the swoosh, which became one of the best-known logos in the world. The company named itself Nike, after the Greek goddess of victory. In 1983, when Nike had become hugely successful, Knight gave Davidson a gold ring with a swoosh set in diamonds, and an envelope with shares of the company. A logo that cost thirty-five dollars is now worth far more than that.

# Coca-Cola did not invent Santa's red suit
@src Sundblom, Coca-Cola archives, 1931; Nast, Harper's Weekly, 1860s

It's often said that Coca-Cola invented the modern Santa Claus, with his red suit and white beard. The truth is a little different. Santa was shown in red as early as the 1800s. The cartoonist Thomas Nast drew a plump, jolly Santa in a red suit for Harper's Weekly in the 1860s, and many other images of Santa in red appeared before the 1930s, in stores, magazines and advertisements.

What Coca-Cola did, from 1931, was to commission the artist Haddon Sundblom to paint Santa for its Christmas ads, and the ads ran for more than thirty years. They showed a warm, human, friendly Santa, drinking a Coke. It made the image more widespread, and more consistent. It's a case of a company taking an existing idea, and by repeating it, making it its own.

# Attention, interest, desire, action
@src Strong, The Psychology of Selling and Advertising, 1925

Advertisers have long used a simple model of how a sale works. First, you get the customer's attention. Then you arouse their interest. Then you create a desire for the product. Finally, you move them to action, such as buying. The four steps are known by their initial letters, AIDA. The model is usually credited to an American advertising pioneer, E. St. Elmo Lewis, who proposed it around 1898.

The scheme has been criticized as too simple, since people don't always move through the steps in order, and some buy without much desire. But it's a useful checklist for a message. Does it catch attention? Does it hold interest? Does it make the person want it? Does it make it easy to act? It's still taught in business schools more than a hundred years later.

# When people see through the pitch
@src Friestad & Wright, Journal of Consumer Research, 1994

How do we deal with attempts to persuade us? In 1994 Marian Friestad and Peter Wright proposed that all of us develop what they called persuasion knowledge. Over the years, we learn how advertisers and salespeople work. We learn to recognize their tricks, such as a fake sale, a flattering remark or an appeal to fear, and we learn to guess their motives.

When people notice a persuasion attempt, they tend to resist. They become more skeptical, and may react against the message. The knowledge develops with age, and children before the age of seven or eight often don't see that an ad is trying to sell. It's why marketers try to hide the ad, as in sponsored content or product placement. Being aware of the techniques is our main defense.

# Banner blindness
@src Benway & Lane, 1998

Early in the history of the web, researchers noticed something odd. People looking for information on a web page often failed to see things that were in plain view, if they looked like advertisements. In 1998 Jan Panero Benway and David Lane tested this. They designed a page in which the important information was in a large, brightly colored banner at the top.

Many of the users, who were searching for the information, didn't notice it. They had learned to ignore the area at the top, and anything that looked like an ad. It's called banner blindness. Later eye-tracking studies confirmed that people tend to look at the content, and skip over the places where ads usually are. Advertisers responded by making ads look more like content, which is why some of them are hard to tell apart.

# The first banner ad
@src HotWired, October 27, 1994

On October 27, 1994, the online magazine HotWired put an advertisement on its web page. It was a rectangular banner, and it was paid for by AT&T. It said, Have you ever clicked your mouse right here? YOU WILL. An arrow pointed at the words. It was one of the first ads to be displayed on the web.

It reportedly drew a response from a large share of those who saw it. Figures of forty-four percent are often given, though the web had few users, and the ad was a novelty. Today the average rate of clicking on a banner is a small fraction of a percent. The first banner shows how a new medium can make a message stand out, and how the effect fades as people get used to it.

# The button that raised sign-ups
@src Siroker, Optimizely; Christian, Wired, 2012

In 2007 the presidential campaign of Barack Obama used a website to collect the email addresses of supporters. A young engineer named Dan Siroker, who was working with the campaign, suggested that they test different versions of the page. They tried several different buttons, with words such as Sign Up, Learn More and Join Us Now. They also tried different images and videos.

The best combination, a family photograph and the button Learn More, produced about forty percent more sign-ups than the original. Over the course of the campaign, the team estimated that the improvement brought in millions of extra email addresses, and tens of millions of dollars in donations. Siroker later started a company, Optimizely, that offered such tests to others. It showed that small details can have big effects, and that guesses about what works are often wrong.

# Why a song gets stuck
@src Liikkanen, Psychology of Music, 2012; Hyman et al., Applied Cognitive Psychology, 2013

Almost everyone knows the experience of having a tune play over and over in the mind, without wanting it to. It's called an earworm, a word borrowed from German, Ohrwurm. In surveys, around nine in ten people say that they experience it at least once a week. Most say that it's not very bothersome.

Research has found some patterns. Earworms tend to be songs that were heard recently, and often. They are usually simple, with a fast tempo and a repeating melody. A trigger might be a memory, a word or a mood. Some people try to get rid of a song by chewing gum, which interferes with the inner voice, and a study found that it helped. Listening to the whole song may also work, or solving a puzzle. Advertisers use catchy jingles on purpose.
