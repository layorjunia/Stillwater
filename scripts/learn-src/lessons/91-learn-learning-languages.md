# When does the window for grammar close?
@src Hartshorne, Tenenbaum & Pinker, Cognition, 2018

It's often said that children learn languages better than adults. But when exactly does the ability fall, and how fast? In 2018 Joshua Hartshorne, Joshua Tenenbaum and Steven Pinker put a grammar quiz online. It was taken by about two-thirds of a million people, from many countries, who had begun learning English at all kinds of ages. Because the quiz went viral, the sample was far larger than any lab could recruit.

The ability to pick up grammar stayed high until about the age of seventeen, and only then began to decline. That is much later than most researchers had expected. Reaching a fully native-like level still seemed to need an early start, but people who began later could reach a high level. The authors suggested that the decline may reflect changes in life, such as leaving school and having less time for immersion, as well as biology. Starting early helps, but starting late is not hopeless.

# No sudden end to the learning years
@src Hakuta, Bialystok & Wiley, Psychological Science, 2003
@conf mixed

Is there a sudden end to the best years for learning a language, as the critical period idea says, or does the ability fade slowly? In 2003 Kenji Hakuta, Ellen Bialystok and Edward Wiley used the 1990 United States census, which asked immigrants how well they spoke English. They looked at about 2.3 million people from Spanish and Chinese speaking backgrounds, and at the age at which each person had arrived.

If a critical period existed, they argued, there should be a sharp break in the results at some age. They tested for a break at fifteen and at twenty, and found none. English skill fell steadily as the age of arrival rose, with no sudden jump. A critic challenged how the data were analyzed, and the authors replied that further tests gave the same answer. The debate over the critical period is not settled, but this study made the case for a gradual decline.

# Subtitles and listening
@src Mitterer & McQueen, PLoS ONE, 2009

Many people learn a language by watching films with subtitles. Does it help? In 2009 Holger Mitterer and James McQueen tested Dutch students who found Scottish and Australian English hard to understand. The students watched short videos with Dutch subtitles, with English subtitles that matched what was said, or with no subtitles. Afterward, they had to repeat what they heard in audio clips.

English subtitles gave the best results, both for clips the students had seen and for new clips in the same accents. Dutch subtitles helped with the clips they had already seen, but made them worse at understanding new ones. The researchers suggested that the viewers used the meaning given in their own language to decode the speech, and so did not learn how the accent sounded. If you want to learn from a film, use subtitles in the language of the film.

# How many words do you need?
@src Nation, Canadian Modern Language Review, 2006

How many words must a learner know to read a book or follow a conversation? In 2006 the linguist Paul Nation analyzed large collections of written and spoken English, and counted how much of each text is covered by the most common words. He counted word families, such as help, helps, helped and helpful, as a single item.

The most common few thousand families cover most of the words in everyday English, but the last few percent are hard to get. To know about ninety-eight percent of the words, which is roughly what is needed to read without help, takes eight to nine thousand families for written text, and six to seven thousand for speech. So the first words are the most valuable, and each later word brings less. It makes sense to learn the most common words first.

# Learning vocabulary during sleep
@src Schreiner & Rasch, Cerebral Cortex, 2015
@conf mixed

Can you learn a language in your sleep? Earlier studies found that people do not learn new material that is played to them while they are asleep. In 2015 Thomas Schreiner and Björn Rasch tried something more modest, which was to use sleep to strengthen words that had already been learned. German speakers studied Dutch words with their German translations in the evening, and then slept in a lab. During deep sleep, some of the Dutch words were quietly played again, without the translations.

The next morning, the sleepers remembered the translations of the replayed words better than those of the words that were not replayed. The words that were not replayed were remembered about as well as in a group that heard nothing. Replaying the words while people were awake did not help. The cue seems to reactivate a memory that is already there, so the words had to be studied first. It is a lab finding, and not a shortcut to learning.

# Language learning changes the brain
@src Mechelli et al., Nature, 2004

Does learning a second language leave a mark on the brain? In 2004 Andrea Mechelli and her colleagues at University College London scanned the brains of eighty-three people. Twenty-five spoke only one language. The others spoke English and Italian, twenty-five of whom had learned their second language early in life, and thirty-three later. The scans measured the density of gray matter, the tissue that contains the cell bodies of neurons.

The bilingual people had denser gray matter in the left inferior parietal cortex, a region involved in language, than the others. The difference was larger in those who had learned their second language early, and it was related to how well they spoke it. That fits the idea that the brain changes with experience. But a scan is a snapshot, so it cannot show which came first, and later studies that followed people as they learned were needed to test the idea.

# Languages and aging
@src Bak, Nissan, Allerhand & Deary, Annals of Neurology, 2014
@conf mixed

In 1947 almost every eleven-year-old in Scotland took an intelligence test. In 2008 to 2010, Thomas Bak and his colleagues at the University of Edinburgh tracked down 853 of the people born in 1936 who had taken it, and tested them again at about seventy-three. Of these, 262 said that they spoke at least one language besides English. The researchers asked whether the later test results were better or worse than the scores at eleven would predict.

The bilingual people did better than predicted, especially in general intelligence and reading. The benefit appeared even in those who had learned their second language as adults. The effect was small, and the study was observational, so it cannot show that language learning caused the difference. Other studies of bilingualism and aging have had mixed results. It is a promising hint, but not a proof.

# Do language apps work?
@src Vesselinov & Grego, Duolingo Effectiveness Study, 2012
@conf mixed

Language apps have millions of users, but do they teach? In 2012 Roumen Vesselinov, of Queens College, and John Grego, of the University of South Carolina, studied the best known of them, Duolingo. The company paid for the study, and the researchers say that they did the analysis independently. Volunteers who answered an ad took a Spanish test, used the app for about eight weeks, and took a test again.

The report estimated that about thirty-four hours of use equaled a semester of college Spanish. Critics pointed out that the volunteers answered an ad, and that the study did not show that the app was better than other ways of learning. Independent research on language apps is limited. They may help with vocabulary and with building a daily habit, but it is good to add real conversation as well.

# Why English is hard to learn to read
@src Seymour, Aro & Erskine, British Journal of Psychology, 2003

Why does learning to read English take children so long? In 2003 Philip Seymour, Mikko Aro and Jane Erskine tested young children in thirteen European writing systems, including English. They checked how well children in their first year of school knew their letters, read familiar words, and sounded out simple made-up words.

Most European children became accurate and fluent by the end of the first year. The exceptions were children learning French, Portuguese, Danish and, above all, English. English reading developed more than twice as slowly as in the languages with the most regular spelling, such as Finnish and Italian. The gap was not explained by when the children started school or by how well they knew their letters. It was the spelling system itself, in which one letter can stand for many sounds, that made the first steps slower.

# Dyslexia in three languages
@src Paulesu et al., Science, 2001
@conf mixed

Dyslexia is a difficulty with reading, and it is found in every country. But does it look the same in every language? In 2001 Eraldo Paulesu and an international team studied dyslexic adults in Italy, France and Britain, and compared them with ordinary readers in each country. They tested reading and sound skills, and used PET scans to look at brain activity during reading.

The Italian dyslexic readers did better at reading than the French and British ones, since Italian spelling is regular and easier to sound out. But compared with the ordinary readers in their own country, all three groups were equally impaired, and all showed reduced activity in the same region of the left temporal lobe. The researchers concluded that dyslexia has a common biological basis, and that the language decides how much it shows. A later study of Chinese readers suggested that the brain differences may depend on the writing system.

# Singing foreign phrases
@src Ludke, Ferreira & Overy, Memory & Cognition, 2014

Is it easier to remember foreign phrases if you sing them? In a study published in 2014, Karen Ludke and her colleagues taught Hungarian phrases to sixty adults who did not know the language. They were assigned at random to learn by speaking the phrases, by speaking them in rhythm, or by singing them to a simple tune. Each group had fifteen minutes.

Afterward, all of them were tested. The singing group did better overall than the other two, and it was best at remembering the phrases word for word. Because one group spoke in rhythm, the benefit was not due to rhythm alone. A tune may link the words together, so that each one cues the next. It was a short test with phrases, and not a whole language, but it suggests that a song is a good way to hold a phrase in mind.

# Acting out new words
@src Macedonia, Müller & Friederici, Human Brain Mapping, 2011
@conf mixed

Do hand gestures help us remember new words? In 2011 Manuela Macedonia and her colleagues at the Max Planck Institute in Leipzig taught adults new words from an artificial language. Some of the words were paired with gestures that acted out the meaning, and others with gestures that had nothing to do with it.

The words that were learned with meaningful gestures were remembered better over time. When the learners later recognized the words inside a brain scanner, the movement areas of their cortex were active for those words, as if the memory included the action. The effect is not guaranteed, since some later studies found that gestures can sometimes get in the way of learning words. Still, if you are learning vocabulary, it is worth trying to act the words out.

# Thinking in a foreign tongue
@src Keysar, Hayakawa & An, Psychological Science, 2012
@conf mixed

Do we make different decisions in a foreign language? In 2012 Boaz Keysar, Sayuri Hayakawa and Sun Gyu An tested students who had learned a foreign language in class. In one test, people read a problem about a disease outbreak, in which the same outcome was described either as lives saved or as lives lost. In their own language, people usually chose differently depending on the wording.

In the foreign language, that effect mostly disappeared. In other experiments, people were more willing to accept bets with a good expected payoff, so they seemed less afraid of losing. The researchers suggested that a foreign language creates distance, and moves us from gut feelings toward slower, more deliberate thinking. Others have suggested that it simply carries less emotion. The effect has been found with many languages, but how it works is still debated.
