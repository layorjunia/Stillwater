# Counting what is easy to count
@conf mixed
@src Yankelovich, 1971 and 1972

The social scientist Daniel Yankelovich described, in the early 1970s, a trap that he named after Robert McNamara, the executive who ran Ford and then the Defense Department. The first step is to measure whatever can be easily measured, which is fine as far as it goes. The second is to disregard what cannot be easily measured, or to give it an arbitrary value. The third is to presume that what cannot be measured is not important.

The fourth step is to say that what cannot be measured does not exist. Later writers applied the idea to the Vietnam War, where the count of enemy dead was treated as a precise sign of progress, and the feelings of the people in the villages were left out. The point applies to schools, hospitals and companies. A number can tell you something real, but it is never the whole thing. If it is the only thing that you look at, you will miss the rest.

# The road that made traffic worse
@src Braess, 1968; Stuttgart, 1969

Imagine that 4,000 drivers must get from a start to an end. There are two routes. Each has one road that takes 45 minutes whatever the traffic, and one road that takes the number of cars divided by 100 minutes. The roads are arranged so that the drivers split evenly, and each trip takes 20 minutes plus 45, which is 65 minutes.

Now add a free shortcut between the two middle points. Every driver sees that the best choice is to use both of the variable roads, since that is faster for one person, and so all 4,000 use them. Each trip now takes 40 minutes plus 40, which is 80 minutes. Everyone is worse off. The German mathematician Dietrich Braess described this in 1968. It is said that in Stuttgart in 1969, and in New York in 1990, closing a road made traffic flow better.

# When a lower price means less is bought
@src Jensen & Miller, American Economic Review, 2008

Most goods sell more when they become cheaper. In economics textbooks there is an exception, called a Giffen good, named after the Victorian economist Robert Giffen. It is a staple food that is so important to a very poor family that when its price falls, they buy less of it. The reason is that the money that they save lets them buy a little meat or something else that they want more, instead of the staple.

It was long thought to be a curiosity, and the stories about potatoes in Ireland are doubted. In 2008 Robert Jensen and Nolan Miller tested it. They gave very poor households in the Chinese province of Hunan a subsidy on rice, which made it cheaper, and they measured what the families bought. They found strong evidence of Giffen behavior for rice. The households bought less rice when it was cheaper. The effect depended on how poor the family was.

# Does more money make a country happier?
@conf mixed
@src Easterlin, 1974; Stevenson & Wolfers, Brookings Papers on Economic Activity, 2008

In 1974 the economist Richard Easterlin pointed to what looked like a puzzle. Within a country, richer people say that they are happier than poorer people. But when he compared countries, and looked at countries over time, he found little sign that a country's average happiness rose as it grew richer. His idea was that people judge their income by comparison with others, so when everyone gets richer, no one feels it. This was called the Easterlin paradox.

In 2008 Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers collected much more data, from many more countries and years. They found a clear link between average happiness and income per person across countries, and no sign that the link fades at high levels. In the countries that grew, happiness tended to rise as well. They concluded that absolute income matters a good deal, and comparison with others matters less. Easterlin has defended his view, so the argument continues.

# The tutors who were not teachers
@src Banerjee, Cole, Duflo & Linden, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2007

In many schools in India, children reach the later grades without being able to read well. In the early 2000s the organization Pratham ran a program in the cities of Mumbai and Vadodara. It hired young women from the neighborhood, called balsakhis, who were not trained teachers, to work with the children who were furthest behind, for part of each day. The economists Abhijit Banerjee, Shawn Cole, Esther Duflo and Leigh Linden evaluated it by an experiment.

Schools were assigned to the program by chance. The average test scores in the schools rose by about a quarter of a standard deviation after two years, and most of the gain came from the children at the bottom. The children who were not tutored saw no measurable gain. The program was cheap. Banerjee and Duflo shared the Nobel Prize in economics in 2019 for this approach of testing ways to help the poor by randomized trials.

# Why shops cluster
@src Hotelling, Economic Journal, 1929

Suppose that two ice cream sellers set up on a long beach, with customers spread evenly along it, and that each customer buys from the nearer seller. Where should each seller stand? Put them at one-quarter and three-quarters along the beach and each serves half the customers, with the shortest walk for the customers. But a seller can do better by moving a little toward the middle, taking some of the other's customers.

The economist Harold Hotelling showed in 1929 that the sellers end up back to back, in the middle. The customers are worse off, because the average walk is longer, though the sellers gain nothing. The idea is used to explain why gas stations, shops and even political parties tend to look alike. In politics it says that candidates will move toward the center to catch the votes of the voter in the middle.

# Why big groups do not act
@src Olson, The Logic of Collective Action, 1965

If everyone in a group would gain from a certain action, do they act? In 1965 the economist Mancur Olson argued that often they do not. If a benefit goes to everyone, whether or not they helped, each person has a reason to let the others do the work. This is called free riding. It is a bigger problem when the group is large, since each person's share of the effort matters less and is harder to see.

Olson used it to explain why small groups, such as a few firms in an industry, often organize better than large ones, such as consumers or taxpayers. Large groups hold together when they can give their members private benefits that others do not get, such as insurance or discounts, or when they can require membership. It explains why a trade association can lobby more effectively than millions of consumers, whose interests are the same.

# The curve that broke
@conf mixed
@src Phillips, Economica, 1958; Friedman, 1968

In 1958 the New Zealand economist A. W. Phillips plotted the rate at which wages rose in the United Kingdom against the unemployment rate, for the years from 1861 to 1957. He found that when unemployment was low, wages rose faster, and when it was high, they rose slowly. Others turned it into a menu for governments. If you accept a little more inflation, you can have less unemployment.

In 1967 and 1968 Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps argued that it would break down, since workers would come to expect inflation and demand higher wages. In the 1970s many countries had high inflation and high unemployment together, which was called stagflation. The curve has since been revived in a modified form, in which expectations matter. It is a lesson in how a pattern that holds for years can fail once people act on it.

# The stages of a bubble
@conf mixed
@src Kindleberger, Manias, Panics, and Crashes, 1978

The economist Charles Kindleberger, building on the work of Hyman Minsky, described how financial bubbles tend to unfold in his book Manias, Panics, and Crashes. It starts with a displacement, which is some new event that changes the outlook, such as a new technology or a drop in interest rates. Then comes a boom, in which prices rise and credit grows, and then euphoria, when buyers purchase because prices are rising.

Then come profit taking, when insiders begin to sell, and panic, when prices fall and everyone tries to get out at once. He gave examples from the Dutch tulip mania to the crash of 1929. The pattern is a description, and not a forecast, since no one knows when euphoria will turn. But it is a useful check when you hear that this time is different. Many bubbles share these stages.

# The problem that experts got wrong
@src vos Savant, Parade, 1990; Herbranson & Schroeder, Journal of Comparative Psychology, 2010

You are on a game show, and there are three doors. Behind one is a car, and behind the others are goats. You pick door one. The host, who knows where the car is, opens door three and shows a goat. He asks if you want to switch to door two. In 1990 Marilyn vos Savant said in her column in Parade magazine that you should switch, since that wins two times in three. She received thousands of letters, many from people with doctorates, who told her that she was wrong.

She was right. The first choice has a one in three chance, and the host's action leaves the other two thirds on the remaining door. In 2010 Walter Herbranson and Julia Schroeder tested pigeons on a version of the game with colored lights. Over a month the pigeons moved from switching about a third of the time to about 96 percent, while people tested on a similar task did not learn the best strategy. The pigeons learned by counting what worked, and not from rules of thumb.

# Hiding something makes people look
@src Streisand v. Adelman, 2003

In 2002 the photographer Kenneth Adelman took thousands of aerial photographs of the California coast, to record erosion, and put them online. One showed the clifftop home of the singer Barbra Streisand, in Malibu. In May 2003 she sued him for fifty million dollars, for invasion of privacy, asking the court to have the picture removed. Before the lawsuit, the picture had been downloaded six times, and two of those were by her own lawyers.

After the suit was reported, the website had about 420,000 visitors in a month. The court threw out the case, and she had to pay Adelman's legal fees. In 2005 the writer Mike Masnick gave the pattern a name, the Streisand effect. It is what happens when an attempt to hide or remove something draws a lot of attention to it. It is a reason to think twice before making a fuss about something that few have seen.

# When the number becomes the goal
@src Campbell, 1979; Georgia Governor's report, 2011

The social scientist Donald Campbell wrote that the more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision making, the more subject it is to corruption pressures, and the more apt it is to distort and corrupt the social processes that it is intended to monitor. It is known as Campbell's law. It says that if you attach rewards and punishments to a number, people will find ways to improve the number, and not always the thing that it measures.

A case is the Atlanta Public Schools. In 2011 a state investigation reported that 178 educators, including 38 principals, had taken part in cheating on the state's tests, and that it had happened in 44 of the 56 schools examined. Teachers erased wrong answers, and filled in right ones. Pressure to meet targets was blamed by some of those involved. In 2015 a jury convicted 11 of 12 educators who were tried. A test score is useful, but it can be corrupted when too much rides on it.

# Why a crowd can be right
@src Condorcet, Essay on the Application of Analysis to the Probability of Majority Decisions, 1785

In 1785 the French mathematician the Marquis de Condorcet proved something about group decisions. Suppose that a group has to choose between two options, one of which is right. Each member is more likely than not to be right, say 60 percent, and each decides on their own. Then the majority will be right more often than any one member, and the larger the group, the more likely that the majority is right.

With 3 members at 60 percent the majority is right 65 percent of the time. With 11 members it is right about 75 percent of the time, and with 101 members it is right about 98 percent of the time. The result cuts both ways. If each member is more likely to be wrong, at 40 percent right, the larger the group the surer it is to be wrong. And it needs the members to decide independently, which is hard when they copy each other.

# The filter that creates a false link
@src Berkson, Biometrics Bulletin, 1946

In 1946 the physician and statistician Joseph Berkson pointed out a trap in studies that use patients in hospitals. Suppose that two diseases are unrelated in the general population. A person with both is more likely to be admitted to the hospital than a person with only one. So among the patients, it can look as if people who have one disease are less likely to have the other, though no such link exists outside the hospital.

The trap applies whenever a group is chosen by a filter that depends on two traits. A familiar example is the claim that among the people you date, the good-looking ones are less kind. If you only date people who are good-looking or kind or both, then among those whom you date, those who are not good-looking must be kind. The link comes from your filter. It is worth asking, before you see a connection, how the sample was chosen.
