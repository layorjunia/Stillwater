# Gallium, element 31
@src Lecoq de Boisbaudran, 1875

Gallium is a soft metal that melts at 29.8 degrees Celsius, just below body temperature, so a lump of it will melt in your hand. It's a favorite of science teachers, who sometimes show a spoon made of it dissolving in a cup of hot tea.

In 1871 Mendeleev predicted it as "eka-aluminum," and in 1875 Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran discovered it, and measured properties that matched. Mendeleev wrote to him to say that the density he had reported was a little off, and he turned out to be right. Today, compounds of gallium are used in blue and white LED lights and in some of the most efficient solar cells. It's a good example of a prediction that came true, to the decimal.

# Germanium, element 32
@src Bell Labs, 1947

In December 1947, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain at Bell Labs built the first working transistor, and its crystal was germanium. The transistor replaced bulky vacuum tubes that were hot, fragile and burned out, and it launched the age of electronics.

It won a Nobel Prize for them and for William Shockley, who led the group. Silicon later took over as the main material because it's cheaper and works at higher temperatures, but germanium is still found in some fiber-optic and infrared devices. Mendeleev had predicted it too, as "eka-silicon," and it was found in 1886 by Clemens Winkler, who named it for Germany, his homeland. So the element behind the first transistor was one of the table's successful predictions.

# Arsenic, element 33
@src Marsh, 1836

For centuries, arsenic was the classic murder poison. It was odorless and tasteless, and its symptoms resembled those of common illnesses, so it earned the nickname "inheritance powder." Poisoners could get away with it because no one could prove what had happened.

In 1836 the chemist James Marsh devised a sensitive test that could detect even tiny traces in a body. It was the first time that courts had reliable forensic proof of arsenic poisoning, and it made such murders much harder to get away with. Today, arsenic is still a problem of a different kind. In parts of Bangladesh and India, well water naturally contains it, and millions of people have been exposed. Testing the water is the first step toward fixing it.

# Selenium, element 34
@src NIH

Selenium is a trace element that your body needs in tiny amounts to make antioxidant enzymes, but too much is toxic. The gap between enough and too much is narrow, which makes it a tricky mineral to take as a supplement.

Brazil nuts are unusually rich in it, and a single nut can contain more than a day's supply. Its name comes from Selene, the Greek goddess of the Moon, chosen because it was found along with tellurium, whose name comes from the Latin for Earth. Soil in some parts of the world has little selenium, and people there can run short, while in other places, there's so much that it harms livestock. Most people who eat a normal diet get enough without thinking about it.

# Bromine, element 35
@src Standard chemistry text

Bromine and mercury are the only two elements that are liquid at room temperature. Bromine is a dark reddish-brown liquid that gives off a harsh, irritating vapor, and its name comes from the Greek for "stench."

Compounds of bromine have been used in photographic film, in fire retardants and in some medicines. Bromides were once a common sedative, and in the nineteenth century were given for nervousness and epilepsy. Their use faded when safer drugs came along. The element is found in seawater, and the Dead Sea is especially rich in it. Today, it's still used for disinfecting swimming pools and hot tubs, where a bromine compound does a job like that of chlorine.

# Krypton, element 36
@src BIPM

For 23 years, the meter was defined by this gas. From 1960 to 1983, one meter was officially 1,650,763.73 wavelengths of the orange-red light given off by krypton-86 atoms. That was more precise and reproducible than the old metal bar kept in Paris.

In 1983 it was replaced by a definition based on the speed of light, which is even more precise. The name comes from the Greek for "hidden," because it makes up only a tiny trace of the air and was discovered by William Ramsay and Morris Travers in 1898. Superman's home planet shares its name, though the comics' fictional mineral has nothing to do with the real element. It's still used in some lighting and in high-speed photography flashes.

# Rubidium, element 37
@src NIST

Atoms of rubidium can be made to flip between two energy states at an extremely precise frequency, so they make excellent timekeepers. Small rubidium atomic clocks are cheap and compact enough to be used in cell phone towers, in satellites and in telecommunications equipment, where accurate timing is vital.

The name comes from the Latin for deep red, from the color of its spectral lines. Robert Bunsen and Gustav Kirchhoff discovered it in 1861, and it was one of the first elements found with the help of the spectroscope. The metal itself is soft and reacts violently with water, but the atoms in a clock are kept in a small sealed glass cell. It's the little cousin of the cesium clock, and a lot cheaper.

# Strontium, element 38
@src Standard chemistry text

Strontium salts burn with a brilliant crimson color, and they're used in red fireworks and emergency flares. The next time you see a vivid red in a fireworks display, you're likely looking at strontium. It was named for Strontian, a village in Scotland where the mineral was first found.

It also has a more worrying form. Strontium-90, a radioactive isotope released by nuclear weapons tests and accidents, is dangerous because the body mistakes it for calcium and deposits it in the bones, where it irradiates the marrow for years. After the Chernobyl accident in 1986, it was one of the contaminants that people worried about. It's a clear case where a chemical lookalike lets a hazard slip into the body.

# Ytterby: the village with four elements
@src Standard chemistry text

Not far from Stockholm in Sweden, there's a village called Ytterby, and it has given its name to four elements: yttrium, ytterbium, terbium and erbium. The reason is a quarry there, which yielded a heavy black mineral that was found in 1787 by an army lieutenant, Carl Axel Arrhenius.

Over the next hundred years, chemists found that this one mineral was a mixture of many new elements, and several of them were named for the place. Yttrium compounds are used in LED lights, in lasers and in superconductors, and they also helped create the red in old television screens. Gadolinium, holmium and others from the same family also trace back to the quarry. No other place on Earth has so many chemical elements named after it.

# Zirconium, element 40
@src Standard chemistry text

Zirconium is remarkable because it barely absorbs neutrons, which makes it ideal for holding nuclear fuel. The fuel rods in most nuclear reactors are covered in a zirconium alloy, and it's also very resistant to corrosion and heat.

The mineral zircon is also the source of cubic zirconia, the sparkling diamond lookalike used in jewelry, which is made from zirconium dioxide. Zircon crystals themselves have been used to learn about the early Earth. Some tiny zircon grains from Western Australia are 4.4 billion years old, the oldest pieces of the Earth's crust ever found. Because the crystals are so tough, they have survived while almost everything else from that time has been destroyed.

# Niobium, element 41
@src Standard chemistry text

Alloys of niobium and titanium become superconductors when cooled below about 10 kelvin, meaning they carry electricity with no resistance at all. They're used to make the powerful magnets in MRI machines and in particle accelerators, including the Large Hadron Collider.

The element was named for Niobe, the daughter of Tantalus in Greek mythology, since it's always found with tantalum and is chemically very similar. For a long time chemists confused the two, and some in the US called it columbium instead. The name niobium was officially adopted in 1950. Most of the world's niobium comes from a single large mine in Brazil, and it's added in small amounts to steel to make it stronger.

# Molybdenum, element 42
@src Standard chemistry text

Molybdenum has one of the highest melting points of all metals, about 2,620 degrees Celsius, and it makes steel stronger at high temperatures. It's used in jet engines, in tools and in the pipelines that carry oil and gas.

It's also essential to life. Plants need it for the enzyme that turns nitrogen into forms they can use, and many enzymes in your own body contain it. It's named from the Greek word for lead, because it was once confused with it. In fact, the "lead" in a pencil is really graphite, and for a long time graphite and molybdenum were mixed up, too. It's a modest metal with a quietly large role in both industry and biology.

# Technetium, element 43
@src Perrier & Segrè, 1937

Technetium was the first element to be made artificially. In 1937 Emilio Segrè, an Italian physicist, got a strip of molybdenum that had been bombarded in a cyclotron at Berkeley, and with Carlo Perrier found the new element in it. The name comes from the Greek for "artificial."

It has no stable isotopes, so it's nearly absent in nature. One form, technetium-99m, is widely used in medicine. It's involved in about 40 million diagnostic scans every year. The "m" stands for metastable, and the isotope gives off a gamma ray and has a half-life of about six hours, which is short enough to be safe for patients and long enough to carry out a scan. Mendeleev had left a gap for it, so it was another piece that fit the table.

# Ruthenium, rhodium and palladium
@src Claus, 1844; Wollaston, 1803

These three are among the platinum-group metals, rare and valuable cousins of platinum that tend to turn up in the same ores. Ruthenium was named for Ruthenia, the Latin name for Russia. The Russian-Baltic chemist Karl Claus identified it in 1844, from platinum ore from the Ural Mountains. It's used to harden platinum and palladium, and for tough electrical contacts.

Rhodium is one of the rarest and most valuable metals. About 80 percent of it goes to make catalytic converters, which turn the most harmful gases in car exhaust into less harmful ones. Its name comes from the Greek for rose, because many of its salts are pink or red. Palladium, which William Wollaston discovered in 1802 and named for the asteroid Pallas, can absorb hundreds of times its own volume of hydrogen. It too is used in catalytic converters. At times, rhodium and palladium have each been priced higher than gold.

# Silver, element 47
@src Standard chemistry text

Silver is the best conductor of both electricity and heat among all the metals. It tarnishes because it reacts with tiny traces of sulfur in the air, forming black silver sulfide. That's why old silverware turns dark, and why a piece kept in a sealed bag stays bright.

Silver compounds were once the light-sensitive part of all photographic film and paper, because they darken when exposed to light. Photography as it was practiced for a century and a half depended on it. It's also a natural antibacterial, which is why silver was used in medicine. Ancient people knew that water stored in silver vessels kept better, and today it's still used in some wound dressings. Its chemical symbol, Ag, comes from argentum, the Latin word for it.

# Cadmium, element 48
@src Standard toxicology text

Cadmium was used in rechargeable batteries and in pigments, but it's a poison. In the early 1900s, people in parts of Japan along the Jinzu River developed itai-itai disease, whose name means "ouch-ouch." It was a painful bone disease caused by mine waste containing cadmium that got into rice and water.

It led to stricter limits on cadmium, and today its use is much more limited. Cadmium builds up in the kidneys over many years, and cigarette smoke is one of the main ways people take it in. It's a story about how a pollutant can travel through the food chain, from a river to rice fields to the bones of people eating the rice. It's one of the reasons we monitor heavy metals in the environment.

# Indium, element 49
@src Standard chemistry text

Indium is a soft metal that, when combined with tin and oxygen, forms indium tin oxide, a coating that is both transparent and electrically conductive. That's the layer that senses your touch on the screen of almost every phone, tablet and flat-panel TV.

The name comes from the bright indigo line in its spectrum, which is how it was identified in 1863 by the German chemists Ferdinand Reich and Theodor Richter. Reich was colorblind, so he asked his assistant to look at the spectrum for him. It's a rare element and is mostly obtained as a byproduct of zinc mining. A small amount is spread across a very large number of devices, which has made people look for ways to recycle it.

# Tin, element 50
@src Standard chemistry text

Tin was one of the keys to early civilization. About 3300 BC people discovered that mixing tin with copper makes bronze, which is much harder than copper alone, and the Bronze Age began. Because tin was scarce, traders carried it great distances, and some of the earliest long-distance trade routes grew up around it.

When you bend a bar of pure tin, it makes a faint crackling noise called "tin cry," caused by the crystals inside it rubbing against each other. Tin is also what coats the inside of a "tin can," which is really made of steel. The thin layer keeps food from touching the steel. Its symbol, Sn, comes from the Latin word for it, stannum.

# Antimony, element 51
@src Standard chemistry text

Antimony has been known since ancient times. In Egypt and elsewhere, a black powder from the mineral stibnite was ground up and used as kohl, an eye cosmetic. That makeup was used for beauty, and also for protection from the sun's glare.

The chemical symbol Sb comes from "stibium," the Latin name for the mineral. Today, antimony is used to make flame retardants and to harden lead in batteries. It was also once used to make type metal for printing, because it expands slightly as it cools, which makes sharp letters. A few medicines of the past contained it, though many were toxic. It's a reminder that cosmetics and chemistry have long gone together.

# Tellurium, element 52
@src USGS

Tellurium is one of the rarest stable elements in Earth's crust, rarer than platinum. It's used in solar panels made with cadmium telluride, the second most common type of solar panel after silicon.

It was first isolated in 1782 by a Hungarian-Austrian mineralogist, Franz-Joseph Müller von Reichenstein, and named by Martin Klaproth for the Latin word for Earth, tellus. People who've been exposed to a small amount sometimes develop a strong garlic smell on their breath that can last for weeks. It's an element that most people never hear of, but it turns up in thin-film solar cells that cover large fields. Because so little of it is mined, and most comes as a byproduct of refining copper, its supply is a worry for the solar industry.

# Iodine, element 53
@src NIH; WHO

Your thyroid gland needs iodine to make the hormones that regulate your metabolism. Without enough, it swells into a goiter. For much of history, whole regions far from the sea, where the soil is poor in iodine, had a high rate of goiter.

In 1924 Michigan began adding iodine to table salt, and the practice spread across the US, nearly eliminating goiter. Iodine deficiency is still a problem in parts of the world, and is a leading preventable cause of intellectual disability. It's one of the cheapest public health measures ever devised. A tiny amount of one element, added to the salt on the table, has protected generations from a harmful condition.

# Xenon, element 54
@src Ramsay & Travers, 1898

Xenon is a heavy noble gas, and its name comes from the Greek for "stranger." It was discovered in 1898 by William Ramsay and Morris Travers, in the same series of experiments that found neon and krypton.

It produces a bright, bluish-white light in car headlights and flash lamps. It's also used in ion engines for spacecraft, including NASA's Dawn mission to the asteroid belt, because its heavy atoms give more thrust. The engines give a very gentle push, but they can run for years. It's been used as an anesthetic as well, though it's too expensive for general use. A "stranger" that was once thought to be inert turned out to have many uses.

# Cesium, element 55
@src BIPM

The length of a second is defined by cesium. Since 1967, one second has been 9,192,631,770 cycles of the microwave radiation given off by a cesium-133 atom. The ticking of a cesium atom has replaced the rotation of the Earth as our measure of time.

Atomic clocks based on it are accurate to within about a second over tens of millions of years. It's the reason your phone's time is right, and satellite navigation systems depend on that precision. The metal itself is one of the most reactive there is, and it's liquid just above room temperature, at about 28 degrees Celsius. The name comes from the Latin for "sky blue," after the bright blue lines in its spectrum.

# Barium, element 56
@src Standard chemistry text

Barium compounds block X-rays, so patients are given a "barium meal," a chalky drink, to make the digestive tract show up clearly on an X-ray image. The drink outlines the stomach and intestines in white, so a doctor can see any problem.

Barium salts also burn a bright green in fireworks. Although soluble barium compounds are poisonous, the one used for X-rays, barium sulfate, doesn't dissolve and passes through safely. It's a good example of how the exact form of a chemical matters. One barium compound is a medical aid, and another can be a poison, and that's why chemists pay such close attention to what a substance is, and not just to what element it contains.

# Lanthanum and cerium
@src USGS

Lanthanum is the first of the lanthanides, a row of 15 similar elements. Glass that contains lanthanum bends light strongly, which lets lens makers produce thinner and sharper camera and telescope lenses. It's also used in some batteries for hybrid cars. The name comes from the Greek for "to lie hidden," because it was hidden within other minerals when it was found in 1839.

Cerium is the most abundant of the rare earths, about as common in the Earth's crust as copper. When cerium is mixed with iron, it makes ferrocerium, the material in lighter flints: scrape it, and tiny hot particles fly off as sparks. It's also in catalytic converters and in some self-cleaning oven walls, where it helps break down grease. Both metals were found within minerals that were mined for other things, which is how the rare earths were hidden for so long.

# Praseodymium and neodymium
@src Auer von Welsbach, 1885; Sagawa, 1982

For about forty years, scientists believed that "didymium," whose name means "twin," was a single element. In 1885 the Austrian chemist Carl Auer von Welsbach separated it into two. He called them praseodymium, "green twin," and neodymium, "new twin." Praseodymium gives glass and ceramics a yellow-green color, and is used in some strong magnets and welding goggles.

The neodymium-iron-boron magnet, developed in 1982, is the strongest type of permanent magnet commercially available. A small one can lift hundreds of times its own weight. You'll find them in headphones, computer hard drives, electric car motors and wind turbine generators. It's a nice irony that something once thought to be a single element, a twin, turned out to be a pair, and one of them now sits inside a lot of modern machinery.

# Promethium, element 61
@src Marinsky et al., 1945

Promethium has no stable isotopes, so it's extremely rare in nature, with only a tiny amount at any moment in Earth's crust. For decades, chemists searched for it among the rare earths and found nothing, because there was nothing there to find.

It was first identified in 1945 by scientists at Oak Ridge in Tennessee, from the fission products of uranium. It's named for Prometheus, the Titan who stole fire from the gods for humans, which fits for an element obtained from nuclear fission. It was once used to make luminous paint for watch dials, as a safer substitute for radium. It's also been used in small batteries for spacecraft and in some thickness gauges. Its story is a reminder that nature doesn't always have an element where the table says there should be one.
