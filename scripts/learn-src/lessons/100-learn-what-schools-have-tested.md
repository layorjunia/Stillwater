# A preschool, followed for forty years
@conf mixed
@src Schweinhart et al., High/Scope Perry Preschool Study Through Age 40, 2005; Heckman et al., 2010

In the 1960s, in Ypsilanti, Michigan, a team gave 123 low-income Black children, who were at high risk of failing at school, a chance to join the Perry Preschool program. About half were assigned to it, and the others were not. The children in the program went to a preschool for up to two years, and a teacher visited their homes each week. The researchers then followed both groups for decades, up to the age of forty.

The people who had been in the program had fewer arrests, and did better at employment and earnings. The economist James Heckman and his colleagues estimated that the program paid back to society a yearly rate of return of seven to ten percent. They also stressed the weaknesses. The way the children were assigned was compromised, the sample was small, and they had to guess about the years after forty. It is among the best long-term evidence that early education can pay off.

# The infants who went to a different kind of day care
@src Campbell et al., Developmental Psychology, 2012; Science, 2014

In the 1970s, in North Carolina, researchers recruited 111 infants from poor families, nearly all of them Black. By lottery, half were offered full-day, high-quality care and education from the first months of life until age five. The program, called the Abecedarian Project, included games and talking that were designed to build language. The other children were not in the program, though they received some support. Both groups were followed for decades.

At age thirty, the people who had been in the program were more likely to have a college degree, to have a job, and to have delayed having children. In 2014 Frances Campbell and James Heckman and their colleagues reported on their health in their mid-thirties. The group that had been in the program had better physical health. The men had lower blood pressure, and the check was done by doctors who did not know who had been in which group. The sample was small, but the results are among the longest that we have.

# A school that teaches in its own way
@conf mixed
@src Lillard & Else-Quest, Science, 2006

In Montessori schools, children choose their own activities from a prepared set of materials, they work in mixed-age classes, and the teacher guides more than lectures. Do they do better? That is hard to test, because families who choose such schools differ from others. In 2006 Angeline Lillard and Nicole Else-Quest took advantage of a lottery. A public Montessori school in Milwaukee had more applicants than places, and so children were admitted by chance.

They tested 30 five-year-olds and 29 twelve-year-olds, some who had won places and some who had not. The Montessori children did better on a range of measures, including reading, math, and social skills such as how fairly they thought about disputes. The lottery design makes it close to a real experiment. But the sample was small, and Montessori schools vary widely in how closely they follow the method, so the results may not hold everywhere.

# What happened when phones were banned
@src Beland & Murphy, Ill Communication, 2016

Do mobile phones distract students? In the early 2000s, many schools in England banned them, at different times, and the researchers Louis-Philippe Beland and Richard Murphy used that as a natural experiment. They looked at the test scores of students in 91 schools in four English cities, Birmingham, London, Leicester and Manchester, from 2001 to 2013, before and after each school's ban.

Test scores rose after the bans. The gains were not spread evenly. They were concentrated among the students who had been doing worst, who gained the most, and the highest scoring students were not much affected. The size of the average effect in the published version was small, about 0.07 of a standard deviation. The authors suggested that phones are a distraction, and that a ban helps students who find it hardest to concentrate. It may not apply to a ban in another setting.

# The online course that did not take over
@src Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente, Science, 2019

In 2012 free online courses, called MOOCs, were expected to change higher education. Millions of people signed up. In 2019 Justin Reich and José Ruipérez-Valiente looked at the data from the first six years of courses from Harvard and MIT on the platform edX, up to May 2018. They asked how many people finished, who took the courses, and what happened to the business.

The completion rate had barely changed in six years, and it was low. More than 80 percent of the learners came from countries with high or very high development, and many already had degrees. The number who came back for a second year fell from 38 percent in the first group to 7 percent in a recent one. Providers have moved toward selling online master's degrees to universities. The authors' view is that free online courses did not do what was promised, though the platform's founder said that the paper underplayed its growth.

# Ten principles of teaching
@src Rosenshine, American Educator, 2012

In 2012 the educational psychologist Barak Rosenshine pulled together ten principles of good instruction, from three kinds of evidence. They came from studies in cognitive science, from studies of the best teachers in classrooms, and from studies of how to help students to learn complex tasks. He wrote them down so that teachers could use them.

The best teachers began with a short review of earlier learning, and presented new material in small steps, with practice after each. They asked many questions and checked the answers of all the students. They gave models, such as worked examples, and guided practice with support that was slowly withdrawn. They aimed for a high success rate in practice, about 80 percent, and reviewed material weekly and monthly. None of it is exotic. It describes careful teaching that makes sure that students have learned something before they go on.
