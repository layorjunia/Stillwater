# Take nobody's word for it
@src Royal Society records; Hunter, Establishing the New Science, 1989

In November 1660, after a lecture at Gresham College in London by the young architect and astronomer Christopher Wren, a group of men met to form a society for the promotion of experimental knowledge. It became the Royal Society, chartered by King Charles II in 1662. Its early members included Robert Boyle, Robert Hooke and, later, Isaac Newton.

The society's motto, adopted early on, is Nullius in verba, which means take nobody's word for it. It was a declaration that claims should be settled by evidence and experiment, and not by the authority of an ancient writer or a famous person. Members were expected to show their results before witnesses. In 1665 the society began to publish Philosophical Transactions, which is the oldest scientific journal still in print. The spirit of the motto is the center of science.

# Einstein and the anonymous referee
@src Kennefick, Physics Today, 2005

Peer review, in which unnamed experts evaluate a paper before it is published, was not a routine practice until the second half of the twentieth century. In 1936 Albert Einstein, who was then living in Princeton, sent a paper to the American journal Physical Review. He and a colleague, Nathan Rosen, argued that gravitational waves could not exist.

The editor sent it to a reviewer, who found a mistake, and wrote a long critical report. Einstein was furious. He wrote to the editor that he had not authorized him to show the paper to anyone before it was printed, and that he saw no reason to address the comments of an anonymous expert. He withdrew the paper, and is said never to have sent anything to that journal again. Later, with the help of a colleague, he found that the reviewer was right. Gravitational waves were detected in 2015.

# The Encyclopédie
@src Diderot & d'Alembert, Encyclopédie, 1751 to 1772; Blom, Enlightening the World, 2004

In 1747 the French writer Denis Diderot was asked to translate an English encyclopedia. He turned it into something much larger. With the mathematician Jean le Rond d'Alembert, he set out to gather all human knowledge in one work, and to make it accessible. The Encyclopédie took twenty-five years. It filled twenty-eight volumes, with about seventy-two thousand articles, and thousands of illustrations of trades and machines.

Hundreds of people wrote for it, including Voltaire, Rousseau and Montesquieu. Its articles promoted reason, science and tolerance, and criticized superstition and absolute power. The Church and the government opposed it, and it was banned more than once. Diderot worked on it in secret at times. Many historians see it as the most important project of the Enlightenment. It showed that knowledge could be organized and spread widely.

# Flying a kite in a storm
@src Franklin, Pennsylvania Gazette, October 19, 1752; Schiffer, Draw the Lightning Down, 2003

Benjamin Franklin suspected that lightning was electrical. In the spring of 1752, he proposed an experiment using a tall metal rod on a building, or a sentry box. A French team tried it first, in May, near Paris, and saw sparks from the rod. In June 1752, according to his own account, Franklin flew a kite in a thunderstorm in Philadelphia, with a key at the end of the string, and was able to draw sparks from the key.

The experiment was dangerous. In 1753 a Russian physicist, Georg Richmann, was killed in St. Petersburg when he tried a similar experiment, and was hit by a ball of lightning. Franklin used what he had learned to invent the lightning rod, which protects buildings by providing a safe path for the current. It was one of the first practical results of research into electricity.

# The first balloon flight
@src Gillispie, The Montgolfier Brothers and the Invention of Aviation, 1983

In 1783 two paper manufacturers in the French town of Annonay, Joseph-Michel and Jacques-Étienne Montgolfier, noticed that smoke rose. They made a large balloon of paper and cloth, and filled it with hot air from a fire. It rose in front of a crowd. In September of that year, they sent up a balloon from the palace of Versailles, in front of the king, with a sheep, a duck and a rooster in a basket. The animals landed safely.

On November 21, 1783, in Paris, two men, Jean-François Pilâtre de Rozier and the Marquis d'Arlandes, made the first free flight by humans. They rose in a Montgolfier balloon, and flew for about twenty-five minutes, crossing part of the city, and landed about five miles away. Crowds watched. Ten days later, a hydrogen balloon, built by the physicist Jacques Charles, carried people too. The era of flight had begun.

# The Luddites
@src Thompson, The Making of the English Working Class, 1963; Binfield, The Luddites, 2004

In the years 1811 to 1816, bands of workers in the textile towns of the English Midlands and North broke into factories and smashed machines. They claimed to be led by a mythical figure, General Ned Ludd, and they called themselves Luddites. They were mainly skilled weavers and knitters, who were losing their income because of new machines that allowed unskilled workers, women and children, to do the job at a lower wage.

The government responded with force. It sent in thousands of troops, and made frame-breaking a crime that was punishable by death. Several men were hanged, and others were transported. Today, the word Luddite is often used for someone who dislikes technology. But the Luddites were not against machines as such. They were fighting for decent wages and conditions, at a time of hardship.

# On the Beagle
@src Darwin, Journal of Researches, 1839; Browne, Charles Darwin: Voyaging, 1995

In December 1831 the British Navy ship HMS Beagle left Plymouth on a survey voyage around the world. On board as a companion to the captain, Robert FitzRoy, was a twenty-two-year-old man who had abandoned a medical degree, and was training for the clergy, Charles Darwin. The voyage was meant to last two years. It took nearly five.

Darwin collected specimens, kept notes, and read Charles Lyell's new book on geology, which said that the Earth was very old. He saw fossils of giant extinct animals in South America, and the different birds on the Galápagos Islands. He came home in 1836 with ideas that took him more than twenty years to develop. When On the Origin of Species was published in November 1859, the whole first printing of twelve hundred and fifty copies sold on the first day.

# The Crystal Palace
@src Auerbach, The Great Exhibition of 1851, 1999

In 1851 Britain held the Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations, in Hyde Park, London. It was Prince Albert's idea, and it was meant to display the achievements of the Industrial Revolution, and to show British superiority. The building was as remarkable as the exhibits. Designed by Joseph Paxton, a gardener, it was a huge hall of glass and cast iron, nearly two thousand feet long, called the Crystal Palace.

It was built in about nine months, from prefabricated parts. More than six million people visited it in five months. They saw steam engines, textile machines, a huge diamond, the first public toilets, and inventions from many countries. The profits were used to found museums in South Kensington, such as the Victoria and Albert Museum. The building was moved to south London, where it burned down in 1936.

# The Great Stink
@src Halliday, The Great Stink of London, 1999

In the nineteenth century, London was growing rapidly, and its sewage flowed into the River Thames, the source of much of the city's drinking water. Cholera outbreaks killed thousands. At the time, most doctors believed that disease was caused by bad air, miasma, and not by germs in water. In the hot summer of 1858 the smell from the river became unbearable, and Parliament, which stands on the bank, had to hang curtains soaked in chloride of lime over its windows.

The Great Stink, as it was called, gave politicians a reason to act. Within weeks, they passed a bill to build a new sewer system, designed by the engineer Joseph Bazalgette. It took about sixteen years, and included about eighty miles of large intercepting sewers, which carried waste to the east, away from the center. The system is still in use. It's often credited with ending cholera epidemics in London.

# The miracle year
@src Einstein, Annalen der Physik, 1905; Stachel, Einstein's Miraculous Year, 1998

In 1905 Albert Einstein was twenty-six years old, and working as a clerk in the patent office in Bern, Switzerland. He had failed to get a university post. In that one year, in his spare time, he published four papers in the journal Annalen der Physik, each of which changed physics. He called them revolutionary in a letter to a friend.

The first explained the photoelectric effect, by suggesting that light comes in packets, now called photons, which helped to found quantum theory. It earned him the Nobel Prize. The second explained Brownian motion, the jiggling of particles in a fluid, as the effect of molecules, and so gave strong evidence for atoms. The third was the special theory of relativity. The fourth, a short follow-up, gave the equation E equals mc squared. The year is called his annus mirabilis.

# The Solvay conference of 1927
@src Bacciagaluppi & Valentini, Quantum Theory at the Crossroads, 2009

In October 1927 the fifth Solvay Conference was held in Brussels. It had been organized by the Belgian industrialist Ernest Solvay, and its subject was electrons and photons. Twenty-nine of the world's top physicists attended, and seventeen of them won, or were later to win, a Nobel Prize. They included Einstein, Bohr, Heisenberg, Schrödinger, Planck, Curie and Dirac.

The conference is known for the debates between Niels Bohr and Albert Einstein about the meaning of quantum mechanics. Einstein disliked the idea that nature is governed by chance, saying that God does not play dice. Bohr is said to have replied that Einstein should stop telling God what to do. Each morning, Einstein would propose a thought experiment to show a flaw, and by evening, Bohr would have found the answer. The photograph of the group is one of the most famous in science.
