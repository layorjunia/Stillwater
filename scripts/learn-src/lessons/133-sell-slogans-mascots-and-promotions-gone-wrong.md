# Number two, and trying harder
@src Avis Rent a Car; Doyle Dane Bernbach campaign, 1962

Avis was founded in 1946 by Warren Avis, with three cars at Willow Run Airport near Ypsilanti, Michigan. It was the first car rental company to have an office at an airport. By 1953 it was the second largest in the United States, and by its tenth anniversary in 1956 it had opened offices in Europe, Canada and Mexico.

Its best-known step was its advertising. For years Avis ran second to Hertz, and its advertising agency, Doyle Dane Bernbach, took an unusual approach. Instead of hiding that Avis was the smaller company, the ads said so. They carried the line that Avis was only number two, so it tried harder. The motto, We Try Harder, has been one of the most remembered in advertising.

The campaign turned a weakness into a reason to choose the company. A customer might expect that a firm that was not the leader would work harder to please. Avis's share of the market and its profits are said to have risen after it began. The lesson that is often drawn is that honesty about a limit can be more persuasive than a claim to be the best, if it is tied to a promise.

# The stove salesman who fathered advertising
@series people
@src David Ogilvy, 1911 to 1999

David Ogilvy was born in England in 1911. He won scholarships, but left Oxford after two years, having failed his exams. In 1931 he worked as a kitchen hand at the Hotel Majestic in Paris, and then went back to Scotland and sold AGA cooking stoves door to door. He was so good at it that his employer asked him to write a manual for the other salesmen, and it was admired for years.

He trained in research at the Gallup organization, and he founded the agency Ogilvy and Mather. He made campaigns for Rolls-Royce, for Dove soap, and for Hathaway shirts. He credited his success to careful research into how consumers behave, and not to inspiration alone. He is often called the Father of Advertising.

His beliefs were practical. He thought that an ad should give facts, and that people who buy a product are intelligent. He wrote that the consumer is not a moron, she is your wife. He argued for long headlines and clear writing, and for testing. His early experience of selling to people in their homes, face to face, taught him that an ad is a salesman in print.

# The cartoon camel and the six-year-olds
@series sell
@src Fischer et al., JAMA, 1991; Federal Trade Commission

Joe Camel was a cartoon mascot used by the R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Company for its Camel cigarettes. The character was created in 1974 for a French advertising campaign, and was redesigned for American audiences in 1988. He was a humanoid with a camel's head, often shown in a tuxedo or sunglasses, in a heroic pose, surrounded by women or in a bar.

In 1991 the Journal of the American Medical Association published a study that suggested that the campaign was reaching children. It found that Joe Camel was recognized as well as the logo of the Disney Channel by six-year-olds, over 90 percent of whom knew each. High school students were more familiar with him than adults were, and Camel's share of the market among young smokers had risen sharply.

The research led to a lawsuit in California, and to a formal complaint by the Federal Trade Commission, for unfair practices. The company denied that it was aiming at children. In 1997, after growing litigation and pressure from federal agencies, it ended the campaign. The case helped to bring about limits on tobacco advertising. It showed that a mascot can be remembered by very young people long before they can legally buy the product.

# The campaign that began with four percent
@series sell
@src Unilever, Dove Campaign for Real Beauty, 2004; Ogilvy

In 2004 Dove, a brand of Unilever, launched what it called the Campaign for Real Beauty. It came out of a three-year study of how women see themselves, done in partnership with three universities. The research found that only 4 percent of women thought of themselves as beautiful. Dove and its agency, Ogilvy, decided to build a campaign around that finding.

The first stage was a series of billboards in Germany and Britain. They showed photographs of ordinary women, and not professional models, taken by the portrait photographer Rankin. The billboards invited passersby to vote on questions such as whether a woman was fat or fab, or wrinkled or wonderful, and the results of the votes were shown on the board. A corporate study, the Dove Report, was published with it. A photography show, Beyond Compare, with the work of 67 women photographers, was also part of the start.

The campaign was widely discussed and was followed by videos, one of them called Evolution, which showed how a photograph of a woman is altered. It made the brand's name for self-confidence. It was also criticized, since the same parent company sold products that played on worries about looks. It shows how a message about values can be a way of selling.

# The man your man could smell like
@series sell
@src Wieden+Kennedy, Old Spice, 2010

By the early 2000s, Old Spice was the leading brand of men's deodorant and body wash in the United States, with about a fifth of the market. But the brand was regarded as old-fashioned. The company, Procter and Gamble, hired the agency Wieden and Kennedy to bring it up to date, and in 2010 it launched an advertising campaign called Smell Like a Man, Man, which is also known as The Man Your Man Could Smell Like.

An unusual feature was its target. The ads were aimed at women. The company had found that women often make the decisions on which hygiene products to buy, even for the men in the household. The commercial starred the actor Isaiah Mustafa, who spoke straight to the camera in a confident, fast monologue.

During the monologue, he moved through a series of places, costumes and impossible situations, all in a single unbroken shot, with a surprise at the end. The first ad was for a product called Red Zone After Hours body wash, and the campaign was soon extended to others, because of its success. A well-known product with a dull image was given new life by humor, speed and a clever choice of who to talk to.

# The slogan that was thought lazy
@series sell
@src Goodby Silverstein & Partners, 1993; California Milk Processor Board

In 1993 an advertising agency, Goodby Silverstein and Partners, made a campaign for the California Milk Processor Board, to bring up the sales of milk. The slogan was Got Milk? It was launched with a television commercial, called Aaron Burr, directed by Michael Bay. In 1995 a national campaign began to put the phrase on its milk mustache ads, which showed celebrities with a white line on the lip.

The slogan almost did not become a campaign. In an interview in a documentary, Art and Copy, in 2009, the founders of the agency, Jeff Goodby and Rich Silverstein, said that they thought the phrase was lazy, and not even grammatically correct. They worried about it. It worked because it was short, and it described the feeling of being out of milk, at the very moment when you want it.

The national campaign ended in 2014, and a new one, Milk Life, replaced it. The slogan stayed in California, and its trademark is licensed for products. The campaign is credited with an increase in milk sales in California, but there is no strong evidence of its effect across the whole country. A slogan can be remembered for years even if its sales results are hard to prove.

# The brand name that means absolutely pure
@series sell
@src Vin & Sprit, 1977; Lars Olsson Smith; Pernod Ricard

Absolut Vodka is a Swedish brand that was developed and launched by the state-owned company Vin and Sprit in 1977. It is now the second largest vodka brand in the world. It is made from winter wheat, and is produced only in the town of Åhus, in the south of Sweden.

The name has an older history. In the 1860s, a distiller named Lars Olsson Smith set up a business in Stockholm, and used a method of repeated distillation to make a spirit called brännvin. At first it was marketed as tenfold purified brännvin, and later as Absolut rent brännvin, which means absolutely pure. When Vin and Sprit began to develop a product for export in 1977, advertising experts Gunnar Broman and Hans Brindfors drew on that name.

The company's later success owed much to its advertising. It ran a long series of ads that showed the shape of the bottle, with a play on words in the caption, and the artist Andy Warhol made a painting of the bottle for it in 1985. Since 2008 the brand has belonged to the French group Pernod Ricard. A name with a long history helped to give a new bottle a sense of age and purity.

# The store where everything cost five cents
@series history
@src F. W. Woolworth Company, 1879; Woolworth Building, 1913

Frank Winfield Woolworth opened his first store on February 22, 1879, in Utica, New York. It was called Woolworth's Great Five Cent Store. It did not succeed, and it closed after three months. He tried again, and on June 21, 1879, opened a store in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, which was successful. He brought in his brother Charles to help.

The idea was to sell a wide range of goods at fixed low prices, in a shop where customers could handle the goods themselves. The five-and-dime store spread across the country and abroad. The company entered Britain in 1909, Ireland in 1914, Germany in 1927 and Mexico in 1956. It became one of the biggest retailers of the twentieth century.

Woolworth built a headquarters in New York, the Woolworth Building, designed by Cass Gilbert. It is 792 feet tall, and from 1913 to 1930 it was the tallest building in the world. In 1974 the company created the Foot Locker chain, from the Kinney shoe business that it had bought. The shops faced decline from the 1980s, and the last American Woolworth department stores closed in 1997. A store that failed in three months led to a skyscraper.

# The book seller who turned to perfume
@series sell
@src Avon Products; David H. McConnell, 1886

David McConnell began as a door-to-door book salesman in New York. He found that when he offered a little bottle of perfume as a gift to the housewives he called on, they welcomed him in. They seemed more interested in the perfume than the books. In September 1886 he decided to sell perfume instead, and started a business in a small office on Chambers Street in Manhattan.

In 1892 he renamed it the California Perfume Company. His partner, who lived in California, had suggested the name because of the abundance of flowers there. The company built a laboratory in Suffern, New York, in 1897. It grew by recruiting women to sell the products to friends and neighbors, which gave many women a chance to earn their own money at a time when few had one.

Later the company took the name Avon, and it became a multinational firm that sells cosmetics, skin care and personal care products. It is a multi-level marketing company, in which independent sellers earn money from their own sales. The Avon lady with her sample case became a symbol of direct selling. The idea began with a salesman who noticed that a small gift opened doors.

# Selling through your friends
@series sell
@conf mixed
@src Amway; multi-level marketing; U.S. Federal Trade Commission

Amway was founded in 1959 by Jay Van Andel and Richard DeVos, in Michigan. Its name is short for American Way. It sells health, beauty and home care products, and it is the largest multi-level marketing company in the world by sales, with reported sales of 8.9 billion dollars in 2019. It operates in more than a hundred countries and territories.

Multi-level marketing is a system in which independent distributors earn money in two ways. They make a profit from selling products, and they receive commissions on the sales of the people whom they recruit into the business, and of those people's recruits. The structure resembles a pyramid, in which a few at the top do very well, and many at the bottom earn little.

For that reason such companies have often been investigated. Amway has been looked at in many countries, and by the United States Federal Trade Commission, over claims that it was a pyramid scheme. The legal line between a legitimate direct-selling business and a pyramid scheme depends on whether income comes mainly from sales to real customers, or from recruiting. It is a distinction that matters to anyone who is offered a chance to join.

# The boycott over baby formula
@series history
@src Nestlé boycott, launched July 4, 1977; International Baby Food Action Network; World Health Organization

On July 4, 1977, a boycott of the Swiss food company Nestlé was launched in the United States. It spread to Europe in the early 1980s. It was a protest against the way in which Nestlé and other companies marketed infant formula as a substitute for breast milk, especially in poor countries.

Critics said that the promotion was aggressive, and that it discouraged mothers from breast-feeding. Where water was not clean, and families were poor, mixing formula with unsafe water or diluting it to make it last could harm babies. The campaign was supported by groups such as the International Baby Food Action Network and Save the Children. The World Health Organization recommends that infants be fed only on breast milk for the first six months of life.

The boycott has been called off and renewed over the years, depending on the practices of the companies. It was unusual in tying human rights and humanitarian concern to the ordinary act of shopping. One analysis said that consumers acted as global citizens, using the market as a place of protest. It led to an international code on the marketing of breast milk substitutes, and showed that the choice of what we buy can be an act of politics.

# The peanut that a schoolboy drew
@series history
@src Planters Peanut Company; Antonio Gentile, 1916

Mr. Peanut, the mascot of Planters, is a peanut in its shell, dressed in a top hat, a monocle, white gloves, spats and a cane. He is said to be British, and to have a full name, Bartholomew Richard Fitzgerald-Smythe. The company began in 1906 in Wilkes-Barre, Pennsylvania, founded by Amedeo Obici.

In 1916 the company ran a contest for a design, and a schoolboy, Antonio Gentile, sent in drawings of a peanut with arms and legs. His was chosen. A commercial artist, Andrew S. Wallach, then added the monocle and the top hat, which gave the figure its air of a gentleman. The Gentile family was given five dollars for the win.

Obici befriended the family, and paid for Antonio and four of his brothers and sisters to go to college. He also paid for Antonio's medical school, and Antonio became a doctor in Newport News, Virginia, where he died of a heart attack in 1939. Another artist, Frank Krize, is also said to have added the finishing touches, and the question is disputed. A drawing by a boy became one of the best-known characters in advertising.

# The man made of tires
@series history
@src Michelin; O'Galop, 1898; Lyon Exposition, 1894

Bibendum, better known as the Michelin Man, is the mascot of the French tire company Michelin. He is a figure made of stacked white tires, and he is one of the oldest trademarks that is still in use. His slogan, Nunc est bibendum, which means now is the time to drink, is taken from an ode by the Roman poet Horace.

The idea began in 1894 at the Universal and Colonial Exposition in Lyon, where the brothers Édouard and André Michelin noticed a pile of tires that looked to Édouard like a man without arms. Four years later, in 1898, André met a cartoonist, Marius Rossillon, known as O'Galop, who showed him an image that a brewery had rejected. It was of a big man raising a glass, with the Latin motto. The Michelins asked him to turn it into a figure of tires.

Bibendum was shown as a master of industry, and as a patriotic exponent of the French spirit. In the 1920s he urged Frenchmen to take up the American factory system, but to do better. As cars came within reach of the middle classes, the company's advertising and its restaurant and hotel guides followed them. A figure that began as a joke about a pile of tires has lasted well over a century.

# The offer that was too good for the company
@series sell
@src Hoover free flights promotion, 1992; BBC documentary, 2004

In late 1992, in the middle of a recession, the British division of the Hoover company ran a promotion to boost its falling sales. It offered two free round-trip plane tickets to the United States, worth about 600 pounds, to any customer who bought at least 100 pounds of Hoover products. The company hoped that most people would not spend that much, and that many would be put off by a difficult application process, or would fail to meet the exact terms.

Instead the response was much bigger than expected. Many customers bought the minimum, and filled in the forms. The demand for tickets was far more than the company could afford, and it was disastrous for the 84-year-old firm. Hoover canceled the promotion after people had already bought the goods and applied for millions of pounds' worth of tickets.

Customers who did not get their tickets protested and took legal action. The campaign cost the company a great deal of money, and, after a BBC documentary in 2004, it lost its royal warrant. The European business was later sold to a rival, Candy. The story is a standard case in marketing of an offer designed to be mostly unused, which went wrong because people took it up.
