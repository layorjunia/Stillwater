# Ethos, pathos, logos
@src Aristotle, Rhetoric, about 330 BC

About 2,300 years ago, Aristotle wrote a book on how speakers persuade people. He said that there are three means of persuasion. Ethos is the character of the speaker, whether they seem trustworthy, knowledgeable and well-meaning. Pathos is the emotion that the speech arouses in the audience. Logos is the argument itself, with its reasons and evidence.

A good speech uses all three. A speaker with sound arguments, but who seems untrustworthy, may not convince. A speaker with ethos and emotion but no real reasons may sway people, and mislead them. Modern research on persuasion has confirmed that credibility, feeling and argument quality each matter. The three terms are still used to analyze speeches, advertising and political messages. It's a short list that has lasted for twenty-three centuries.

# The man with pebbles in his mouth
@src Plutarch, Life of Demosthenes
@conf mixed

Demosthenes was an Athenian orator of the fourth century BC, and is often named as the greatest speaker of ancient Greece. According to the biographer Plutarch, writing about four hundred years later, he started out with a weak voice, a stammer and an awkward way of speaking. His first speeches in the assembly were mocked.

Plutarch says that he trained himself by practicing with pebbles in his mouth, and by reciting verses as he ran uphill, or spoke against the roar of the sea. He is also said to have shaved half of his head so that he would be ashamed to go out, and would stay at home to study. How much of this is true is unknown. But the story has stuck as an image of how practice can overcome a handicap.

# Pericles and the funeral oration
@src Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War, Book 2

In the winter of 431 BC, at the end of the first year of the war with Sparta, Athens held a public funeral for those who had died. Pericles, its leading statesman, gave the speech. The version that survives was written by the historian Thucydides, who said he had given the general sense of what was said.

In it, Pericles praises Athens, and not just the dead. He says that the constitution is called a democracy because power is in the hands of the many, and not of a few. Everyone is equal before the law, and advancement is by merit. He speaks of freedom in daily life, and of the courage of citizens. The speech has been read as one of the great statements of democratic ideals. It was given at a moment when the city was about to face the plague.

# The Gettysburg Address
@src Library of Congress; Wills, Lincoln at Gettysburg, 1992

On November 19, 1863, a cemetery was dedicated at Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, four months after the battle in which tens of thousands of soldiers were killed or wounded. The main speaker was Edward Everett, a famous orator, who spoke for about two hours. President Abraham Lincoln was asked to follow with a few remarks.

He spoke for about two minutes, in roughly 270 words. He began with four score and seven years ago, and said that the nation was conceived in liberty and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal. He closed with the hope that government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth. Everett wrote to Lincoln the next day that he wished he could have come as near to the central idea in two hours as Lincoln did in two minutes.

# We shall fight on the beaches
@src Churchill, House of Commons, June 4, 1940; Lukacs, Five Days in London, 1999

On June 4, 1940, Winston Churchill spoke in the House of Commons about the evacuation of Dunkirk. In a few weeks, more than three hundred thousand British and French soldiers had been taken off the beaches by an array of ships. He warned that wars are not won by evacuations. Then he said that Britain would go on to the end, that we shall fight on the beaches, on the landing grounds, in the fields and in the streets, and that we shall never surrender.

The speech was not broadcast live. It was given in the Commons, and the news that night told the public what he had said. A BBC announcer read out extracts. Churchill didn't make a recording of the speech until 1949. Many people remember hearing him say it on the radio, but what they remember was probably his later broadcasts. The words became famous because they were printed and repeated.

# I have a dream
@src King, Lincoln Memorial, August 28, 1963; Jones, Behind the Dream, 2011

On August 28, 1963, Martin Luther King Jr. spoke to a crowd of about a quarter of a million people at the March on Washington. He had prepared a text. As he came to the end, according to those near him, the gospel singer Mahalia Jackson called out from behind him, tell them about the dream, Martin. He put aside his script, and began to speak of his dream.

The lines about his four little children, and about a day when they would be judged by the content of their character, were not in the prepared text. He had used the dream theme in earlier speeches, and now he drew on it. The speech has been called one of the greatest of the twentieth century. It shows how an orator can respond to an audience, and how a speech grows from a text, with a cadence that comes from the preaching tradition.

# Freytag's pyramid
@src Freytag, Die Technik des Dramas, 1863

In 1863 the German novelist and critic Gustav Freytag wrote a book on the technique of drama. Studying ancient Greek tragedies and the plays of Shakespeare, he saw that most of them follow a five-part pattern. It's usually drawn as a pyramid, rising and then falling.

It starts with an introduction, which sets the scene. Then comes a rising action, in which tension builds. At the peak is the climax, a turning point. After it comes the falling action, as consequences unfold. The final part is the resolution, or catastrophe in a tragedy. The scheme doesn't fit every story, and it was based on a limited set of plays. But it's still widely taught, and it's a useful way of seeing how stories are shaped to hold attention.

# The six shapes of stories
@src Reagan, Mitchell, Kiley, Danforth & Dodds, EPJ Data Science, 2016

Kurt Vonnegut once gave a lecture in which he drew the shapes of stories on a blackboard, as lines that rise and fall with the fortunes of the hero. In 2016 Andrew Reagan and his colleagues tested the idea with data. They analyzed the words in about 1,300 works of fiction from Project Gutenberg, tracking how happy or sad the language was as the story went on.

They found six basic arcs. Rags to riches is a steady rise. Riches to rags is a fall. There are also a fall then a rise, a rise then a fall, a rise then a fall then a rise, and a fall then a rise then a fall. The arcs with more twists, such as Cinderella, which rises, falls and rises again, and Oedipus, which falls, rises and falls, were downloaded more than the simple rise or fall. The study is a nice example of using computers to find patterns in the way that stories are told.

# Orwell's six rules
@src Orwell, Politics and the English Language, 1946

In 1946 George Orwell wrote an essay arguing that bad writing can make clear thinking harder, and that it helps political lies to sound respectable. He ended with six rules. Never use a metaphor, simile or figure of speech that you are used to seeing in print. Never use a long word where a short one will do. If it's possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.

Never use the passive where you can use the active. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent. And break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous. His argument was that vague, stale language lets us avoid thinking. The essay is still widely read, and the rules are a good checklist for anyone who writes.

# Did Shakespeare invent 1,700 words?
@src Oxford English Dictionary; Crystal, Think on My Words, 2008
@conf mixed

William Shakespeare is often credited with having invented about seventeen hundred English words, such as lonely, bedroom, eyeball, swagger and assassination. The claim is based on the Oxford English Dictionary, which lists his plays as the earliest known use of many words. It's repeated in books and on websites.

The truth is more complicated. The dictionary's editors, over a century ago, relied on what they could read, and Shakespeare was widely studied, so he was cited more than other writers. As more early texts have been searched, many of his words have turned up in earlier works. He certainly used new words, and combined old ones in new ways, but he was probably not the sole inventor of most. He also made words popular, which is a form of influence.

# OMG is a hundred years old
@src Oxford English Dictionary; Fisher, letter to Churchill, 1917
@series words

OMG seems like a product of the age of text messages, but it's older. The Oxford English Dictionary's earliest known use is in a letter of 1917 from Admiral John Fisher, a retired British naval officer, to Winston Churchill. Fisher wrote that he had heard of a new order of chivalry that was being planned, and he added O.M.G., Oh! My God!, urging that its honors be showered on the Admiralty.

So the abbreviation is more than a century old. Telegrams and notes used many such shortenings, and slang often arises where people write quickly. The phrase became widespread in the 1990s with online chats, and entered the dictionary in 2011. It's a reminder that new communication tools often revive old habits, and that what seems new may have a longer history.

# Languages at risk
@src Ethnologue, 2023; UNESCO Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger
@series words

There are about seven thousand languages in the world. Many of them have very few speakers. Almost half of the world's languages are classed as endangered, and a small number of languages, such as Mandarin, Spanish and English, are spoken by hundreds of millions or even billions of people. Many languages have only a few hundred speakers, and some only a handful of elderly ones.

A language is endangered when children stop learning it. This often happens when a community moves to cities, or when a dominant language is the language of schools, work and media. Linguists fear that a large share of today's languages may fall silent within this century. When a language disappears, so does a store of knowledge about plants, animals, stories and ways of seeing the world. Some communities, such as the Māori in New Zealand, have worked to revive their languages.

# The revival of Hebrew
@src Fellman, The Revival of a Classical Tongue, 1973
@series words

For about eighteen hundred years, Hebrew was not a spoken language in daily life. It was used for prayer, and for writing, but people spoke the languages of the countries where they lived. In the late nineteenth century, Eliezer Ben-Yehuda, who emigrated to Jerusalem in 1881, set out to make it a living language again. He insisted on speaking only Hebrew at home.

His son Ittamar, born in 1882, is often called the first native speaker of modern Hebrew. Ben-Yehuda compiled a large dictionary, and helped to invent words for modern things, such as the words for newspaper and bicycle. Others took up the cause, with schools that taught in Hebrew. Today, Hebrew is the national language of Israel, and is spoken by millions. It's the only case of a dead language being revived as the everyday speech of a nation.

# Esperanto
@src Zamenhof, Unua Libro, 1887
@series words

In 1887 Ludwik Zamenhof, a Polish-Jewish eye doctor, published a book that introduced a constructed language. He had grown up in a city, Białystok, where Poles, Russians, Germans and Jews spoke different languages and often fought. He thought that a neutral, easy language could help people to understand one another. He published it under the pen name Doktoro Esperanto, meaning the one who hopes.

The language has simple rules, a small number of roots, and regular grammar. There are no exceptions to learn. It has attracted speakers around the world, though estimates of their numbers vary widely, from tens of thousands to a couple of million. It has not become a global language, since English has filled that role. Still, it is the most successful of the constructed languages, and has a literature and annual world congresses.

# Sign language is real language
@src Stokoe, Sign Language Structure, 1960
@series words

Until the middle of the twentieth century, many people believed that sign languages were just gestures, or a simple code for spoken language. In 1960 William Stokoe, a professor at Gallaudet University, a school for the deaf in Washington, published an analysis of American Sign Language. He showed that signs are made of smaller parts that are combined.

Each sign has a handshape, a location and a movement, which are like the sounds of a spoken language. Changing just one of these changes the meaning, as changing a sound does in a word. Later research showed that sign languages have complex grammar, and they are learned by children in the same stages as spoken languages are. The brain processes them in the same areas that process speech. It's now accepted that sign languages are full languages in their own right.

# The language that whistles
@src Classe, 1957; Meyer, Whistled Languages, 2015
@series words

On La Gomera, one of the Canary Islands, shepherds and farmers used to talk across deep ravines by whistling. The whistled language, called Silbo Gomero, is a way of speaking Spanish. Each word is whistled, using a limited set of tones and breaks that stand for the vowels and consonants. A whistle can be heard up to a few kilometers away, far more than a shout.

Brain scans of whistlers show that they use the language areas of the brain, and not just the areas for sound. Similar whistled languages are used in Turkey, Greece, Mexico and elsewhere in places with steep valleys or dense forests. The practice declined in the twentieth century with roads and phones. In the 1990s, Silbo was introduced into the island's schools, and UNESCO added it to its list of intangible cultural heritage in 2009.

# Basque
@src Trask, The History of Basque, 1997
@series words

Almost every language in Europe belongs to one of a few large families. Basque does not. It's spoken in the mountains along the border between Spain and France, by about three-quarters of a million people. It's called a language isolate, since no relationship with any other living language has been proved.

Many theories have been proposed, linking it to Caucasian languages, Berber, or others, but none has been accepted. The best guess is that it descends from a language spoken in the region before the arrival of Indo-European languages, perhaps for thousands of years. It was suppressed under Franco in Spain, and was nearly lost. It has since been revived, taught in schools and used on television. Its grammar and vocabulary are very different from its neighbors'.

# Computers in Icelandic
@src Icelandic Language Institute
@series words

Most languages take new words for new things from other languages. Icelandic often does the opposite. It creates new words from old roots. The word for computer, tölva, was invented in 1965 by a professor, Sigurður Nordal. It combines tala, meaning number, and völva, an old word for a seeress or prophetess. So it means a number prophetess.

The word for telephone is sími, an old word for thread. Television is sjónvarp, a vision-caster. A committee and many volunteers coin the terms. Icelanders can still read the medieval sagas, written eight hundred years ago, with little difficulty, because the language has changed little. This careful protection of the language is possible partly because of the small number of speakers, about three hundred and fifty thousand, and the isolation of the island.

# Hangul
@src Kim-Renaud, The Korean Alphabet, 1997
@series words

Before the fifteenth century, Koreans wrote with Chinese characters, which took years to learn, and were used mainly by the educated elite. King Sejong the Great, who ruled from 1418 to 1450, wanted a system that ordinary people could learn easily. A group of scholars, probably with the king's own contribution, created an alphabet. It was introduced in 1443, and published in 1446 as the Hunminjeongeum, the correct sounds for the instruction of the people.

Hangul is unusual in that the shapes of the consonants are based on the position of the mouth and tongue when making them. The letters are grouped into blocks that make up syllables. The alphabet has only twenty-four basic letters, and it's regarded as one of the most scientific writing systems. Literacy rates in South Korea are among the highest in the world. Hangul Day is a national day of celebration.

# Sequoyah and the Cherokee syllabary
@src Walker & Sarbaugh, Early American Indian Documents, 1993
@series words

Sequoyah was a Cherokee silversmith, who could not read English. He was impressed that white people could send messages on paper, and he wanted to do the same for his own language. Around 1809, he began to work on a writing system. At first he tried a symbol for each word, and found that there were too many. Then he decided to use a symbol for each syllable.

In 1821 he finished a set of eighty-five characters, each standing for a syllable of Cherokee. It was so well suited to the language that people could learn to read it in a few days. Within a few years, many Cherokees were literate, and in 1828 the tribe began to print a newspaper, the Cherokee Phoenix. Sequoyah is one of the few people in history who is known to have created a writing system, on his own, from nothing.
