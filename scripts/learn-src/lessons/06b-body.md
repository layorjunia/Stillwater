# Social jetlag
@src Roenneberg et al., 2006

Till Roenneberg, a German chronobiologist, found that many people sleep much later on weekends than on workdays. He called the mismatch "social jetlag," because it's like flying a few time zones west on Friday night and back east on Monday morning, without ever leaving home.

People with a bigger gap between their workday and weekend sleep tend to be more tired and in a worse mood, and to have more health problems. The body clock doesn't adjust instantly, so Monday morning can feel like the middle of the night. Keeping a regular wake-up time, even on days off, helps. If you have to catch up on sleep, it's better to go to bed earlier than to sleep in much later.

# The body clock is not exactly 24 hours
@src Czeisler et al., 1999

Your internal clock runs slightly longer than a day. In 1999 Charles Czeisler and his colleagues kept volunteers in dim light for weeks, with no clocks and no daylight cues, and measured an average internal day of 24 hours and 11 minutes.

Each morning, light resets the clock to the 24-hour day. That's why a bright morning can make you feel better, and why jet lag takes days to clear. It also explains why many people drift later when they don't have a reason to get up. Without the daily nudge from light, the clock slips a few minutes every day. A walk outside in the morning, even on a cloudy day, is one of the best ways to keep your clock on schedule.

# Teens' clocks shift
@src Carskadon, 2011

During puberty, the body clock moves later. Mary Carskadon at Brown University found that teenagers don't get sleepy until later in the evening, and naturally want to wake later in the morning, and it isn't just laziness. It's biology.

When schools have started later, studies have found that students got more sleep and had better attendance. A 7:30 start asks a teenager to wake at what feels like the middle of their night. If you live with a teenager, it may help to remember that the grumpy morning isn't defiance. Their clock is running behind the school's, and you can help by keeping the evening screens down and the bedroom dark, but you can't fully fight their biology.

# Rods and cones
@src Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science

The retina of each eye has two kinds of light sensors. Rods, about 120 million of them, work in dim light but can't see color, which is why the world looks gray at night. Cones, about 6 million, work in bright light and give you color and fine detail.

The cones are concentrated in the center of your vision, where you focus. That's why you can't read out of the corner of your eye, and why a faint star seems to vanish when you look straight at it, but reappears when you look slightly to one side. The two systems together let us see in both starlight and sunshine, a range of brightness that spans many millions to one.

# The blind spot
@src Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science

Each eye has a place where the optic nerve leaves the back of the eye, and there are no light sensors there. So each of us has a small hole in our vision. We don't notice it, because the brain fills it in using the surroundings and the other eye.

You can find yours. Close your left eye, look at a mark on the left side of a page with your right eye, and move the page slowly toward you until a second mark to its right disappears. The brain doesn't just ignore the gap. It paints over it with whatever pattern is nearby, so what you see there is a guess. It's a small, neat reminder that vision is something the brain builds and not a window.

# Saccades
@src Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science

Your eyes don't glide smoothly across a scene. They hop about three times a second, in quick jumps called saccades, and stop to take in information between the jumps. If you watch someone's eyes as they read, you can see the hops.

During each jump, your vision is briefly suppressed, so you don't see the blur. What feels like a smooth, continuous view is in fact built from many snapshots stitched together by your brain. You can test it by looking in a mirror and trying to watch your own eyes move from one to the other. You'll never see them move, because you're blind during the jump. Someone else would.

# Color vision
@src Young, 1802; Helmholtz

Thomas Young proposed in 1802 that the eye has three kinds of color receptors, and later experiments confirmed it. Each type of cone responds best to a different range of light, roughly red, green and blue, and your brain compares their signals to give you the full range of colors.

Yellow, for instance, isn't detected by a yellow sensor. It's what the brain makes when the red and green cones are both stimulated. About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have a form of color blindness, because the genes involved are on the X chromosome, and men have only one copy of it. Most people with it can see colors, but they confuse certain pairs, such as red and green.

# The cochlea
@src Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science

The cochlea, a coiled tube in the inner ear that looks like a snail shell, is where sound turns into nerve signals. It's lined with thousands of tiny hair cells, each tuned to a certain pitch, a little like the keys on a piano.

In humans, once those cells are destroyed, they don't grow back, which is why damage from loud noise is permanent. Young people can hear from about 20 to 20,000 hertz, and the upper range shrinks with age. By middle age, many people can no longer hear the highest tones, though everyday speech is mostly unaffected at first. The hair cells you have now are the ones you'll have for life, which is worth remembering the next time you are close to a loud speaker.

# Protecting your hearing
@src NIOSH, 1998

Sounds at 85 decibels, about as loud as heavy city traffic, can damage hearing if you hear them for hours. For every 3 decibels louder, the safe time is cut in half. So the louder the sound, the sooner it does harm.

A rock concert at 100 decibels is safe for only about 15 minutes without protection. Hearing loss from noise builds up silently and permanently, and the first sign is often a ringing in the ears after a loud event. A pair of cheap foam earplugs makes a real difference, and so does standing a little further from the speaker. The earlier you protect your hearing, the more of it you will have in old age.

# The five tastes
@src Ikeda, 1908; Chandrashekar et al., 2006

Your tongue detects five basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. The Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda identified umami in 1908, when he worked out what made seaweed broth so savory. It's the taste of glutamate, found in broth, cheese and ripe tomatoes.

You may have seen a "tongue map" with different zones for each taste. It's a myth, and every area of the tongue can detect all five. The idea came from a misreading of an old German paper. Most of what we call flavor is really smell, which is why food tastes bland when you have a cold. The tongue gives you a few simple signals, and the nose adds the thousands of subtle shades.

# Smell and memory
@src Herz & Engen, 1996
@conf mixed

The sense of smell has a more direct route to the brain's emotional and memory areas than the other senses do. Scent signals reach the amygdala and the hippocampus after only a couple of connections.

That may be why a smell, like a perfume or a kitchen, can suddenly bring back a vivid scene from childhood. Studies find that odor-evoked memories tend to be more emotional, though they aren't necessarily more accurate. The evidence for the idea that smell is special is mixed, so it's best held as a likely but unproven link. Still, many people can name a scent that takes them straight back to a place, and it's a pleasant thing to notice.

# The one-trillion-smells claim
@src Bushdid et al., 2014; Gerkin & Castro, 2015
@conf contested

In 2014 a study in Science claimed that humans can tell apart more than a trillion different odors. It was a striking number and it got a lot of press, since it seemed to overturn the old idea that people have a poor sense of smell.

Other scientists looked at the math and said that the conclusion didn't follow from the data, and that the true number could be far lower, perhaps only in the thousands. We still don't know how many smells people can really distinguish. It's a good lesson in reading headlines about big numbers. A surprising figure from a single study is an invitation to look further, not a fact to repeat.

# The gate control theory of pain
@src Melzack & Wall, 1965

In 1965 Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall proposed that the spinal cord has a kind of gate that can let pain signals through or block them. Signals from touch, for example, can close the gate, which is why rubbing a bumped elbow helps.

Signals from the brain can also open or close it, so your attention, mood and expectations change how much a given injury hurts. Soldiers wounded in battle sometimes report little pain, and a stubbed toe hurts more when you're already upset. The theory isn't exactly right in its details, but its main message holds. Pain isn't just a signal that goes straight to the brain. It's shaped on the way, and what's going on in your mind matters.

# Phantom limbs
@src Ramachandran & Rogers-Ramachandran, 1996
@conf mixed

After an amputation, many people go on feeling the missing limb, sometimes with pain. It can feel as real as the limb did, and a person may reach out with it or feel it clench.

V. S. Ramachandran invented a mirror box in the 1990s. A patient puts the intact hand in one side so that its reflection looks like the missing one, then moves it, and some felt relief from the pain. Later trials have had mixed results, so it's an interesting idea that works for some people and not others. The phenomenon itself is well documented. It shows that the brain keeps a map of the body, and that the map doesn't go away when the part does.

# Proprioception
@src Sherrington, 1906

Close your eyes and touch your nose. You can do it because of a sense you rarely notice, proprioception, which tells you where your limbs are. In 1906 the physiologist Charles Sherrington gave it its name.

Stretch sensors in your muscles, tendons and joints send constant information to the brain. People who lose it, from nerve disease, have to watch their limbs to move them at all. If they close their eyes, they collapse. It's the quiet sense behind walking in the dark, typing without looking and catching a ball. Practicing movements, as in yoga or dance, can sharpen it, and so can balance exercises, which become more important as you age.

# The vestibular system
@src Kandel et al., Principles of Neural Science

Deep in each inner ear are three fluid-filled loops that sense rotation of the head, and two small organs that sense tilt and acceleration. Together they're the vestibular system, which keeps you balanced and your gaze steady as you move.

When you turn your head, the fluid sloshes, and the brain uses the signal to move your eyes the opposite way, so the world doesn't blur. Motion sickness happens when the signals from this system don't match what your eyes see, as when you read in a moving car, so your brain gets conflicting reports. That's why looking out the window at the horizon helps. It gives your eyes and your ears the same story.

# The heart
@src Guyton & Hall, Textbook of Medical Physiology

Your heart beats about 100,000 times a day without a break. At rest it pumps about five liters of blood around the body each minute, which is roughly all the blood you have, so a drop of blood makes a complete trip in about a minute.

Over a long lifetime it will beat more than 2.5 billion times. It's a muscle, and regular exercise makes it stronger and more efficient. A trained athlete's resting heart rate can be as low as 40 beats per minute, because each beat moves more blood. It's the one muscle that never rests, and it's worth looking after. A brisk walk each day is one of the kindest things you can do for it.

# The skin
@src Guyton & Hall, Textbook of Medical Physiology

Skin is the body's largest organ, covering about two square meters in an adult. It keeps water in and germs out, helps regulate your temperature, makes vitamin D when exposed to sunlight, and gives you the sense of touch.

The outer layer constantly sheds and replaces itself, about once a month, so some of the dust in a home is shed skin. When you're hot, the skin sweats, and when you're cold, its blood vessels narrow to save heat. It's easy to take for granted, since we only notice it when it goes wrong, but it's a remarkable coat. It heals itself, it repairs cuts, and it is waterproof and flexible at the same time.

# The liver
@src Michalopoulos & DeFrances, 1997

The liver can do something no other internal organ does: regrow. If part of it is removed, as in surgery or in a living donation, the remainder can grow back to nearly the original size within weeks.

It's also a chemical workhorse that filters the blood, makes bile for digesting fat, stores sugar and breaks down drugs and toxins. It's why living-donor liver transplants are possible. A healthy person can give a portion to someone who is sick, and both livers grow to a useful size. The Greek myth of Prometheus, whose liver was eaten each day and grew back each night, may hint that the ancients knew something about it.

# The kidneys
@src Guyton & Hall, Textbook of Medical Physiology

Your two kidneys filter about 180 liters of fluid from your blood each day, far more than the volume of fluid in the body. Almost all of it is reclaimed, leaving about 1.5 liters to leave as urine.

The kidneys keep the balance of salts and water just right, and help control blood pressure. Each one has about a million tiny filters. People can live a healthy life with just one, which is why living kidney donation works. Because you can lose a lot of kidney function before you feel anything, problems are often found late. A blood test and a urine test are the way to catch them early, especially if you have diabetes or high blood pressure.

# The skeleton
@src Gray's Anatomy

An adult has 206 bones, but a newborn has around 300. Many of a baby's bones fuse together as it grows, such as the separate plates of the skull and the bones at the base of the spine.

Bone isn't dead. It's living tissue that constantly rebuilds itself, with old bone absorbed and new bone laid down, and it gets stronger with weight-bearing exercise. Over about a decade, most of the adult skeleton is replaced. That's why walking, lifting and jumping help keep bones dense, and why people who don't use their bones lose them. The skeleton is less like a scaffold and more like a garden that needs tending.

# Muscles
@src Gray's Anatomy

A muscle can only contract. It can pull, but it can never push. That's why muscles work in pairs on either side of a joint: one bends your elbow and its partner straightens it.

The body has more than 600 muscles. The heart is a special kind, which works without ever stopping. Moving the body well is a matter of timing and coordination between them. A simple step involves dozens of muscles switching on and off in order. When you train, you're improving the strength of the muscles, and also the way the brain directs them, which is why beginners often get stronger quickly, before their muscles have grown at all.

# The gut microbiome
@src Sender, Fuchs & Milo, 2016

The human body hosts trillions of microbes, mostly bacteria in the gut. For years, it was said that they outnumbered our own cells ten to one. In 2016 Ron Sender, Shai Fuchs and Ron Milo recalculated and found about 38 trillion bacteria and about 30 trillion human cells, closer to even.

They help digest food, make some vitamins and train the immune system, and scientists are still learning how much they affect health. A varied diet with plenty of fiber seems to support a varied community of bacteria. The old ten-to-one figure is a good example of a "fact" that was repeated for decades without being checked, and it was corrected when someone did the arithmetic.

# The gut-brain axis
@src Mayer, 2011
@conf mixed

Your gut and your brain are in constant conversation, mostly along the vagus nerve, a long nerve that runs between them. It's why stress can upset your stomach and why anxiety can make your gut feel tight.

In animal studies, changes in gut bacteria change behavior. How much this affects human mood is still being worked out, and claims made for probiotics are ahead of the evidence. The link is real, but the practical advice is not yet clear. For now, what's well supported is the simple things: eating regular meals, getting enough fiber and sleep, and noticing how stress shows up in your stomach.

# Innate and adaptive immunity
@src Janeway's Immunobiology

The immune system has two parts. The innate part is fast and general. Within hours, cells such as macrophages attack anything that looks foreign. They don't need to have met it before.

The adaptive part is slower but precise. T cells and B cells learn the particular shape of a germ over days, and make antibodies that fit it. Afterward, memory cells remember it, sometimes for decades, which is the principle behind vaccines. A vaccine shows the adaptive system a harmless piece of a germ, so that when the real one arrives, the response is quick. It's a bit like giving a guard a photograph of a burglar.

# Fever
@src Evans et al., 2015

A fever feels miserable, but it's part of the body's defense. A higher temperature slows the growth of many germs and helps your immune cells work faster. It isn't the illness itself. It's the body's response to it.

Treating a mild fever is mainly for comfort, and it isn't always necessary. If you feel better with a fever reducer, that's fine, but the fever is rarely dangerous by itself. A very high fever, or any fever in a young infant, is a reason to see a doctor, since it can signal a serious infection. How the person looks and acts is often more important than the number on the thermometer.

# Boosting your immune system
@src Harvard Health, 2020

You see "immune boosting" on supplement labels, but scientifically it isn't a meaningful idea. The immune system isn't a muscle that gets stronger the more it's stimulated. It's a balance, and an overactive one is what causes allergies and autoimmune diseases.

What actually supports it is plain: enough sleep, regular exercise, a balanced diet, vaccination, not smoking and managing stress. None of those is exciting, and none of them can be bottled. If a product claims to boost your immunity, it's reasonable to ask what that would mean, and what evidence there is. The honest answer is usually that nobody knows, and that the money would be better spent on food and a good pair of walking shoes.

# Allergies
@src Du Toit et al., 2015

An allergy is when the immune system treats something harmless, such as pollen or peanut, as a threat and produces antibodies called IgE against it. The next exposure triggers sneezing, itching or, in serious cases, anaphylaxis.

For years parents were told to keep peanuts away from babies. In 2015 the LEAP trial showed the opposite. Giving peanut foods early to infants at high risk cut their chance of a peanut allergy by about 80 percent. The old advice, though well meant, had probably made the problem worse. It's an example of how medical advice changes when it's properly tested, and why it's worth asking a doctor what the current guidance is.

# Insulin
@src Banting & Best, 1922

Insulin is the hormone that tells your cells to take up sugar from the blood. In 1921 Frederick Banting and Charles Best, working in a Toronto lab, isolated it. In January 1922 a 14-year-old boy named Leonard Thompson, who was dying of type 1 diabetes, got the first injection.

Within a year, people who would have died within months were being kept alive. Before that, the treatment for type 1 diabetes was a near-starvation diet that only prolonged life for a short time. Banting sold the patent for one dollar, saying that insulin belonged to the world. It's one of the great stories of modern medicine, and it happened not so long ago.

# Leptin
@src Zhang et al., Nature, 1994

In 1994 Jeffrey Friedman's team at Rockefeller University discovered leptin, a hormone made by fat cells that signals to the brain how much energy is stored. It tells the brain how well-fed the body is.

Mice that lack it eat constantly and become very obese, and so do rare people with the same genetic fault. Most people with obesity, however, have high levels of leptin, and the brain has become less responsive to it. So it isn't a simple cure. The discovery showed that body weight is regulated by hormones, and not only by willpower, which changed how researchers think about weight. Hunger and fullness are chemical signals, and they can be disrupted.

# Your DNA
@src Human Genome Project, 2003

The human genome is about 3 billion letters long, and any two people share about 99.9 percent of them. There are around 20,000 genes that code for proteins, but they make up only 1 or 2 percent of the sequence.

The rest includes regulatory regions that switch genes on and off, and a lot that we're still learning about. Small differences in those letters help explain why people differ in traits and in disease risk. It's humbling that the first full sequence took 13 years and billions of dollars, and that today it can be done in a day for a few hundred. Those few differences between us are tiny, compared with all that we share.
