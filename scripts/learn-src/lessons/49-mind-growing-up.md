# Piaget's map of a child's mind
@src Piaget, The Origins of Intelligence in Children, 1936; Flavell, 1963
@conf mixed

The Swiss psychologist Jean Piaget watched his own children and many others, and in the 1930s he described four stages of thinking. Babies learn through their senses and movements. Toddlers and preschoolers think with symbols, but not yet logically. School-age children can reason about concrete things in front of them. Teenagers can reason about abstract ideas and possibilities.

He was right that children think differently from adults, and not just that they know less. Later research found that the stages are not so sharp. Babies know more than Piaget thought, and many adults don't use abstract reasoning in every area. The stages work better as a rough map than as a ladder with fixed ages. But Piaget changed how we see children. They are not empty vessels. They are active thinkers building their own theories of the world.

# The babies who saw the impossible
@src Baillargeon, Spelke & Wasserman, Cognition, 1985

Piaget believed that very young babies lose track of an object once it is out of sight. In 1985 Renée Baillargeon and her colleagues tested this in a different way. Babies of about four months watched a screen swing up and down like a drawbridge. Then a box was placed behind the screen's path. In one version, the screen stopped when it reached the box, as it should. In another, the screen swung right through the space where the box had to be.

The babies looked much longer at the impossible event. They seemed to know that the box was still there, though they couldn't see it, and that solid things don't pass through each other. Babies can't tell us what they know, but they look longer at what surprises them. Studies like this suggest that infants have some understanding of the physical world very early.

# Where does Sally look?
@src Wimmer & Perner, Cognition, 1983; Baron-Cohen, Leslie & Frith, Cognition, 1985

Sally puts her marble in a basket and leaves the room. While she is gone, Anne moves the marble to a box. When Sally comes back, where will she look for it? Adults say the basket, since that's where Sally thinks it is. Most four-year-olds say the same. Many three-year-olds say the box, since that's where it really is.

Psychologists use this task to study the theory of mind, the understanding that other people have beliefs that may differ from what is true, or from what we know. Around the age of four, most children begin to grasp it. In 1985 Simon Baron-Cohen and his colleagues found that many children with autism were slower to pass the test, even when their other abilities were good. It's a window on how children learn to read other minds.

# Do babies pass the false belief test?
@src Onishi & Baillargeon, Science, 2005
@conf mixed

If four-year-olds pass the Sally-Anne test and three-year-olds fail it, does that mean that younger children can't understand false belief? In 2005 Kristine Onishi and Renée Baillargeon tried a version without words. Fifteen-month-old babies watched an actor reach for a toy hidden in one of two boxes. The toy moved while the actor wasn't looking, and then the actor reached for one box or the other.

The babies looked longer when the actor reached into the box where the toy really was, which was a surprise if the actor believed it was elsewhere. That suggested that they tracked what the actor believed. But attempts to repeat the study have had mixed results, and there is a debate about whether babies really think about beliefs or are following simpler rules. The question of when we begin to understand other minds remains open.

# The visual cliff
@src Gibson & Walk, Scientific American, 1960; Sorce et al., Developmental Psychology, 1985

In 1960 Eleanor Gibson and Richard Walk built an apparatus for testing depth perception in babies. It was a table covered with thick glass. One side had a pattern directly under the glass, and on the other side, the pattern was several feet below, so that it looked like a drop. They put infants in the middle, and had their mothers call to them from the far side.

Most of the babies who were old enough to crawl came across on the shallow side, but would not cross over the drop, though the glass would hold them. In 1985 Joseph Sorce and his colleagues made the cliff shallower and so ambiguous. When mothers looked happy, most babies crossed. When mothers looked afraid, none did. Babies look to a parent's face to tell them how to feel about something unclear.

# The face in the mirror
@src Amsterdam, Developmental Psychobiology, 1972; Lewis & Brooks-Gunn, 1979

When do children realize that the image in the mirror is themselves? In the 1970s Beulah Amsterdam and, later, Michael Lewis and Jeanne Brooks-Gunn tested infants by putting a spot of rouge on their noses without their noticing, and then putting them in front of a mirror. If a child sees the spot and touches their own nose, it suggests that they know it is a reflection of them.

Babies under about a year looked at the mirror image as if it were another child. The first signs of self-recognition came at around fifteen to eighteen months, and by about two years, most children touched the mark on their own nose. It's the same mirror test that has been used with chimpanzees. It marks an important step in the sense of self, and it comes with other changes, such as the first use of "me" and "mine."

# Born listening
@src DeCasper & Fifer, Science, 1980; DeCasper & Spence, 1986

Can newborns recognize their mother's voice? In 1980 Anthony DeCasper and William Fifer let newborns suck on a nipple connected to a recorder. By sucking in one pattern, a baby could turn on a recording of their mother's voice, and in another pattern, a stranger's voice. Within a few minutes, the babies sucked in the pattern that brought their mothers' voices.

The babies had been exposed to their mothers' voices only in the womb, in muffled form. In 1986 DeCasper and Melanie Spence asked pregnant women to read a children's story aloud every day for the last six weeks of pregnancy. After birth, the babies preferred to hear that story over a new one. Learning begins before birth. Babies are born attuned to the voice and the rhythm of the language that they will speak.

# Do newborns imitate?
@src Meltzoff & Moore, Science, 1977; Oostenbroek et al., Current Biology, 2016
@conf contested

In 1977 Andrew Meltzoff and Keith Moore reported something remarkable. Newborns only a few days old, and some just a few hours, seemed to copy an adult who stuck out his tongue or opened his mouth. It appeared to show an inborn ability to match what we see with what we do. It became a staple of psychology textbooks, and it was cited as evidence that babies are born with a link between their own bodies and others'.

In 2016 Janine Oostenbroek and her colleagues ran a large study that followed babies from one week to nine weeks of age and tested eleven different gestures. They found no evidence that babies imitated at any age. The babies stuck out their tongues when they were interested in something, no matter what the adult did. The older finding is now widely doubted. Imitation develops, but probably later than was claimed.

# Counting in infancy
@src Wynn, Nature, 1992
@conf mixed

In 1992 Karen Wynn showed five-month-old babies a doll on a stage. A screen came up and hid the doll. Then a hand placed a second doll behind the screen, in sight of the baby. When the screen dropped, the baby saw either two dolls, which is the right answer, or one doll, which is impossible.

The babies looked longer at the impossible result of one doll. Wynn concluded that babies can do simple arithmetic, adding one and one. The result was widely cited, and also criticized, since the babies might have been responding to changes in the amount of stuff on the stage, and not to numbers. Some later attempts to repeat it failed. It's agreed that infants have a rough sense of quantity. How much further it goes remains a matter of debate.

# Kohlberg's stages of moral reasoning
@src Kohlberg, 1958 and 1981; Gilligan, In a Different Voice, 1982
@conf mixed

In the 1950s Lawrence Kohlberg asked children and adults how they would solve moral dilemmas. The most famous is the story of Heinz, whose wife is dying. A druggist is charging a price that Heinz can't pay for a medicine that might save her. Should Heinz steal it? What mattered to Kohlberg was the reasoning, and not the answer.

He described three levels. At the first, people think about punishment and reward. At the second, they think about rules, duty and what others expect. At the third, they think about principles, such as justice and rights, that may go beyond the law. Carol Gilligan argued that his scheme favored an ethic of justice over an ethic of care. Studies in other cultures also found that the stages fit less well. It's a valuable idea about moral growth, but not a universal ladder.

# Erikson's eight crises
@src Erikson, Childhood and Society, 1950
@conf mixed

In 1950 the psychoanalyst Erik Erikson proposed that life is made up of eight stages, each with a central struggle. An infant faces trust versus mistrust. A toddler, autonomy versus shame. A child of school age, industry versus inferiority. A teenager, identity versus confusion. Then come intimacy versus isolation, generativity versus stagnation, and, in old age, integrity versus despair.

The stages are not tested in the way experiments would test them, and they are better seen as a way of thinking than as a scientific finding. But they have been influential. They gave us the term identity crisis, and they suggested that development continues throughout life, and not just in childhood. They also remind us that people at different times in life can have different tasks. It's useful to ask what the current one might be.

# Identity statuses
@src Marcia, JPSP, 1966

How do young people form an identity? In 1966 James Marcia interviewed college students about their choices of career and beliefs. He saw that two questions mattered. Had they explored different possibilities, and had they made a commitment? Combining the two gave four statuses.

Someone who has explored and committed has achieved an identity. Someone who is exploring and has not yet committed is in moratorium. Someone who has committed without exploring, often following parents' expectations, is in foreclosure. And someone who has neither explored nor committed is diffuse. People move among the statuses, and the order isn't fixed. The scheme is a handy way of seeing that not choosing yet may be part of growing up, and that a choice made without exploring may not hold.

# The years in between
@src Arnett, American Psychologist, 2000

In earlier generations, many people finished school, took a job, married and had children in their early twenties. In industrialized countries today, many do these things later. In 2000 Jeffrey Arnett proposed that there is now a stage between adolescence and adulthood, from about eighteen to twenty-five, which he called emerging adulthood.

He found that people in this stage describe it as a time of exploring identity, instability, a focus on themselves, feeling in between, and a sense of possibility. They don't feel that they are quite adults, but they are no longer teenagers. The idea was influential, though critics say it applies mostly to people with the money and the freedom to delay commitments. It suggests that adulthood doesn't arrive on a fixed date, and that it takes some people longer to find a footing.

# The midlife crisis
@src Wethington, 2000; Lachman, Teshale & Agrigoroaei, 2015
@conf mixed

The midlife crisis is a staple of popular culture, the idea that people in their forties and fifties go through a period of turmoil, and make dramatic changes. In 2000 Elaine Wethington surveyed a national sample of American adults. Only about one in four said that they'd had a midlife crisis, and most of those said that it was triggered by a specific event, such as a death or a divorce, and not by turning forty.

Other researchers have found a similar pattern. Midlife is often a busy and stressful time, with the care of children and parents and the peak of careers, but turmoil isn't universal. Many people report feeling more in control. The crisis seems to be a story that is more common than the experience. It's a reminder that how we think a stage of life will feel can differ from how it does.

# Two kinds of intelligence
@src Cattell, 1963; Hartshorne & Germine, Psychological Science, 2015

In 1963 Raymond Cattell described two kinds of intelligence. Fluid intelligence is the ability to solve new problems and to reason without relying on what you've learned. Crystallized intelligence is the knowledge and skills that you have built up over time, such as vocabulary and facts. They develop differently.

In 2015 Joshua Hartshorne and Laura Germine tested tens of thousands of people on a range of tasks, and found that different abilities peak at different ages. Speed of processing peaks in the late teens, and memory for names in the early thirties. Vocabulary keeps rising until the sixties or even later. There is no single peak for the mind. We lose some things as we age, and gain others. The skills that grow tend to be those that depend on knowledge.

# Why time left changes what we want
@src Carstensen, Isaacowitz & Charles, American Psychologist, 1999

Why do older people often seem calmer and more content? In the 1990s Laura Carstensen proposed socioemotional selectivity theory. When people see their time as long, they pursue goals for the future, such as learning, meeting new people and building a career. When they see time as limited, they shift to goals that bring meaning and emotional satisfaction now.

It's about the sense of time, and not about age as such. Young people who are told to imagine that they are about to move far away choose, like older people, to spend time with close friends and family. Older people have smaller social circles, but they are often closer and more satisfying. The theory suggests that the sense of what matters most shifts when we think that time is short, and that this can be a source of wisdom.

# Wiser with age
@src Grossmann et al., PNAS, 2010

Does wisdom increase with age? In 2010 Igor Grossmann and his colleagues asked people of different ages to think about conflicts, such as problems between groups or in relationships, that they read about in newspaper stories. They looked at how people reasoned. Did they consider other points of view, recognize uncertainty and look for compromise?

Older people did better. They were more likely to take different perspectives and to look for a middle path. Wise reasoning was not related to intelligence, but increased with age, up to the oldest group in the study, and people in their sixties and seventies did best. The results suggest that experience builds a particular kind of social reasoning. It also shows that some of what we gain with age is not something that tests of speed and memory measure.

# The Matthew effect in reading
@src Stanovich, Reading Research Quarterly, 1986

In the Gospel of Matthew, it says that to those who have, more will be given. In 1986 Keith Stanovich applied the phrase to reading. Children who start out as good readers read more, learn more words and gain more knowledge, and so get better still. Children who start out slowly read less, and fall further behind.

The gap isn't just in reading skill. Reading builds vocabulary and background knowledge, which in turn make everything else in school easier. The differences can grow over the years. This is why help in the early years is so valuable, since a small gap can become a large one. It also gives a reason for adults to read, as a way to keep growing.

# The summer slide
@src Cooper, Nye, Charlton, Lindsay & Greathouse, Review of Educational Research, 1996

What happens to what children learn during the long summer break? In 1996 Harris Cooper and his colleagues combined the results of thirty-nine studies that had tested students at the end of one school year and the start of the next. On average, students lost about a month of learning over the summer.

Math skills dropped more than reading skills, on average. For reading, the loss was larger for children from low-income families, who had fewer books and activities in the summer. Middle-class children sometimes held steady or improved. The idea of a summer slide has become an argument for summer reading and programs. Some later studies find that the loss is smaller than first thought, but the pattern of unequal summers has been seen again.

# Small classes
@src Krueger, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 1999

Does class size matter? Many studies had tried to answer this, with mixed results, because schools with smaller classes differ in other ways. In the 1980s the state of Tennessee ran an experiment, called Project STAR. Thousands of children in kindergarten were randomly assigned to small classes of thirteen to seventeen, or regular classes of twenty-two to twenty-five.

The students in the smaller classes did better on tests, and the gains were largest for children from low-income families and for minority students. Follow-ups found some benefits lasting into later years, such as higher rates of college entry. In 1999 Alan Krueger reanalyzed the data and confirmed the effect. Because the assignment was random, it's some of the best evidence that we have. It's expensive, though, so the question is whether the same money could do more elsewhere.

# What a great teacher is worth
@src Chetty, Friedman & Rockoff, American Economic Review, 2014

In 2014 Raj Chetty, John Friedman and Jonah Rockoff used records of millions of students in a large American city, and tracked them from elementary school into adulthood. They asked whether teachers who raised their students' test scores more than others did something that mattered later in life.

They found that students of high value-added teachers were more likely to go to college and earned more as adults. By their estimates, replacing a teacher in the bottom five percent with an average one would raise the lifetime income of a class by hundreds of thousands of dollars. The work has been debated, in particular the use of test scores to judge individual teachers. But it suggests that a good teacher has an effect that lasts long after the school year ends.

# Phones out of the classroom
@src Beland & Murphy, 2016

Do mobile phones harm learning in schools? In 2016 Louis-Philippe Beland and Richard Murphy studied schools in four English cities. Some of the schools had introduced a ban on phones, and others had not. The researchers looked at students' test results before and after the bans.

After a ban, test scores rose a little, a modest but real amount. The students who gained the most were the ones with the lowest results, while the highest achievers were not affected. This suggests that phones are a distraction, most for those who find it hardest to stay focused. The effect was modest, but the cost was low, and it fits the other studies of the way that distraction harms learning.

# Does homework help?
@src Cooper, Robinson & Patall, Review of Educational Research, 2006

Does homework make students learn more? In 2006 Harris Cooper and his colleagues reviewed the research from the previous twenty years. They found that students who did more homework tended to do better in school. But the link was much stronger in middle and high school than in elementary school.

For young children, the link was weak or absent. For older students, a modest amount helped, and the benefits seemed to level off after about one to two hours a night in high school. Most of the studies were correlations, so the cause is unclear. Students who do well may also do more homework. The evidence suggests that the amount matters less than whether it is purposeful, and that long hours for young children probably help little.

# Words that make people walk slowly
@src Bargh, Chen & Burrows, JPSP, 1996; Doyen et al., PLOS ONE, 2012
@conf contested

In 1996 John Bargh and his colleagues asked students to make sentences from scrambled words. For some students, the words were related to old age, such as Florida, wrinkle and gray. Others got neutral words. Then the students walked down a hallway, and a researcher timed them. The ones who had seen the old-age words walked more slowly.

It was a famous finding, and it was taken as proof that words can influence behavior without our knowing it. In 2012 Stéphane Doyen and his colleagues tried to repeat it, using automatic timing instead of an experimenter. They could not find the effect, unless the experimenters had been told to expect it. It suggests that the original result may have come from the experimenters' expectations. It's a major case in the debate about replication in psychology.

# Does disgust make us moral judges?
@src Schnall, Haidt, Clore & Jordan, PSPB, 2008; Johnson et al., Social Psychological and Personality Science, 2016
@conf contested

In 2008 Simone Schnall and her colleagues tested whether feelings of disgust change moral judgments. In one experiment, people filled out questionnaires about moral dilemmas, such as whether it is wrong to lie on a résumé. Some people did it in a room that had been sprayed with a foul smell, and others in a clean room. The people in the smelly room judged the acts to be more wrong.

The result fit a theory that moral judgment is rooted in emotion. In 2016 a group led by David Johnson tried to repeat one of the studies with a larger sample, and found no effect. Others have found effects that are small, or only in certain groups. The theory isn't dead, but the specific claim that a bad smell makes us harsher judges is on shaky ground.

# Does thinking about money make us selfish?
@src Vohs, Mead & Goode, Science, 2006
@conf contested

In 2006 Kathleen Vohs and her colleagues reported that even faint reminders of money change behavior. People who did tasks that mentioned money, such as unscrambling phrases about cash, or sitting near a pile of Monopoly money, later worked longer on a hard puzzle before asking for help. They also helped others less, and preferred to play and work alone.

The study was widely cited as evidence that money makes people self-reliant and less social. Later, other labs tried to repeat the finding, and many of them failed, though a few found small effects. It's another example of an appealing result that didn't hold up. It's a reminder to treat a single study as a hint and not as a fact.

# Is the bystander effect real?
@src Philpot et al., American Psychologist, 2020

For decades the textbook story was that people don't help in an emergency when others are around. The finding was based on lab experiments, and on an account of the murder of Kitty Genovese in 1964 that turned out to be exaggerated. In 2020 Richard Philpot and his colleagues did something new. They studied security camera footage of 219 real public conflicts, such as fights and threats, in Amsterdam, Cape Town and Lancaster.

In ninety-one percent of the incidents, at least one person stepped in to help. The more people there were around, the more likely it was that someone would help. The researchers suggested that in real, dangerous situations, people are more likely to see that help is needed than they are in a lab. The old effect is real in some settings, but it's weaker than the textbooks say.

# Terror and judgment
@src Rosenblatt, Greenberg, Solomon, Pyszczynski & Lyon, JPSP, 1989
@conf mixed

In 1989 Abram Rosenblatt and his colleagues asked municipal court judges to set bail for a woman accused of prostitution. Half of the judges were first asked to fill out a questionnaire that reminded them of their own death. The others answered neutral questions. The judges who had thought about their own death set a much higher bail.

The finding was an early result for terror management theory. The idea is that awareness of death makes people cling to their worldviews and to those who share them, and be harsher on those who violate them. There were hundreds of studies. But large replication efforts have not always found the effect, and the theory is debated. It's an intriguing idea about how fear shapes values, with an uncertain base.
