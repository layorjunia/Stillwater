# Do women really talk more?
@src Mehl et al., Science, 2007

It's a popular belief that women talk much more than men, often put at three times as much. In 2007 Matthias Mehl and his colleagues put the claim to a direct test. They gave 396 university students small recorders that captured snippets of their speech throughout the day, for periods of two to ten days, and counted the words. Women spoke about 16,200 words a day, and men about 15,700. The difference was tiny and not statistically significant.

There was a lot of variation, but it was between individuals, not between the sexes. Some people said under 1,000 words a day, and some said more than 40,000. It's an example of how a belief can feel obviously true, and be supported by anecdotes, and still fail when somebody counts. Talkativeness is a personal trait, and whether you're male or female tells you very little about it.

# Different styles, not different manners
@src Tannen, That's Not What I Meant!, 1986

The linguist Deborah Tannen studied recordings of dinner conversations among friends, and noticed that some people talk in a style that she called high involvement. They speak quickly, overlap each other, jump in with enthusiasm and ask rapid questions. Others, with a high considerateness style, wait for a clear gap before speaking and leave pauses. Neither is rude in their own eyes.

Trouble starts when the two meet. The high-involvement speaker sees the pauses of the other as a lack of interest, and the considerate speaker feels run over by what seems like interruptions. Each may think the other is at fault. Tannen's point is that many conflicts are about style, and not about substance. If a conversation keeps going wrong, it can help to ask whether you and the other person simply have different rules for when to speak.

# Direct and indirect cultures
@src Hall, Beyond Culture, 1976
@conf mixed

The anthropologist Edward Hall proposed in 1976 that cultures differ in how much meaning is carried by the words themselves. In a low-context culture, such as the United States or Germany, people tend to say things explicitly, and a good communicator is a clear one. In a high-context culture, such as Japan or many Arab countries, much of the meaning is carried by the situation, the relationship and what is left unsaid.

The distinction is a useful lens, though it's a generalization, and individuals vary a great deal. Misunderstandings occur when someone from a low-context background hears an indirect "That might be difficult" as a maybe, when it means no. And a person from a high-context background can find a blunt remark rude. A good habit when talking across cultures is to ask, kindly and plainly, what the other person means.

# What the first hundred milliseconds tell us
@src Willis & Todorov, Psychological Science, 2006

In 2006 Janine Willis and Alexander Todorov showed people photographs of unfamiliar faces for a tenth of a second, half a second or a full second, and asked them to rate traits like trustworthiness and competence. Ratings made after only a tenth of a second were strongly correlated with ratings made with no time limit. More time made people more confident, but not different in their judgments.

So our first impressions form almost instantly. That doesn't mean they're accurate. Faces say little about character, and snap judgments of trustworthiness have been shown to predict very little about how people really behave. But they do affect how we treat each other, in hiring, voting and everyday dealings. Knowing that the impression forms in a flash, it's worth giving people a second chance to correct it.

# Thin slices
@src Ambady & Rosenthal, JPSP, 1993

In 1993 Nalini Ambady and Robert Rosenthal showed people silent video clips of college teachers, lasting only thirty seconds or less. The viewers rated the teachers on qualities like warmth and enthusiasm. Remarkably, their ratings predicted how students rated the same teachers at the end of a whole semester. Even clips of just six seconds worked nearly as well.

They called it thin-slicing, which is judging from a very small sample of behavior. It shows that a lot of social information is available at a glance, and that we pick it up without trying. But thin slices can mislead, and they work best when they capture real behavior, such as warmth, and not stereotypes. It's a reminder that your manner, in the first few seconds, is already telling people something.

# Nobody is looking at you as much as you think
@src Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky, JPSP, 2000

In 2000 Thomas Gilovich and his colleagues asked college students to put on a T-shirt with a picture of Barry Manilow, which most of them found embarrassing, and walk into a room full of other students. Afterward, they were asked how many people had noticed the shirt. The wearers guessed about half. In fact, only about a quarter of the people had noticed.

The researchers called it the spotlight effect. We feel that we're at the center of everyone's attention, because we're at the center of our own. In reality, others are busy with their own worries. It applies to a bad haircut, a stumble in a speech and a slip of the tongue. Most of what we're sure that others noticed, they didn't, and what they did notice, they forgot much sooner than we did.

# People can't see how nervous you are
@src Savitsky & Gilovich, JESP, 2003

When you give a talk, you may feel that your nerves show: your hands tremble, your voice wobbles and everyone can see it. In 2003 Kenneth Savitsky and Thomas Gilovich had students give speeches, and asked them to rate how nervous they appeared. Then they asked the audience. The speakers thought they looked more nervous than the audience judged them to be.

The researchers call it the illusion of transparency, the belief that our inner states leak out more than they do. In a second experiment, speakers who were told about this illusion beforehand gave better talks and felt calmer. It's a comforting thing to remember. The feeling of anxiety is much louder inside your head than it is from the audience's seat, and the audience is mostly on your side.

# Get excited
@src Brooks, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2014

Before a speech, an exam or a performance, people often try to calm themselves, and are told, "Just relax." In 2014 Alison Wood Brooks tested a different approach. She had people sing a song in front of an audience, give a speech, or do a hard math test. Before they began, some were told to say out loud, "I am anxious." Others said, "I am excited."

Those who said that they were excited felt more excited, and their performance was better. They sang more accurately, spoke more persuasively and scored higher on the math. The explanation is that anxiety and excitement are both states of high arousal, and it's much easier to turn one into the other than to switch it off. Saying "I'm excited" tells your mind that the racing heart means something good.

# Fast talkers sound smarter
@src Miller, Maruyama, Beaber & Valone, JPSP, 1976

In 1976 Norman Miller and his colleagues played people recordings of speakers who said the same words at different speeds, from slow to quite fast, and asked them how credible and persuasive the speakers were. Faster speakers were rated as more knowledgeable, more intelligent and more convincing, and they changed more minds.

The pattern isn't a license to gabble. The fastest speech was a bit faster than normal, but still clear, and a speaker who is hard to follow loses an audience. The finding suggests that a slow, hesitant delivery can make a good argument seem less sure. Speaking a little more briskly, with confidence and with pauses at the right places, can help your listeners to trust what you say.

# The voice of a leader
@src Klofstad, Anderson & Peters, Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 2012

In 2012 Casey Klofstad and his colleagues played people pairs of recordings of the same speaker, one with a slightly deeper pitch and one with a higher pitch, saying, "I urge you to vote for me this November." They asked which of the two they'd rather vote for. Listeners favored the lower voice, and this held for both men and women.

The preference was strongest for both sexes in the same direction, and voters said that the lower voice seemed stronger and more competent. Real elections show a similar pattern. Candidates with lower voices have won more often in some studies. Of course, pitch says nothing about whether a leader is any good. It's a bias worth knowing about, since you might be affected by a voice without realizing it.

# Short words win
@src Oppenheimer, Applied Cognitive Psychology, 2006

Many people believe that using big words makes them sound intelligent. In 2006 Daniel Oppenheimer tested it. He took texts, such as graduate school application essays and translations of Descartes, and replaced words with longer synonyms, and then asked people to rate the intelligence of the authors. The authors of the simpler versions were rated as more intelligent, and the ones with the long words were rated as less.

His paper was called "Consequences of erudite vernacular utilized irrespective of necessity," which made its own point. Readers find complicated writing harder to process, and they interpret the difficulty as a sign of something wrong with the author. If you want to be thought smart, write plainly. A short word is almost always better than a long one with the same meaning.

# Rhyme as reason
@src McGlone & Tofighbakhsh, Psychological Science, 2000

Does a saying seem truer if it rhymes? In 2000 Matthew McGlone and Jessica Tofighbakhsh gave people unfamiliar aphorisms and asked how accurate they thought they were. Some of the sayings rhymed, such as "What sobriety conceals, alcohol reveals," and others were reworded so that they didn't, such as "What sobriety conceals, alcohol unmasks." The rhyming ones were judged to be more accurate.

The sound shouldn't matter to the truth of a saying, and yet it did. Rhyme makes a sentence easier to process, and ease can pass for truth, which we saw in the illusory truth effect. It's why slogans and proverbs are so persuasive, and a reminder that a catchy phrase isn't evidence.

# Why we laugh
@src Provine, Laughter: A Scientific Investigation, 2000

The neuroscientist Robert Provine did something unusual: he went out and listened to people laugh. He and his students recorded what was said right before laughter in conversations in public places, hundreds of cases. Very little of the laughter followed a joke. Most followed ordinary remarks, such as "I'll see you guys later" or "It was nice meeting you."

His conclusion was that laughter is mainly a social signal, and not a response to humor. It shows that we're friendly, and smooths over awkwardness. Speakers laugh more than listeners do, and we're about thirty times more likely to laugh when we're with others than when we're alone. So when someone laughs at your small remark, they probably aren't judging how funny you are. They're telling you that they like you.

# Laughing together
@src Dunbar et al., Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 2012

Laughter doesn't only mark that we're friendly. It seems to help to bind us together. In 2012 Robin Dunbar and his colleagues tested the pain threshold of people before and after they watched a funny video, and compared it with other people who'd watched something dull. Those who laughed hard together could tolerate more pain afterward. They tested this by measuring how long people could keep a frozen wine sleeve on their arm.

Dunbar's explanation is that laughing triggers a release of endorphins, the body's natural opioids, which also help to create warm feelings between people. It echoes the idea that language and laughter evolved as ways of keeping groups close. If it's true, laughing with someone is a bit like grooming. It's a good reason not to be too serious around the people you care about.

# The happiest people talk more deeply
@src Mehl, Vazire, Holleran & Clark, Psychological Science, 2010
@conf mixed

In 2010 Matthias Mehl and his colleagues had 79 people carry a recorder that sampled their conversations for four days, and then compared the conversations with each person's reported well-being. The happiest people spent less time alone and more time talking to others. And they'd had about twice as many substantive conversations as the unhappiest people, and about a third as much small talk.

The study doesn't prove that deep talk makes people happy. It might be that happy people simply seek it out. A later attempt to repeat the work, with nearly 500 people, found that substantive conversation and time with others went with life satisfaction, but it found no reliable link for small talk. So the case for deeper talk has held up better than the case against small talk. It's an invitation to move one step past "How was your day?" with the people you like.

# Conversation, not word counts
@src Romeo et al., Psychological Science, 2018
@conf mixed

In the 1990s researchers Betty Hart and Todd Risley reported that poor children heard far fewer words at home than richer children, a gap that was later popularized as 30 million words. The finding shaped many programs for parents, and it's been criticized for its small sample and for how it was measured, and a later attempt to repeat it found much smaller differences.

In 2018 Rachel Romeo and her colleagues recorded children at home and scanned their brains. They found that what predicted language skill wasn't the number of words that a child heard, but the number of back-and-forth turns between the child and an adult. Children who had more conversational turns also showed more activity in a brain region that is important for language. The lesson is a warm one. Talking with a child, and not just at them, is what helps.

# The sound of a foreign language
@src Keysar, Hayakawa & An, Psychological Science, 2012

In 2012 Boaz Keysar and his colleagues tested whether the language that you think in changes how you make decisions. In a series of experiments, people made choices in their native tongue or in a foreign language that they'd learned well. One example was a gamble in which a sure gain was compared with a risky one. In their native language, people showed the usual bias, preferring the sure thing when it was framed as a gain and the gamble when framed as a loss. In the foreign language, the bias was much weaker.

The authors suggest that a foreign language is less emotional, and so makes us think more slowly and carefully. It's a curious and encouraging finding. If you have a big decision, you might try thinking it through in a second language, or at least put it into plain words, and see if your view of it changes.

# Do words change what we see?
@src Winawer et al., PNAS, 2007
@conf mixed

Russian has two basic words for blue, one for light blue and another for dark. English speakers have only one. In 2007 Jonathan Winawer and his colleagues showed Russian and English speakers three squares of blue and asked which of two matched a third. Russian speakers were faster when the two shades fell on different sides of their language's light-dark boundary. English speakers showed no such pattern.

The finding is among the best evidence for the idea that language shapes perception a little. The effect was small, and it disappeared when people repeated a string of digits aloud while doing the task, which suggests that the words were helping them in real time. The strong claim, that people think in ways determined by their language, has not been supported. But the weak one, that words nudge us, seems to be right.

# The snow words that were not
@src Pullum, The Great Eskimo Vocabulary Hoax, 1991

You may have heard that the Eskimo people have fifty, a hundred or even more words for snow, which supposedly shows how their language shapes their thought. The linguist Geoffrey Pullum traced the claim and showed it to be largely a myth. The story grew with each retelling, from a handful of words in a 1911 essay by the anthropologist Franz Boas to hundreds in popular articles.

In fact, the Inuit and Yupik languages build words by adding endings, so a single root can be made into a huge number of forms, much as English can say "snowy," "snowing" or "snowdrift." Counting the forms isn't meaningful. English also has many words for snow, from slush to powder, among skiers. The case is a good lesson in how an appealing fact can spread without being checked.

# Why swearing helps
@src Stephens, Atkins & Kingston, NeuroReport, 2009; Stephens & Umland, 2011

In 2009 Richard Stephens and his colleagues at Keele University asked students to hold a hand in a bucket of icy water for as long as they could. In one trial, they could repeat a swear word of their choice. In another, they repeated a neutral word. The students lasted longer, and felt less pain, when they were swearing.

The researchers think that swearing triggers an emotional response, which may be a mild stress reaction, that dampens pain. It worked best for people who didn't swear all the time, and it faded for those who used swear words habitually. So a mild curse after you stub a toe may really help. It's also a reminder that language isn't just for conveying information. Words can act on the body.

# Talking to yourself helps
@src Lupyan & Swingley, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 2012

Many people talk to themselves when they look for something. In 2012 Gary Lupyan and Daniel Swingley tested whether it helps. People were asked to find an object, such as a banana, among others in a picture or in a pretend store. Some were told to say the name of the object out loud as they searched. Those who spoke it found the item faster than those who stayed quiet, as long as the object's name was one that they actually knew.

The effect was real, though small. Saying a word aloud seems to prime your visual system to look for it. Talking to yourself isn't a sign of a problem. It's a normal and useful tool. Next time you're hunting for your keys, try saying "keys" out loud.

# The anger that does not leave
@src Bushman, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2002

Many people believe that venting anger is healthy, for example by hitting a pillow. In 2002 Brad Bushman tested the idea. He made students angry with a harsh comment on an essay. Then some were asked to hit a punching bag while thinking of the person who'd insulted them. Others sat quietly, or did a different kind of exercise. Afterward, they were given a chance to blast the person with loud noise.

Those who had punched the bag while thinking of the person were the most aggressive. Those who sat quietly were the least. The result goes against the old idea of catharsis, which says that expressing anger discharges it. The evidence suggests that acting on anger keeps it going. Better ways to cool off are to wait, breathe, or do something calming and unrelated.

# When to take a break
@src Gottman, The Relationship Cure, 2001
@conf mixed

John Gottman noticed that in heated arguments, people's heart rates shot up. When it went above about 100 beats a minute, he said, they entered a state of flooding, in which they couldn't listen or think well. They'd get defensive, or shut down, and talking more didn't help.

His advice was to call a timeout when you feel flooded, and to take at least 20 minutes before resuming, doing something calming, and not rehearsing the argument in your head. The specific numbers come from his own lab observations and haven't been tested as widely as the idea. But the principle is sound. It's hard to be reasonable when your body is in alarm, and a short pause gives your body time to settle before you try again.

# Getting to yes: interests, not positions
@src Fisher & Ury, Getting to Yes, 1981

In 1981 Roger Fisher and William Ury, who worked at the Harvard Negotiation Project, published a book with a simple idea. People in a dispute usually argue over positions, what they say they want. But behind each position is an interest, the reason why they want it. A classic illustration in the negotiation literature: two sisters fight over the last orange, and each demands it. A compromise cuts it in half, but one wanted the juice and the other wanted the peel for baking. Each could have had all that she needed.

The authors urged negotiators to ask why, to look for interests that are compatible, and to separate people from the problem. They also advised inventing options for mutual gain before deciding, and insisting on fair criteria. The method works in a business deal or a family argument. When you're stuck, stop asking what the other person wants and begin asking why.

# Knowing your walk-away option
@src Fisher & Ury, Getting to Yes, 1981

Fisher and Ury introduced a useful term for negotiators: BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. It's what you'll do if the talks fail. If you're selling a car and have no other buyer, your alternative is weak, and you'll be keen to accept a low offer. If another buyer has offered a good price, your alternative is strong and you can hold out for more.

The idea is that your power in a negotiation comes mostly from your alternatives, and not from how tough you act. It suggests two practical steps. Before you negotiate, work out your best alternative, and try to improve it. And try to understand the other side's alternative, since it determines how far they'll go. Many bad deals are made by people who hadn't thought about what they'd do if they said no.

# The first number sets the stage
@src Galinsky & Mussweiler, JPSP, 2001

In negotiation, who should make the first offer? In 2001 Adam Galinsky and Thomas Mussweiler had students negotiate the sale of a gas station in a role-play. When the seller made the first offer, the final price was higher than when the buyer did. The first number acted as an anchor, as we saw in the anchoring effect, and the final deal tended to land closer to it.

The advantage goes to the one who opens, but there's a catch. If you open with an absurdly high number, you may offend the other person and kill the deal. And the anchoring effect weakens when the other person focuses on their own goals and alternatives, and not on your number. The practical advice is to do your research, make a bold but defensible first offer, and don't let a number from the other side set your own expectations.

# Perspective beats empathy in negotiation
@src Galinsky, Maddux, Gilin & White, Psychological Science, 2008

You might think that the best negotiators are those who feel for the other side. In 2008 Adam Galinsky and his colleagues tested this. They had people negotiate in role-plays, and encouraged some to focus on what the other side was thinking, which is perspective taking, and others to focus on what the other side was feeling, which is empathy. The perspective takers found more creative solutions and did better for both sides, and for themselves.

Empathizers gained less, and in some tasks gave in more easily. The researchers suggested that understanding the other person's goals and thoughts lets you find trades that suit both, while sharing their feelings makes it hard to hold your ground. It doesn't mean empathy is bad. But in a negotiation, it helps to understand your counterpart's mind, and to keep your own interests in view.

# Unfair offers get rejected
@src Güth, Schmittberger & Schwarze, 1982

In 1982 the economist Werner Güth and his colleagues devised the ultimatum game. One player is given a sum of money, say 10 dollars, and proposes how to split it with a second player. If the second accepts, both get their share. If the second refuses, both get nothing. A purely selfish second player should accept any offer above zero, since something is better than nothing.

But in experiments around the world, people regularly reject offers below roughly a fifth to a third of the total, even at a cost to themselves. Offers close to half are the most common. We seem to care a lot about fairness, and we're willing to pay to punish those who violate it. The game suggests that in negotiations, a very one-sided offer may be refused out of principle, even when it would be profitable to accept.

# Anger can win a deal, but it comes at a cost
@src Van Kleef, De Dreu & Manstead, JPSP, 2004
@conf mixed

Does it help to show anger in a negotiation? In 2004 Gerben van Kleef and his colleagues had people negotiate by computer with a simulated opponent that either showed anger or happiness in its messages. Opponents who seemed angry got more concessions, as people tried to avoid a fight. Those who seemed happy got fewer.

But there's a catch, which later studies have found. Anger can backfire, particularly when the other side has a good alternative, or feels that the anger is unjustified. People may then walk away, or retaliate, or look for a way to get even. In the long run, relationships suffer. It's a tactic with a price, and the price rises when you'll need to deal with the person again.
