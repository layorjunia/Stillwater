# Albert Einstein
@src Einstein, 1905

In 1905, while working as a patent clerk in Bern, Switzerland, the 26-year-old Albert Einstein published four papers in a single year, now called his miracle year. He had a day job and did his physics in his spare time, with no university position.

The papers explained the photoelectric effect, which showed that light comes in packets. They proved the existence of atoms by explaining Brownian motion, and presented the special theory of relativity. And a short follow-up gave the equation E equals mc squared. Any one of them would have made a career. Ten years later, he completed the general theory of relativity. He became a household name in 1919, after an eclipse expedition confirmed one of its predictions.

# Max Planck
@src Planck, 1900

In 1900 Max Planck was trying to explain the glow of hot objects, and found that the numbers worked only if energy came in tiny packets, which he called quanta. The old theory predicted that a hot object would give off an infinite amount of energy at short wavelengths, which was plainly wrong.

He thought of the packets as a mathematical trick and was uneasy about it. It took years for physicists, including Einstein, to realize that it described something real, and Planck's constant became a foundation of quantum theory. He won the 1918 Nobel Prize in Physics. He was a conservative and modest man, and he said later that he'd been forced into the idea as an act of desperation. It shows that great discoveries can come from people who didn't set out to overturn anything.

# Niels Bohr
@src Bohr, 1913

In 1913 the Danish physicist Niels Bohr proposed that electrons in atoms can only occupy certain fixed energy levels and give off light when they jump between them. It explained why hydrogen glows in specific colors, something that no earlier model had managed.

He later led the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics, and his institute in Copenhagen became a gathering place for the world's best young physicists. During the Second World War, he helped Jewish scientists escape from Nazi-occupied Europe, and he escaped to Sweden himself in 1943, by boat, and then flew to Britain. After the war, he campaigned for open sharing of nuclear knowledge. He is remembered as both a great scientist and a man of principle.

# Paul Dirac
@src Dirac, 1928; Anderson, 1932

In 1928 the British physicist Paul Dirac wrote down an equation that combined quantum mechanics and special relativity to describe the electron. To his surprise, it had solutions that implied a particle with the electron's mass but opposite charge, which he first thought was the proton.

In 1932 Carl Anderson found just such a particle, the positron, in cosmic rays. It was the first evidence of antimatter, and Dirac shared the 1933 Nobel Prize. Dirac was famously quiet, so much so that his colleagues joked about a unit of speech, a "dirac," meaning one word per hour. His equation was so elegant that he said that it was more important to have beauty in your equations than to have them fit the experiments. It's an unusual view, but in his case, it worked.

# James Clerk Maxwell
@src Maxwell, 1865

In the 1860s the Scottish physicist James Clerk Maxwell wrote down a set of equations that described how electric and magnetic fields behave. He found that they predicted waves that travel at the speed of light, and he concluded that light itself is an electromagnetic wave.

Later, Heinrich Hertz generated such waves, which led to radio. Einstein said that Maxwell's work was the most profound change in physics since Newton. Maxwell also demonstrated the first color photograph, in 1861, and he contributed to the theory of gases and to understanding Saturn's rings. He died at 48, of cancer. Every time you use a phone, a radio or a microwave oven, you are using a prediction he made on paper, 150 years ago.

# Michael Faraday
@src Faraday, 1831

Michael Faraday left school at 13 to work as a bookbinder's apprentice, and he read the books he bound. One of them, on chemistry, caught his interest, and he attended public lectures by the chemist Humphry Davy and wrote up careful notes.

He became assistant to Davy, and in 1831 he discovered electromagnetic induction: a changing magnetic field creates an electric current. That's how generators work. He also began the Royal Institution's Christmas Lectures for young people in 1825, which are still held every year. He turned down a knighthood and the presidency of the Royal Society, preferring to stay a plain Mr. Faraday. He had almost no mathematics, but he saw the invisible fields so clearly that Maxwell was able to put them into equations.

# Nikola Tesla
@src Tesla, 1888

Nikola Tesla was a Serbian-American inventor who worked briefly for Thomas Edison. He had come to the United States in 1884 with almost no money, and a head full of ideas about electric motors.

He designed a practical alternating-current induction motor, which he patented in 1888, and the American industrialist George Westinghouse bought his patents. In the "war of the currents," Westinghouse and Tesla's alternating current beat Edison's direct current, because AC can be sent over long distances at high voltage. It's how electricity is still distributed today. Tesla had many more ideas, some brilliant and some impractical, and he died in a New York hotel room in 1943, in debt. Today, the unit of magnetic flux density bears his name.

# Richard Feynman
@src Rogers Commission, 1986

Richard Feynman was a physicist who won the 1965 Nobel Prize for his work on quantum electrodynamics. He was also a gifted teacher, and his lectures on physics, given at Caltech in the early 1960s, are still in print and still admired.

After the Challenger disaster in 1986, he served on the investigating commission. At a televised hearing, he dropped a piece of a rubber O-ring into a glass of ice water and showed that it lost its springiness in the cold, which explained why the seals had failed on the cold launch morning. It took a few seconds and a cup of ice water to make clear what weeks of testimony hadn't. It was typical of his approach, which was to look for the simplest way of seeing the truth.

# Claude Shannon
@src Shannon, 1948

In 1937, as a master's student, Claude Shannon realized that electrical circuits could carry out logic, laying the foundations for digital computers. Some have called it the most important master's thesis of the century.

In 1948 he published "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," which founded information theory. It showed that any information can be measured in bits and sent through a noisy channel without error, if coded properly. It's behind everything from phone calls to the internet. Shannon was also playful. He rode a unicycle through the halls of Bell Labs while juggling, and built a mechanical mouse that could solve a maze. A man whose work underlies the digital world was, apparently, having a very good time.

# John von Neumann
@src von Neumann, 1945

John von Neumann was a Hungarian-American mathematician who made major contributions to quantum mechanics, game theory and the design of computers. As a child, he could divide eight-digit numbers in his head, and he'd tell jokes in ancient Greek.

The stored-program architecture used by most computers today, in which instructions and data live in the same memory, is still called the von Neumann architecture. His colleagues, many of them brilliant themselves, often said that he was the fastest mind they ever met. He worked on the Manhattan Project and later on the first hydrogen bomb. He died in 1957, aged 53, of cancer. Many believe that he did more to shape the world of computing than almost anyone else.

# Tim Berners-Lee
@src CERN, 1993

In 1989 Tim Berners-Lee, a British computer scientist at CERN in Switzerland, proposed a system for sharing documents through links over the internet. His boss wrote "vague but exciting" on the proposal. At that time, the internet existed, but it was used mainly by specialists and was hard to navigate.

In 1990 he built the first web browser and server. In 1993 CERN announced that the technology would be free for everyone to use, with no royalties, which is a big reason the web took off. Berners-Lee could have become very rich, but chose not to patent it. The web and the internet are not the same thing. The internet is the network of cables, and the web is one of the things that runs on it.

# Carl Linnaeus
@src Linnaeus, 1735, 1753

The Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus created the system that scientists still use to name living things. In his Systema Naturae of 1735 and in Species Plantarum of 1753, he organized plants and animals into groups, and gave each species a two-part Latin name, a genus and a species.

Our own species name, Homo sapiens, means "wise human." Before this, names were long, descriptive phrases, and they differed from one country to another. Linnaeus's tidy system gave naturalists around the world a common language. He also sent his students on voyages to collect specimens, and a number of them died in the process. Today, every newly discovered species is still given a name in the form that he established.

# Alexander von Humboldt
@src Humboldt, 1807

The German naturalist Alexander von Humboldt traveled through Latin America from 1799 to 1804, measuring and collecting at every opportunity. He climbed volcanoes, measured the temperature of ocean currents and collected thousands of plants.

He saw nature as an interconnected web, in which climate, altitude and plants were all linked, long before the word ecology existed. He influenced Charles Darwin, who carried his books on the Beagle, and many others, including the writer Henry David Thoreau. In 1802 he climbed higher on Chimborazo, in Ecuador, than anyone had climbed before. He was probably the most famous scientist in the world in his time, and many places and species carry his name.

# Mary Anning
@src Natural History Museum, London

Mary Anning grew up poor in the English seaside town of Lyme Regis, where she and her family collected fossils from the cliffs and sold them. Her father died when she was young, and the family depended on what they could find.

Around 1811, when she was about twelve, she and her brother found the skeleton of a giant marine reptile, an ichthyosaur. Later she found the first complete plesiosaur. She was largely uncredited by the scientific community, and as a woman she couldn't join the Geological Society. Even so, scientists traveled to buy her fossils and to ask her advice, and she knew more about the anatomy than many of them. In 2010 the Royal Society included her in a list of the ten British women who most influenced science.

# Jane Goodall
@src Goodall, 1964

In 1960 Jane Goodall, then 26, went to Gombe in Tanzania to study wild chimpanzees. She had no university degree, and she was accompanied by her mother for the first few months, because the authorities wouldn't allow a young woman to go alone.

She watched one, whom she called David Greybeard, strip leaves from a twig and use it to fish termites out of a mound. It was the first observation of tool-making in animals other than humans. Her mentor Louis Leakey wrote that we must now redefine tool, redefine man, or accept chimpanzees as human. She gave her chimps names and described their personalities, which was unconventional at the time. Her research at Gombe is now the longest-running study of wild chimpanzees.

# Rachel Carson
@src Carson, 1962

The American marine biologist Rachel Carson published Silent Spring in 1962, a book that described how pesticides such as DDT were poisoning birds and the food chain. The title imagines a spring without birdsong.

It was attacked by the chemical industry, but it changed public opinion. It helped lead to the US ban on DDT for agricultural use in 1972 and the creation of the Environmental Protection Agency. She wrote it while being treated for breast cancer, and died in 1964. Carson was also a gifted writer, and her earlier book on the sea, The Sea Around Us, was a best seller. She is a model of how clear writing can move a whole society.

# Norman Borlaug
@src Nobel Peace Prize, 1970

The American agronomist Norman Borlaug spent years in Mexico breeding wheat that was high-yielding and resistant to rust disease. He worked in the field himself, in hard conditions, and crossed thousands of varieties.

In the 1960s his varieties were introduced in India and Pakistan, and yields nearly doubled in a few years, helping to prevent widespread famine. This was a key part of what became known as the Green Revolution. He won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970. Some experts have estimated that his work saved hundreds of millions of lives, though that number is hard to pin down. He kept working into his nineties, and he argued that hunger was a problem that science could solve.

# Fritz Haber
@src Haber, 1909

The German chemist Fritz Haber invented a way to make ammonia from nitrogen in the air, which made artificial fertilizer possible and now feeds billions. He won the 1918 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it, and it is one of the most consequential inventions of the twentieth century.

But he also led the German chemical weapons program in the First World War, and personally oversaw the first major chlorine gas attack in 1915. His wife Clara, a chemist who opposed his work, took her own life that year. Haber was Jewish by birth, and he was forced to leave Germany in 1933 after the Nazis came to power. His life shows how science can be a source of both great good and great harm, sometimes in the same person.

# Jonas Salk
@src Francis report, 1955

In 1955 the American physician Jonas Salk announced that his polio vaccine was safe and effective, following a huge trial with 1.8 million children. At the time polio paralyzed and killed thousands every year, and people were jubilant. Church bells rang and factories fell silent.

When the journalist Edward R. Murrow asked who owned the patent, Salk reportedly replied, "Well, the people, I would say. There is no patent. Could you patent the sun?" He never earned money from the vaccine. In the US, polio cases dropped from tens of thousands a year to a few thousand by 1957, and to a few hundred in the early 1960s. Salk went on to found the Salk Institute in California, where research continues today.

# Hedy Lamarr
@src US Patent 2,292,387

Hedy Lamarr was a famous film actress in the 1930s and 40s who was also an inventor. She'd fled Austria and her first husband, an arms dealer, and she had picked up some technical knowledge from listening to his conversations.

In 1941 she and the composer George Antheil filed a patent for a system of "frequency hopping," in which a radio signal rapidly changes frequencies in a pattern known to sender and receiver, to prevent enemy jamming of torpedo guidance. It was granted in 1942. The Navy didn't use it at the time. The principle is related to technologies later used in Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and GPS, though they were developed independently. She was belatedly honored for her invention in 1997.

# Wangari Maathai
@src Nobel Peace Prize, 2004

In 1977 the Kenyan environmentalist Wangari Maathai founded the Green Belt Movement, which paid poor rural women a small amount to plant trees, to restore forests, prevent erosion and provide firewood. She was the first woman in East and Central Africa to earn a doctorate.

Over the decades, the movement planted tens of millions of trees. She was jailed and beaten for her activism, since she also campaigned against corruption and land seizures. In 2004 she won the Nobel Peace Prize, the first African woman to receive it. She said that when she planted a tree, she was planting hope. Her story shows how a simple, practical idea can grow into a movement that changes lives.

# Tu Youyou
@src Tu, 2011

In the late 1960s the Chinese government set up a secret project to find a malaria cure, and Tu Youyou led a team that searched ancient Chinese medical texts. Malaria was killing soldiers on both sides in the Vietnam War, and the existing drugs had stopped working.

A recipe for sweet wormwood gave her an idea. The instructions said to soak the plant in cold water, not boil it, and she realized that heat might destroy the active ingredient. The compound she extracted, artemisinin, has saved millions of lives. She won the 2015 Nobel Prize in Medicine. She had no doctorate and no foreign experience, and she was the first Chinese woman to win a Nobel Prize in science. She tested the extract on herself first, to make sure that it was safe.

# Srinivasa Ramanujan
@src Hardy, 1940

Ramanujan was a clerk in Madras, India, who taught himself advanced mathematics from a single textbook. In 1913 he sent a letter with pages of formulas to the Cambridge mathematician G. H. Hardy, who recognized his genius and brought him to England.

Once when Hardy visited him in the hospital, he mentioned that his taxi had a boring number, 1729. Ramanujan replied that it's the smallest number that can be written as the sum of two cubes in two different ways. Ramanujan worked out thousands of results, many of them without proofs, and mathematicians are still proving them today. He died in 1920 at age 32. Hardy said that his greatest discovery in mathematics was Ramanujan.

# Archimedes
@src Vitruvius; Plutarch

Archimedes of Syracuse was the greatest scientist of the ancient world. He worked out the principle of the lever, found the rule for floating bodies and calculated the area of a circle. He is said to have claimed that, given a place to stand, he could move the Earth.

In the famous story, he stepped into a bath, realized how to measure the volume of a king's crown by the water it displaced, and ran through the streets shouting "Eureka." He was killed in 212 BC when the Romans captured his city. According to one account, he was absorbed in a diagram drawn in the sand when a soldier came upon him. His tomb was marked with a sphere and a cylinder, the result of which he was proudest.

# Eratosthenes
@src Cleomedes

Eratosthenes was the head of the great Library of Alexandria in the 3rd century BC. He'd heard that at noon on midsummer day, the Sun shone straight down a well at Syene in southern Egypt, while in Alexandria, a stick cast a shadow.

From the angle of that shadow and the distance between the two cities, he calculated Earth's circumference. His result was within a few percent of the true value, depending on which unit of length he used. He did it more than two thousand years ago, with a stick, a well and some arithmetic. It's a lasting reminder that educated people knew the Earth was round long before Columbus, and that measuring what's in front of you can reveal what you cannot see.

# Hypatia
@src Socrates Scholasticus

Hypatia was a mathematician, astronomer and philosopher in Alexandria, Egypt, in the late 4th and early 5th centuries. She taught students from various religions and was widely respected. She was the daughter of a mathematician, Theon, and she edited some of his works.

In 415 she was killed by a mob during a period of political and religious conflict in the city, and her death has been remembered as a symbol of the loss of ancient learning. Very little of her own writing survives, so most of what we know comes from letters by her students and from later accounts. Her story is a reminder of how fragile a tradition of learning can be.

# Ibn al-Haytham
@src Book of Optics, c. 1011-21

Ibn al-Haytham, known in Europe as Alhazen, worked in Cairo around the year 1000. In his Book of Optics, he proved that we see because light enters the eye, and not because rays leave the eye, as many ancient thinkers believed.

He tested ideas with experiments, used the camera obscura to study light and insisted that claims should be tested. Some historians call him a founder of the scientific method. According to one story, he pretended to be mad in order to escape a promise to control the flooding of the Nile, which he had realized was impossible, and spent years under house arrest. During that time he wrote his greatest book. He's a good example of how much early science came from the Islamic world.

# Al-Khwarizmi
@src al-Khwarizmi, c. 820

Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi worked in the House of Wisdom in Baghdad in the early 9th century. His book on solving equations, whose title included the word al-jabr, gave us the word algebra.

Another of his books introduced the Hindu-Arabic numerals to the Arab world and, through Latin translations, to Europe. His name, in Latin form, gave us the word algorithm. So two of the most important words in mathematics and computing trace back to one man, who lived more than 1,200 years ago. He also made world maps and helped to calculate the positions of the stars. The House of Wisdom, where he worked, was one of the great centers of learning of its time.

# Avicenna
@src Avicenna, c. 1025

Ibn Sina, known as Avicenna in Europe, was a Persian physician and philosopher. By his early twenties, he had become a well-known doctor, and he is said to have treated a prince of Bukhara.

His Canon of Medicine, completed around 1025, was a huge encyclopedia that described how infectious diseases can spread, recommended quarantine and set out rules for testing drugs. It was translated into Latin and used as a textbook in European universities for about six centuries, into the 1600s. He also wrote on philosophy, and his writings were studied in many countries. His influence on medicine lasted longer than that of nearly any other doctor in history.

# Aryabhata
@src Aryabhata, 499

The Indian mathematician and astronomer Aryabhata wrote a short, dense book in the year 499. In it he stated that the Earth rotates on its axis, and that this causes the apparent daily motion of the stars, a thousand years before Copernicus.

He also gave a value for pi, 3.1416, which is very accurate, and he explained eclipses as shadows, not as acts of demons. India's first satellite, launched in 1975, was named after him. He wrote the book when he was only 23, according to his own account. Like many ancient works, it was compressed into short verses to make it easy to memorize. A single, thin volume that held so much was a remarkable achievement.

# Leonardo da Vinci
@src Kemp, Leonardo (2004)

Leonardo is famous as the painter of the Mona Lisa and The Last Supper, but he filled thousands of pages of notebooks with studies of anatomy, water, flight and machines. About 7,000 pages survive, and many were written in mirror image, from right to left.

He drew the valves of the heart and flying machines centuries before others. But he never published his notes, which were scattered after his death, so they had little influence on science at the time. They were rediscovered only much later. If he had published, the history of anatomy and engineering might have been different. His curiosity was endless, and that is perhaps his greatest gift to us.

# Vesalius and Harvey: opening the body
@src Vesalius, 1543; Harvey, 1628

For about 1,400 years, doctors relied on the writings of the Roman physician Galen, who'd mostly dissected animals. In 1543 the young Flemish anatomist Andreas Vesalius published De humani corporis fabrica, based on his own dissections of human bodies, and showed that Galen was wrong in many details. The book had magnificent illustrations, and it transformed the teaching of medicine.

In 1628 the English physician William Harvey published a book arguing that blood circulates around the body, pumped by the heart. He had made a simple calculation: the heart pumps out far more blood in an hour than the body could possibly manufacture, so it must be going around in a circle. It contradicted ancient teaching, and many doctors rejected it for years, but he was right.

# Hooke and Leeuwenhoek: the first microscopists
@src Hooke, 1665; van Leeuwenhoek, 1676

In 1665 the English scientist Robert Hooke published Micrographia, a book with spectacular drawings of what he'd seen under his microscope, including a flea and a louse. Looking at a slice of cork, he saw little box-like compartments, which reminded him of monks' cells, and he called them "cells." He was also involved in famous disputes with Isaac Newton over credit for discoveries in optics and gravity.

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was a cloth merchant in Delft in the Netherlands who ground his own tiny lenses, some magnifying nearly 270 times, better than any microscope of his time. In the 1670s he looked at pond water, and the plaque on his own teeth, and saw swarms of tiny living things, which he called "animalcules." The Royal Society in London doubted him until Robert Hooke confirmed the observations.

# Benjamin Franklin
@src Franklin, 1751

Benjamin Franklin was a printer, writer and diplomat, and a serious scientist. He proposed in 1749 that a pointed iron rod could protect buildings from lightning, and the lightning rod was in use by 1752. It was one of the first practical results of the study of electricity.

He also invented bifocal glasses, made one of the first maps of the Gulf Stream and introduced the terms positive and negative for electric charge. His famous kite experiment is probably a mix of fact and legend, since it's not clear that he ever did it as the story is told. He refused to patent his inventions, writing that as we benefit from the inventions of others, we should be glad to serve others by any invention of ours.
