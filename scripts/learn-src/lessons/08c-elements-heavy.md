# Europium and terbium: the rare earths that glow
@src Demarçay, 1901

Europium glows brightly red under ultraviolet light, and euro banknotes use it as a security feature. Under UV, the genuine notes show glowing marks and fibers that counterfeit paper lacks. The element was named by the French chemist Eugène-Anatole Demarçay in 1901 for the continent of Europe, so the choice of banknote is a neat coincidence.

Terbium is its green counterpart. When excited, it glows a bright green, which made it useful in the phosphors for fluorescent lamps and older color televisions. Europium supplied the red in those same screens, so between them these two rare earths helped paint the colors of the late twentieth century. Terbium is also used in some solid-state devices and in certain kinds of strong magnets. Neither element is bright when you hold it in your hand. It takes a jolt of energy to make them shine.

# Samarium, dysprosium and holmium: the magnet metals
@src Standard chemistry text

Samarium is a silvery metal named for the mineral samarskite, which was in turn named for a Russian mining official, Colonel Vasili Samarsky-Bykhovets. That makes it the first element named, indirectly, for a person. Samarium-cobalt magnets stay strong at very high temperatures. A radioactive form, samarium-153, is used as a medicine to relieve pain from cancer that has spread to the bones.

Dysprosium is what keeps the strongest magnets working in hot conditions. A small amount added to neodymium magnets lets them keep their strength at high temperatures, which is important in electric car motors and wind turbines. The name comes from the Greek for "hard to get," because it took Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran more than 30 attempts to isolate it from holmium in 1886. Holmium has the strongest magnetic moment of any element, and it's used to concentrate magnetic fields in some of the world's most powerful laboratory magnets. Per Teodor Cleve, who found it in 1878, named it for Holmia, the Latin name for Stockholm.

# Gadolinium and lutetium: elements in the scanners
@src Standard radiology text

Gadolinium is strongly magnetic near room temperature. Compounds containing it are injected into patients as "contrast agents" to make MRI images clearer, because they change the magnetic signal from nearby water molecules. The element is named in honor of Johan Gadolin, the Finnish chemist who discovered the first rare earth mineral in that quarry in Ytterby.

Lutetium is the heaviest and hardest of the lanthanides. Crystals that contain it are used in PET scanners, where they detect the gamma rays that come from a radioactive tracer injected into the patient. A radioactive form of it, lutetium-177, is also used in a newer treatment for certain prostate cancers and tumors, carrying radiation directly to cancer cells. Between them, the two metals show how some of the least familiar elements end up in the hospital, helping to see inside the body and to treat disease.

# Erbium, thulium and ytterbium: cables, lasers and clocks
@src Mears et al., 1987; Ludlow et al., 2015

Erbium is the secret behind much of the internet. In fiber-optic cables, light signals fade as they travel. Erbium-doped fiber amplifiers, developed in 1986 and 1987, strengthen the light directly, without converting it to electricity and back. They sit along the undersea cables that carry internet traffic across oceans. Thulium, the least abundant of the stable rare earths, is still more common than gold or platinum. It's used in some medical lasers and in a few portable X-ray devices, and Per Teodor Cleve named it in 1879 for Thule, the ancient name for the most northerly region of the world.

Ytterbium atoms are used in the most precise clocks ever built. Optical lattice clocks that use them are stable to about one part in 10 to the 18th power, which means that if one had been started at the Big Bang, it would be off by less than a second today. These clocks are so sensitive that they can detect the effect of a height difference of a centimeter on the speed of time.

# Hafnium and tantalum: tiny parts in your pocket
@src Intel, 2007

Hafnium is excellent at absorbing neutrons, so it's used to make control rods that regulate nuclear reactors, including in some nuclear submarines. It also plays a role in computing. Starting in 2007, chipmakers like Intel began using hafnium oxide in place of silicon dioxide as the insulating layer in transistors, which let them keep making chips smaller without leaking current.

Tantalum is used to make tiny, reliable capacitors in phones and laptops, so there's probably some in your pocket. It's also extremely resistant to attack by body fluids, so it's used in surgical implants and in pacemakers. A lot of it comes from a mineral called coltan, and, as with cobalt, mining it in some regions has been linked to conflict and harm to workers. Neither metal is well known, but both sit inside devices that most of us use every day.

# Tungsten, element 74
@src Standard chemistry text

Tungsten has the highest melting point of any metal, 3,422 degrees Celsius, and the highest of all elements except carbon. That's why it was used for the glowing filaments in incandescent light bulbs for about a century. The filament can glow white-hot without melting, which no other practical material could do.

It's also very dense and hard. Tungsten carbide is used to make cutting tools and drill bits, and it can cut through steel. Its chemical symbol, W, comes from its other name, wolfram, which is the name still used in German. The name tungsten comes from the Swedish for "heavy stone." When you see an old light bulb glowing, it's the tungsten that you are looking at, running hotter than the surface of a small star.

# Rhenium, element 75
@src Noddack, Tacke & Berg, 1925

Rhenium was the last stable element to be discovered, in 1925 by Walter Noddack, Ida Tacke and Otto Berg in Germany. It's among the rarest elements in the Earth's crust, so finding it meant sifting through tons of ore for a tiny amount. They named it for the Rhine, the river that runs through their homeland.

It's used in superalloys for jet engine turbine blades, because it keeps its strength at extremely high temperatures, allowing engines to run hotter and more efficiently. Ida Tacke later married Walter Noddack, and the couple worked together for many years. She was nominated for a Nobel Prize more than once, though she never won. It's a rare element with an important job, and a good story of persistence.

# Osmium and iridium: the heavy metals
@src Alvarez et al., 1980

Osmium is the densest naturally occurring element. A cubic centimeter weighs about 22.6 grams, roughly 22 times as much as the same volume of water. A volleyball-size lump would weigh over 200 pounds. Its name comes from the Greek word for smell, because one of its compounds, osmium tetroxide, gives off a strong, unpleasant odor, and it's also highly toxic.

Iridium is a very dense, corrosion-resistant metal that's rare in Earth's crust but much more common in asteroids and meteorites. In 1980 the physicist Luis Alvarez and his son Walter, a geologist, found a thin layer of clay rich in iridium in rocks around the world, exactly at the time the dinosaurs died out. It pointed them to the idea that an asteroid had struck the Earth. A faint line of heavy metal in a rock became evidence of one of the greatest disasters in the planet's history.

# Platinum and the kilogram
@src BIPM

Platinum is a dense, rare metal that doesn't tarnish or corrode, and it's valued for jewelry and as a catalyst in car exhaust systems and chemical manufacturing. Its resistance to corrosion is what made it so useful for the one job that most people never think about, which is defining how heavy a kilogram is.

From 1889 until 2019, the international standard for the kilogram was a small cylinder made of an alloy of 90 percent platinum and 10 percent iridium, kept under three glass bells in a vault near Paris. Copies were sent around the world, and over the years they drifted very slightly from the original. In 2019 the kilogram was redefined in terms of a fundamental constant of physics, so it no longer depends on a lump of metal in a vault.

# Gold, element 79
@src Standard chemistry text

Gold is chemically inert, so it doesn't tarnish or rust, and ancient gold objects still look as bright as the day they were made. A mask from the tomb of Tutankhamun is more than 3,000 years old and shines as if it were new.

Most of its atoms were probably created in the collisions of neutron stars, and were scattered through space long before the Earth formed. It's incredibly malleable. A single gram can be hammered into a sheet about a square meter in size, so thin that light shines through it with a greenish tint. All the gold ever mined would fit into a cube about 22 meters on each side, which is smaller than you might think.

# Mercury, element 80
@src Standard toxicology text

Mercury is the only metal that's liquid at room temperature, and its silvery blobs were fascinating to people for centuries. It was used in thermometers, in barometers and in dental fillings. Ancient doctors even gave it as a medicine.

But it's a potent poison. In the 1950s, in the Japanese city of Minamata, a factory dumped mercury waste into the bay, which contaminated the fish that people ate and caused severe neurological damage and birth defects. Thousands of people were harmed. Thermometers today use other liquids. A 2013 international treaty, named the Minamata Convention after the city, aims to reduce the use and release of mercury around the world.

# Thallium, element 81
@src Standard toxicology text

Thallium is a soft metal whose salts have no taste or smell and cause a slow, mysterious illness that includes hair loss. That made it a favorite weapon of mystery writers, and it was once sold as a rat poison.

In Agatha Christie's 1961 novel The Pale Horse, a murderer uses it, and in 1977 a nurse in London who had read the book recognized the symptoms in a real patient, and the diagnosis saved a life. The patient was a young child who had been critically ill, and doctors had no idea what was wrong. It's one of the rare cases in which a detective story helped in a real medical emergency, and the book is sometimes cited in medical journals.

# Lead, element 82
@src Pirkle et al., 1994

Lead has been used for thousands of years. The Romans made water pipes out of it, and its Latin name, plumbum, gave us the word "plumbing" and the symbol Pb. It's soft, easy to work and doesn't rust, so it has long been tempting to use.

But lead is a neurotoxin, especially harmful to children. After leaded gasoline was phased out in the US, average blood lead levels in Americans fell by about 78 percent between 1976 and 1991. It's one of the great public health successes. The change took decades of work, including by the scientist Clair Patterson, who showed how widespread the pollution was. Lead paint and old pipes can still be a risk in older homes, so it's worth testing if you're not sure.

# Bismuth, element 83
@src Marcillac et al., 2003

Bismuth was long thought to be the heaviest stable element, but in 2003 scientists found that it's very slightly radioactive. Its half-life is about 20 billion billion years, over a billion times the age of the universe, so for all practical purposes it's stable.

It's the key ingredient in Pepto-Bismol, the pink stomach remedy, and it's unusual among heavy metals in being almost nonpoisonous. When melted and cooled slowly, it forms beautiful rainbow-colored stair-step crystals, and the colors come from a thin layer of oxide on the surface. They're a favorite of collectors, and some people grow them at home. It's a surprisingly gentle metal for one that is so heavy, and it was nearly the last stable element on the table.

# Polonium and radium: the Curies' elements
@src Curie, 1898

Marie Curie discovered polonium in 1898, together with her husband Pierre, while searching a uranium ore for the source of its unusual radioactivity. She named it for her native Poland, then partitioned between other countries, as a political statement. It's intensely radioactive and highly toxic. In 2006, a former Russian intelligence officer, Alexander Litvinenko, died in London after being poisoned with it.

The Curies found radium the same year, and it glows faintly in the dark. After that, it was used in everything from luminous watch dials to supposed health tonics. In the 1920s, young women employed to paint watch dials with radium paint, who were told to point their brushes with their lips, developed severe illnesses. Their legal fight, as the "Radium Girls," led to workplace safety laws. Marie Curie herself died in 1934 of an illness that was probably caused by her years of exposure.

# Astatine and francium: the rarest natural elements
@src Perey, 1939

Astatine is the rarest naturally occurring element on Earth. Scientists estimate that less than 30 grams exist in the entire crust at any moment, because its most stable isotopes decay within hours. The name comes from the Greek for "unstable." It was first made artificially in 1940, and is being studied as a way to deliver radiation to cancer cells.

Francium is even more fleeting, with its longest-lived isotope lasting only about 22 minutes. The French physicist Marguerite Perey discovered it in 1939 at the Curie Institute in Paris, and named it for her homeland. She was the first woman elected to the French Academy of Sciences. Only tiny amounts have ever been made, and it has never been seen as a visible sample. Between them, these two elements show that the table has places where matter barely has time to exist.

# Radon, element 86
@src US EPA

Radon is a colorless, odorless radioactive gas that seeps up through the ground as uranium in rocks decays. It can collect in basements and houses, and after smoking it's the second leading cause of lung cancer in the US, according to the EPA.

You can't see it, smell it or taste it, which is why testing is the only way to know. A home test kit costs only a few dollars, and if levels are high, a vent fan system installed under the house can reduce them. Levels vary a lot from one house to the next, even on the same street. It's one of the few household dangers where a small, cheap test and a modest repair can make a real difference to your health.

# Actinium, thorium and protactinium
@src Berzelius, 1828; Fajans & Göhring, 1913

Actinium is a silvery radioactive metal that glows with a pale blue light in the dark, from the radiation it gives off. It's about 150 times as radioactive as radium, and its name comes from the Greek for "ray." One of its isotopes, actinium-225, is being tested in medicine, in drugs that deliver tiny particles of radiation directly to cancer cells.

Thorium is a radioactive metal that's about three times more abundant than uranium in Earth's crust. It has been studied as a possible fuel for nuclear reactors, which some say could be safer. It was discovered in 1828 by the Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius, who named it for Thor, the Norse god of thunder. Protactinium sits between thorium and uranium. It was identified in 1913 by Kasimir Fajans and Oswald Göhring, and its name means "before actinium," because when it decays, it turns into actinium.

# Uranium, element 92
@src Hahn & Strassmann, 1938

Uranium is the heaviest element found in nature in large amounts. In December 1938 the German chemists Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann bombarded uranium with neutrons and found that it split into lighter elements, to their surprise. They hadn't expected an atom to break in two.

Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch explained the process, which they called fission, in early 1939. Meitner had been forced to flee Germany earlier that year because she was Jewish, and she worked out the explanation from exile. Today nuclear power plants supply about 10 percent of the world's electricity. The same discovery that made nuclear energy possible also led to nuclear weapons, a reminder that science can bring benefits and dangers together.

# Neptunium and plutonium: beyond uranium
@src McMillan & Abelson, 1940; Seaborg et al., 1941

Neptunium was the first element heavier than uranium to be made. In 1940 at the University of California, Berkeley, Edwin McMillan and Philip Abelson bombarded uranium with neutrons, creating a new element. It was named for Neptune, the planet beyond Uranus, just as uranium was named for Uranus. Elements beyond uranium are called transuranic elements.

Plutonium was first produced in 1940 as well, by a team led by Glenn Seaborg at Berkeley, and it was named for Pluto, which was then considered a planet. It's the fuel in some nuclear weapons: the bomb dropped on Nagasaki in August 1945 was a plutonium device. Its isotope plutonium-238 produces heat as it decays, and that's used to power spacecraft, including Voyager and the Curiosity rover. The same element appears in both a terrible weapon and a quiet source of power for exploring the solar system.

# Americium, element 95
@src Seaborg et al., 1944

Americium is in more homes than you might guess. Most ionization smoke detectors contain a tiny amount of americium-241, which ionizes the air in a small chamber so that a small electric current flows. When smoke enters, it disrupts the current, and the alarm sounds.

Seaborg's team made the element in 1944, and named it, like the element above it, europium, for a continent. The amount in a detector is about as much as a speck, and the alpha radiation it gives off can be stopped by a sheet of paper. It's an unusual thing, that a man-made element created in a wartime laboratory should end up on the ceiling of so many bedrooms, quietly guarding families.

# Curium, berkelium and californium
@src Thompson et al., 1950

Curium was made in 1944 by Glenn Seaborg's team at Berkeley and named for Marie and Pierre Curie, the pioneers of radioactivity research. One of its isotopes, curium-244, gives off alpha particles, which are used in instruments on several Mars rovers called alpha-particle X-ray spectrometers, to work out which elements are in rocks and soil.

Berkelium was first made in 1949 at the University of California, Berkeley, and named for the city. It's highly radioactive and can only be made in tiny amounts, in specialized reactors. In 2010, a batch of berkelium that was made at Oak Ridge National Laboratory was used to make the first atoms of element 117, tennessine. Californium, first made at Berkeley in 1950, emits a lot of neutrons, which makes it useful for starting up nuclear reactors, detecting explosives and finding gold and oil. Just a microgram of californium-252 can give off about two million neutrons per second.

# Einsteinium and fermium: from the first hydrogen bomb
@src Ghiorso et al., 1955

Einsteinium was discovered in an unusual place: the fallout from the first hydrogen bomb test, "Ivy Mike," in November 1952, in the Pacific. Scientists at Berkeley found it in the debris collected from the explosion. The discovery was kept secret for three years because of the Cold War. The element was named for Albert Einstein, who died in 1955.

Fermium was found in the same fallout. It's named for Enrico Fermi, who built the first nuclear reactor in 1942. It's the heaviest element that can be made by repeated capture of neutrons in a nuclear reactor. To make elements beyond it, scientists have to smash atoms together in particle accelerators. The explosion, in which uranium was hit by an enormous flood of neutrons, did in an instant what a reactor would take years to do.

# Mendelevium, nobelium and lawrencium
@src Ghiorso et al., 1955; IUPAC, 1997

Mendelevium was the first element to be made one atom at a time. In 1955 Albert Ghiorso and colleagues at Berkeley bombarded a tiny speck of einsteinium and detected just 17 atoms in total, one by one, across several experiments. It's named for Dmitri Mendeleev, who created the periodic table, and who had predicted undiscovered elements, so it was fitting.

Nobelium is named for Alfred Nobel, who founded the Nobel Prizes. Its discovery was disputed for years. A group in Stockholm claimed it in 1957, but others couldn't repeat the work, and labs in the US and the Soviet Union each made competing claims. Lawrencium is named for Ernest Lawrence, who invented the cyclotron, the type of particle accelerator that was used to discover so many new elements. It was first made in 1961 and lasts only hours, so studying its chemistry is very difficult.

# Rutherfordium, dubnium and seaborgium
@src IUPAC, 1997

Rutherfordium was the first of the superheavy elements, those beyond the actinide series. It's named for Ernest Rutherford, the New Zealand-born physicist who discovered the atomic nucleus. It has no stable isotopes, and the longest-lived known ones last about an hour. During the Cold War, teams in the US and the Soviet Union both claimed to have made it first, and they disagreed over the name.

Dubnium is named for the town of Dubna in Russia, where the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research is located, and where a team created it. Its most stable known isotope lasts about 16 hours. In 1997 the international chemistry body settled the quarrel over names. Seaborgium honors Glenn Seaborg, who helped discover ten elements, including plutonium. It was controversial because he was still alive when the name was proposed in 1994. He lived to see it officially named, and died in 1999.

# Meitnerium, element 109
@src IUPAC, 1997

Lise Meitner, an Austrian-Swedish physicist, helped to explain nuclear fission in 1938, but was left out when her colleague Otto Hahn received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it in 1944. Many consider this a serious injustice. She had worked with Hahn for thirty years, and it was she who worked out, with her nephew Otto Frisch, what had actually happened to the uranium.

Element 109 was first made in 1982 in Germany, and named for her in 1997. She's the only woman besides Marie Curie to have an element named for her. Meitner never took part in the building of the atomic bomb, and she refused to when asked. She said she wanted nothing to do with it. The element named for her is, like her, a reminder of how much credit can go astray.

# Bohrium, hassium, darmstadtium and roentgenium
@src Münzenberg et al., 1981, 1984; Hofmann et al., 1995

A group of elements came out of a single German laboratory, the GSI near Darmstadt. Bohrium is named for Niels Bohr, the Danish physicist who explained the structure of the atom, and was first made in 1981 when the team fused atoms of bismuth and chromium together. Hassium is named for Hesse, the German state that includes Darmstadt, where it was first made in 1984. In 2001, chemists managed to study a few atoms of it, and showed that it forms a compound similar to that of osmium, as expected from its position in the periodic table.

Darmstadtium, first made in 1994, is named for the city, and its longest-lived known isotope lasts about ten seconds, which is long for an atom this heavy. Roentgenium, also first made in 1994, is named for Wilhelm Röntgen, who discovered X-rays in 1895 and won the first Nobel Prize in Physics in 1901. Only a few atoms of it have ever existed, so nobody has seen a sample, and its properties are mostly predicted.

# Copernicium, element 112
@src IUPAC, 2010

Copernicium honors Nicolaus Copernicus, who proposed that the Earth moves around the Sun. It's fitting that an element at the very edge of the table should be named for a scientist who changed the way people saw their place in the universe. It was made in 1996 at the GSI lab, and officially named in 2010, on the anniversary of Copernicus's birthday.

Chemists have studied a few atoms of it and suggest that it behaves like a very volatile metal, and might be a liquid or even a gas at room temperature. If so, it would be a very unusual metal, and it would show an odd effect of relativity on the heaviest atoms. The electrons move so fast that they behave a little differently from what the usual rules would predict. It's a fine example of how extreme atoms test our theories.

# Nihonium, element 113
@src Morita et al., 2012

Nihonium was the first element ever discovered in Asia. A team at Japan's RIKEN institute, led by Kosuke Morita, made three atoms of it, in 2004, 2005 and 2012, by firing zinc atoms at bismuth. The long wait between the first and the last atom shows how rare these events are. In nine years, only three atoms were seen.

"Nihon" is one of the Japanese names for Japan. The name was officially approved in 2016, and it was celebrated in Japan, where many saw it as the result of persistence. The team's patience was a point of national pride. It was the first time that the discovery of an element was credited to a laboratory in Asia.

# Flerovium, moscovium and livermorium
@src IUPAC, 2012

Flerovium is named for the Flerov Laboratory of Nuclear Reactions in Dubna, Russia, which was named for the physicist Georgy Flerov. It was officially named in 2012. Scientists believe that it lies near an "island of stability," a predicted region where certain superheavy nuclei might live much longer than their neighbors, perhaps for minutes or even much longer.

Moscovium is named for the Moscow region, where Dubna is located. A team of Russian and American scientists first made it in 2003, by firing calcium-48 ions at americium. Livermorium is named for the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in California, which collaborated with the Russian scientists in Dubna to make it in 2000. Their atoms last a fraction of a second, so researchers can only make a few at a time and study how they decay. They were all named officially in 2012 and 2016.

# Tennessine and oganesson: closing the seventh row
@src IUPAC, 2016

Tennessine was first made in 2010 by a team of Russian and American scientists. They needed a rare target, berkelium, which was made at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee, and that's why it's named after the state. It sits in the halogen column of the periodic table, though it's not known how closely it behaves like the others.

Oganesson is the heaviest known element, and it completed the seventh row of the periodic table. It's named for the Russian nuclear physicist Yuri Oganessian, who led the team that made it. He was still alive when it was named in 2016, making him only the second person honored that way after Glenn Seaborg. Only a handful of its atoms have ever been made, and each one lasts for a fraction of a second. With the naming of these two elements in 2016, the table, for now, has no more gaps.
