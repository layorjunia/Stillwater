# Why you remember what frightened you
@src Cahill, Prins, Weber & McGaugh, Nature, 1994

Emotional events tend to stick in memory. In 1994 Larry Cahill and James McGaugh tested why. They showed people a short slide story. One version was neutral, about a boy and his mother visiting a hospital. The other version had the same slides, but with a more disturbing story that included a serious accident. Some of the people took propranolol beforehand, a drug that blocks the action of adrenaline, and others took a placebo.

A week later, the people on the placebo remembered the emotional version better than the neutral one. The propranolol group did not. They remembered the emotional story no better than the neutral one. The result suggested that adrenaline and the amygdala help to mark emotional events for storage. It's why you can recall the details of a scare. It's also why that kind of memory can be hard to forget.

# The gun that took the face
@src Loftus, Loftus & Messo, Law and Human Behavior, 1987

When a witness sees a weapon during a crime, what do they remember? In 1987 Elizabeth Loftus and her colleagues tested this. People watched a short scene in which a customer in a restaurant handed the cashier either a check or a gun. They tracked where the viewers' eyes went, and later asked them to identify the customer from a set of photographs.

People who saw the gun looked at it more, and they were worse at picking out the person's face. The weapon drew their attention, and left less to spend on anything else. It's called weapon focus, and it's one reason that eyewitnesses may describe a gun in great detail and yet get the person wrong. It shows that attention is limited, and that vivid things can hog it.

# Smelling roses while you sleep
@src Rasch, Büchel, Gais & Born, Science, 2007

In 2007 Björn Rasch and his colleagues asked people to learn the locations of pairs of cards in a memory game. While they learned, a rose scent was in the room. Later the people went to sleep in a lab. Some of them were given the same scent during the deepest stage of their sleep, and others got no scent, or got it only while awake.

When tested after sleep, the people who had smelled the rose scent in deep sleep remembered more of the card positions. The scent, they suggested, acted as a cue that reactivated the memory as the brain was storing it. The study was small, and the same trick has not always worked in later studies. But it's a striking sign that memories are processed during sleep, and that we can influence it.

# The cost of taking the photo
@src Henkel, Psychological Science, 2014
@conf mixed

Do you remember a place better if you photograph it? In 2014 Linda Henkel took students on a museum tour. She asked them to look at some objects, and to take pictures of others. The next day, she tested how much they remembered about the objects.

The students remembered fewer of the objects that they had photographed than those that they had only looked at, and they remembered fewer details about them. Henkel called it the photo-taking impairment effect. It seems that when we have a camera, we let it hold the memory for us. Later studies have found that the effect depends on how people take the photos, and that photos can also help people notice and enjoy more. The safest conclusion is that a photo is not the same as paying attention.

# Hands-free is not risk-free
@src Strayer & Johnston, Psychological Science, 2001

Many people believe that talking on a hands-free phone while driving is safe. In 2001 David Strayer and William Johnston tested it in a driving simulator. People did a tracking task and responded to red and green lights, as they would at a traffic signal. In some of the trials, they also had a conversation by phone, either handheld or hands-free.

When people were on the phone, they missed more red lights, and were slower to react to the ones they saw. Hands-free was no better than handheld. The problem isn't in the hands, it's in the mind. A conversation takes up attention, and the driver is looking at the road without seeing as much. Later studies confirmed this. It also applies to talking with a passenger, though a passenger can see the road and may stop talking when it gets difficult.

# Why sentries miss things
@src Mackworth, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1948

During the Second World War, radar operators had to stare at screens for hours, watching for a blip that meant an enemy plane. Some of them started to miss signals. In 1948 the psychologist Norman Mackworth built a test to find out why. He had people watch a clock with a single hand that moved in small steps, and press a key when it made a double step.

Over a two-hour session, the number of missed signals rose. The biggest drop in performance came in the first half hour. The people were not tired, or bored in a general way. They were failing to sustain attention on a task in which very little happened. It's called the vigilance decrement. It's a reason why jobs of watching, such as security screening, need frequent breaks, and rotation between tasks.

# Brief breaks keep you sharp
@src Ariga & Lleras, Cognition, 2011

If you work for a long time on one thing, your attention fades. The usual advice is to take a long break. In 2011 Atsunori Ariga and Alejandro Lleras tried something smaller. People did a boring computer task that lasted fifty minutes. Some did it straight through. Others were briefly interrupted twice, by a different task of memorizing digits, which lasted for under a minute.

The people who worked straight through got steadily worse over the fifty minutes. Those who had two short diversions kept their performance steady. The researchers suggested that the constant task made the goal fade, and that a break restored it. Short, rare breaks may work better than we think. It supports the practice of stepping away for a minute, and coming back to the same job.

# Heavy media multitaskers
@src Ophir, Nass & Wagner, PNAS, 2009
@conf mixed

In 2009 Eyal Ophir, Clifford Nass and Anthony Wagner at Stanford compared two groups of students. Heavy media multitaskers were those who often used several kinds of media at once, such as texting while watching video. Light multitaskers rarely did. Both groups took tests of attention and memory.

The heavy multitaskers did worse. They were more distracted by irrelevant information, and switched between tasks more slowly. The authors suggested that the habit of constant switching makes people worse at filtering. But later studies have had mixed results, and some could not repeat the findings. It's also possible that people who are easily distracted tend to multitask more. The lesson that doing many things at once is bad for you is sound. The cause is unclear.

# Why summers feel shorter as we age
@src Wittmann & Lehnhoff, Psychological Reports, 2005
@conf mixed

Ask an older person how fast time is going, and they'll often say that it flies. In 2005 Marc Wittmann and Sandra Lehnhoff asked people of different ages how fast the last week, month, year and decade had gone by. People of all ages felt that the past week, month and year had gone at about the same speed.

But for the last ten years, older people said that time had gone faster. So the feeling of speeding up seems to be about looking back on long stretches, and not about the days as we live them. One idea is that a life with fewer new experiences leaves fewer distinct memories, so the years seem to run together. If so, doing new things may slow the feeling. It's an idea, and it hasn't been shown directly.

# Imagining it makes it feel true
@src Garry, Manning, Loftus & Sherman, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 1996

In 1996 Maryanne Garry and her colleagues asked people how likely it was that certain events happened to them in childhood. The list included odd events, such as breaking a window with their hand. Later, they asked some of the people to imagine one of those events in detail. Then, they asked them to rate again how likely it was.

People who had imagined an event became more confident that it had really happened to them. The effect was strongest for events that were not very likely to begin with. The researchers called it imagination inflation. The more vividly we picture something, the more it can feel like a memory. It's one more reason why memory isn't a recording, and why detailed recall doesn't prove that something happened.

# The word that wasn't on the list
@src Deese, 1959; Roediger & McDermott, JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 1995

Read these words: bed, rest, awake, tired, dream, wake, snooze, blanket, doze, slumber, snore, nap, peace, yawn, drowsy. Now, without looking back, was the word sleep on the list? Most people say yes, and they feel sure. It wasn't. In 1995 Henry Roediger and Kathleen McDermott used lists like this one, built on an idea of James Deese.

When people tried to recall the words, roughly half of them recalled the missing word, such as sleep, and many were as confident about it as they were about words that really were there. All of the words on the list are related to one idea, and the mind fills it in. This is a false memory made from meaning. It shows that remembering is partly reconstructing, and that a feeling of certainty is not proof.

# Talking to other witnesses
@src Gabbert, Memon & Allan, Applied Cognitive Psychology, 2003

After a crime, witnesses often talk to each other. In 2003 Fiona Gabbert and her colleagues tested what that does. Pairs of people watched a video of the same crime, from different angles, so that each saw some things the other didn't. Some of the pairs then discussed what they had seen. Others did not. Afterward, everyone was tested alone.

Most of the witnesses who had discussed the event included things in their reports that they had only heard about from the other person, and had not seen. Some of them were certain that they'd seen those things. People are easily influenced by what others say, and then forget where they heard it. It's called memory conformity. It is why police try to interview witnesses separately and early, before their accounts blend together.
