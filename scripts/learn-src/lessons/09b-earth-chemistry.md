# Birds are dinosaurs
@src Xu et al., 2014

Birds are not just related to dinosaurs. They are dinosaurs, the living descendants of a group of feathered, two-legged dinosaurs called theropods. That group also included Tyrannosaurus and Velociraptor, so a chicken is a cousin of the most famous predators who ever lived.

Since the 1990s, spectacular fossils in China have shown many dinosaurs with feathers of various kinds, some of them with wings, and some of them too small to fly. In that sense, a sparrow is a small dinosaur, in the same way that you are a mammal. The dinosaurs that we think of as extinct died out 66 million years ago. The ones in your backyard feeder are the survivors, and there are more than ten thousand species of them.

# Blue whales
@src NOAA Fisheries

The blue whale is the largest animal that has ever lived, bigger than any dinosaur. It can grow up to 30 meters long, and weigh around 150 tons. Its blow, the spout of air it breathes out, can rise about 30 feet into the air.

It's astonishing that such an enormous animal feeds mainly on tiny shrimp-like creatures called krill, and it can eat up to about four tons of them a day during the feeding season. It gulps a huge mouthful of water, then pushes it out through plates of baleen, which trap the krill. Blue whales were hunted almost to extinction in the twentieth century, but protection has let some populations begin to recover.

# The dog's long history
@src Bergström et al., 2020
@conf mixed

Dogs were the first animal people domesticated, long before farming began. Genetic studies suggest that their ancestors split from wolves some time between 15,000 and 40,000 years ago, though experts debate exactly when. The date is hard to pin down, because the DNA of ancient animals is so scarce.

Dogs have evolved a talent that wolves, and even chimpanzees, lack: the ability to read human gestures such as pointing and to look to our faces for guidance. A puppy will follow where you point, even without training. It's a sign that dogs have been shaped by living with us for a very long time, and that in some ways we have shaped each other. They are, in a sense, a partnership between two species.

# Earth's layers
@src Lehmann, 1936

Earth has four main layers. There's the thin outer crust that we live on, a thick, slowly flowing mantle, a liquid outer core of molten iron and nickel, and a solid inner core. The crust is thinner, compared with the planet, than the skin of an apple.

The Danish seismologist Inge Lehmann discovered the inner core in 1936, by noticing that some earthquake waves bounced off something solid deep inside the planet. It's about as hot as the surface of the Sun, but it stays solid because of the immense pressure. No one has ever been near it, since the deepest hole ever drilled reaches only about 12 kilometers, and the center lies about 6,400 kilometers down. Everything we know comes from the way earthquake waves travel through.

# Continental drift
@src Wegener, 1915

In 1912 the German scientist Alfred Wegener proposed that the continents had once been joined together and have slowly moved apart. He pointed to the way South America and Africa fit together, and to matching fossils and rocks on both sides of the ocean.

Geologists mocked him because he couldn't explain what could move continents. He died in 1930 on an expedition across Greenland, long before he was proved right. In the 1950s and 60s, discoveries about the seafloor provided the answer, in the theory of plate tectonics. The continents ride on large plates that move at about the speed that fingernails grow. It's a good example of an idea that was rejected because of a missing mechanism, and accepted once the evidence was found.

# Earthquakes: the Ring of Fire and the magnitude scale
@src USGS

Around the edge of the Pacific Ocean lies a horseshoe-shaped zone of volcanoes and earthquake faults, 40,000 kilometers long, known as the Ring of Fire. About 90 percent of the world's earthquakes, and about 75 percent of its active volcanoes, occur there. The reason is that several tectonic plates meet along the Pacific's edge, where one plate slides under another.

The numbers used to describe earthquakes are logarithmic, so each whole step up means about 32 times more energy released. A magnitude 7 earthquake releases roughly 32 times as much energy as a magnitude 6, and about 1,000 times as much as a magnitude 5. The strongest earthquake ever recorded was a magnitude 9.5 in Chile in 1960. That's why a small difference in magnitude can be a very large difference in damage.

# Krakatoa and Tambora: volcanoes that changed weather
@src Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program

On August 27, 1883, the volcano Krakatoa in Indonesia exploded. The sound was heard on Rodrigues Island, 4,800 kilometers away, and is considered the loudest sound in recorded history. The giant tsunamis it generated killed more than 36,000 people. The ash and sulfur it pushed high into the atmosphere dimmed sunsets around the world and lowered global temperatures for several years.

Sixty-eight years earlier, in April 1815, Mount Tambora in Indonesia had the largest eruption in recorded history. It threw a huge cloud of ash and sulfur into the atmosphere, blocking some sunlight. In 1816, the following year, snow fell in New England in June and frosts came through the summer, and crops failed and food ran short across Europe. It was known as the "year without a summer." The weather of a whole planet can be changed by a single mountain.

# The rock cycle
@src Standard geology text

Rocks are constantly being recycled. Magma that cools becomes igneous rock. Wind and water wear rocks down into sediment, which gets pressed into layers of sedimentary rock. Heat and pressure transform either type into metamorphic rock.

Any of the three can be melted, buried, uplifted or worn down to become another, over millions of years, in a slow cycle with no beginning or end. Marble is limestone that has been cooked and squeezed, and sandstone is old beach sand. The rock under your feet has been through many lives. It's a useful way of seeing that the ground isn't permanent. It is a slow-moving river, and the Earth is always remaking it.

# Deep time
@src Hutton, 1788

In 1788 the Scottish farmer and geologist James Hutton studied rock layers at a place called Siccar Point and realized that they showed the same processes we see today, slow erosion and deposition and uplift, repeating over and over. Tilted layers of old rock were overlain by flat layers of younger rock, and it must have taken a very long time.

He wrote that he found "no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end." It made people realize that the Earth is vastly older than the thousands of years that had been assumed. His friend John Playfair, who went there with him, wrote that the mind seemed to grow giddy by looking so far into the abyss of time. We now know that the planet is about 4.5 billion years old, and Hutton's insight was the first step toward that number.

# Earth's history in one day
@src Standard geology text

Imagine all 4.5 billion years of Earth's history squeezed into 24 hours. The planet forms at midnight, and the first life appears around 4 a.m. For most of the day, nothing more than microbes lived here.

Dinosaurs arrive around 10:40 p.m., and they die out at about 11:40 p.m. Modern humans show up only in the last six seconds before midnight. Written history would be a flicker in the last fraction of a second. The comparison is useful because it puts our short time into perspective. Everything people have ever done, every war, every invention and every poem, happened in a sliver of the day, and the planet was very busy long before we came along.

# The Mohs scale
@src Mohs, 1812

In 1812 the German mineralogist Friedrich Mohs created a scale for the hardness of minerals, from 1 for talc, which you can scratch with a fingernail, to 10 for diamond. A harder mineral scratches a softer one, and that's the whole test.

Your fingernail is about 2.5, a steel knife about 5.5 and quartz is 7. The scale shows order only, since the steps aren't equal. Diamond is far harder than corundum, which is 9, a bigger gap than between all of the minerals below it. It's still a handy tool. A geologist in the field can identify many minerals with only a coin, a nail and a pocketknife, by seeing what scratches what.

# Earth's water
@src USGS

Oceans hold about 97 percent of Earth's water, and it's salty. Of the remaining 3 percent that's fresh, most is locked up in glaciers and ice sheets, so only about 1 percent of all the water is fresh water that's easy for us to reach.

Of the liquid fresh water, most is underground, and rivers and lakes hold only a tiny fraction. If all the water on Earth were gathered into a ball, it would be much smaller than you'd expect, about 1,400 kilometers across. Our planet looks blue from space, but the film of water on its surface is thin, and the part we can drink is thinner still. It's a good reason to value what we have.

# The ocean floor
@src Seabed 2030

We know more about the surface of the Moon than about our own seafloor. In 2023, the Seabed 2030 project, which is working to map all of it, reported that about a quarter of the ocean floor had been mapped to high resolution.

The deepest known point is Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench, nearly 11 kilometers down, deeper than Mount Everest is tall. Only a handful of people have ever been there. The sea floor isn't flat, either. It has mountain ranges, canyons and volcanoes, including the longest mountain chain on Earth, the mid-ocean ridge, which winds around the globe. It's a surprising fact that so much of our own planet is still unexplored.

# Layers of air
@src NASA; FAI

The atmosphere is made up of layers. The lowest, the troposphere, reaches about 12 kilometers up and holds most of the air and almost all weather. Commercial jets fly near its top, which is why the air outside is so cold, about minus 55 degrees Celsius.

Above it is the stratosphere, which holds the ozone layer that absorbs harmful ultraviolet light. Space has no sharp edge, but by convention, it starts at the Kármán line, 100 kilometers above the surface. If Earth were the size of an apple, the atmosphere that holds nearly all our air would be about as thin as the skin of that apple. We live at the bottom of a very thin layer, and everything we breathe is in it.

# The ozone hole
@src UNEP, 2022

In the 1980s scientists discovered a hole in the ozone layer over Antarctica, caused by chemicals called CFCs, used in aerosol sprays and refrigerators. Without the ozone layer, much more harmful ultraviolet light would reach the ground, which would raise the risk of skin cancer and damage crops.

In 1987 countries signed the Montreal Protocol, banning them. It worked. The UN reported in 2022 that the ozone layer is on track to recover to 1980 levels by about 2066 over Antarctica, much sooner elsewhere. It's one of the few cases in which the world acted together on an environmental threat and succeeded. The agreement is often cited as a model for what international cooperation can do, and a reason to believe that other global problems can be solved.

# The greenhouse effect
@src Tyndall, 1859; Arrhenius, 1896

Gases in the atmosphere, such as carbon dioxide, water vapor and methane, trap some of the heat that Earth radiates, keeping the planet warmer than it would otherwise be. Without any greenhouse effect, the average temperature would be about minus 18 degrees Celsius, and the oceans would be frozen.

John Tyndall measured it in the lab in 1859, and in 1896 Svante Arrhenius calculated how much extra carbon dioxide would warm the planet. The basic physics has been understood for well over a century. The effect itself is natural and necessary for life. The concern is about what happens when we add more of the gases, in the same way that a thicker blanket would make a bed warmer.

# Carbon dioxide in the air
@src NOAA; IPCC AR6

Before the Industrial Revolution, the concentration of carbon dioxide in the air was about 280 parts per million. Today it's over 420. That's a rise of more than 50 percent, in about two hundred years.

Ice cores, which trap ancient air in bubbles, show that for 800,000 years before industrialization, it stayed between about 180 and 300. Burning coal, oil and gas, and clearing forests, account for the recent rise. A tell-tale sign is the chemistry of the carbon itself, which shows that the extra carbon dioxide comes from fossil fuels. Careful measurements on a mountain in Hawaii have been tracking the climb since 1958.

# Warming so far
@src IPCC AR6, 2021

Earth's average surface temperature has risen by about 1.1 degrees Celsius since the late 1800s. The 2021 report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change states that it's unequivocal that human influence has warmed the planet.

Most of the increase has happened since about 1970, and the last decade has been the warmest on record. It may not sound like much, but the average temperature of the whole planet is hard to move. The difference between today and the last ice age, when ice covered much of North America, was only about five or six degrees. A change of one degree in the global average is a big shift, and it brings more heat waves and heavier rains in many places.

# Ice ages
@src Milanković, 1941; Hays et al., 1976

For the last couple of million years, Earth has swung between long ice ages and warmer interglacial periods. The Serbian scientist Milutin Milanković worked out that slow changes in Earth's orbit and tilt, in cycles of about 100,000, 41,000 and 23,000 years, alter how much sunlight reaches different latitudes and trigger the cycles.

The last glacial maximum was about 20,000 years ago. At that time, ice sheets covered Canada and northern Europe, and the sea was about 120 meters lower than it is today, so that people could walk from Asia to North America. A study of seafloor sediments in 1976 gave strong support to Milanković's idea. The ice ages show how sensitive the planet is to small changes in the sunlight it receives.

# Hurricane fuel
@src NOAA

Hurricanes need warm ocean water, at least about 26.5 degrees Celsius, as fuel. The warm water gives them heat and moisture. When a hurricane moves over land or cooler water, it loses its fuel and weakens quickly.

The Saffir-Simpson scale rates hurricanes from category 1 to 5 by their wind speed, with category 5 starting at 157 miles per hour. That's why the hurricane season peaks in late summer, when the sea is warmest. Hurricanes are called typhoons in the western Pacific and cyclones in the Indian Ocean, but they're the same kind of storm. In the Southern Hemisphere, they spin the other way, clockwise.

# Rainbows
@src Standard optics text

A rainbow forms when sunlight enters raindrops, bends, reflects off the back and bends again as it leaves, spreading into colors. The red light always exits at an angle of about 42 degrees from the direction directly opposite the Sun.

Every raindrop sends colored light in many directions, but you see only the drops at the right angle. So no two people see exactly the same rainbow, and you carry yours with you as you move. The rainbow is also a full circle, though the ground cuts it off. From an airplane, you can sometimes see the whole circle. It's a lovely fact that something so shared and so familiar is, in a way, a private view.

# Snowflakes
@src Libbrecht, 2005

A snowflake starts as a tiny ice crystal, and because of the shape of water molecules, it has six sides. As it falls, the shape it grows into depends on the temperature and humidity along the way, so each flake has its own history.

Wilson Bentley, a farmer in Vermont, began photographing them with a microscope camera in 1885, and took more than 5,000 images. The saying that no two are alike is probably true for the larger, more complicated ones, since there are so many possible paths. Simple, small ones can be identical. Each flake is a record of the weather it has passed through on its way down, written in ice.

# The Coriolis effect
@src Standard meteorology text

Because Earth spins, moving air and water curve to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern. This is the Coriolis effect, and it's why storms rotate counterclockwise in the north and clockwise in the south.

But it's far too weak to decide which way water swirls down a sink or toilet, which depends on the shape of the bowl and any currents in the water. The effect only matters over large distances and long times, such as a hurricane, a long-range artillery shell or the great ocean currents. The old story about drains in the two hemispheres is a popular myth, and you can happily ignore it.

# El Niño
@src NOAA

Every two to seven years, the surface of the central and eastern Pacific Ocean warms, trade winds weaken, and weather patterns shift around the world. This is called El Niño, the Spanish for "the boy," a name given by Peruvian fishermen who noticed the warm water around Christmas.

It can bring floods to Peru, droughts to Australia and Indonesia and milder winters in parts of North America. Its cooler opposite phase is called La Niña. The cycle is one of the largest natural patterns in the climate, and forecasters watch it closely, since it affects crops, fisheries and storms across many countries. It's a reminder that the weather in one place may depend on the temperature of water thousands of miles away.

# The pH scale
@src Sørensen, 1909

The pH scale measures how acidic or basic a liquid is, from 0 to 14, with 7 neutral, like pure water. The Danish chemist Søren Sørensen introduced it in 1909, while working at the Carlsberg brewery's laboratory, where the acidity of beer mattered.

Each step is a tenfold change, so lemon juice, at about pH 2, is 100,000 times more acidic than water. Lye is at the other end, about 13. Your blood is kept very close to 7.4, and even a small shift can be dangerous. The scale is logarithmic, so it's good to remember that a change of one unit is a big deal. A few numbers are enough to describe everything from stomach acid to household bleach.

# Why soap works
@src Standard chemistry text

A soap molecule has two ends: one that's attracted to water and one that's attracted to grease. When you wash, the grease-loving ends stick into oil and dirt, and the water-loving ends face outward, so that water can carry the oil away.

Soap can also break apart the fatty outer envelope of some viruses, including coronaviruses, which is why handwashing works so well. It doesn't matter whether the soap is antibacterial, since regular soap does the job, as long as you scrub for about twenty seconds. The soap and the friction pull germs off the skin, and the water rinses them down the drain. It's a simple chemical trick that has saved countless lives.

# Salt melts ice
@src Standard chemistry text

Dissolving salt in water lowers the temperature at which it freezes. Seawater freezes at about minus 2 degrees Celsius, and a saturated salt solution won't freeze until about minus 21. That's why roads are salted in winter, so that ice melts and doesn't form.

The same trick is used in old-fashioned ice cream makers, where salt added to ice makes the mixture colder than freezing, so the cream freezes. It stops working well when it gets extremely cold, below about minus 10 degrees Celsius, when other chemicals are used. Salt also damages roads, cars and streams, so some cities now use alternatives, such as sand or beet juice, to protect the environment.

# Avogadro's number
@src BIPM, 2019

Atoms are far too small to count one at a time, so chemists count them in huge groups called moles. A mole is 6.022 times 10 to the 23rd particles, known as Avogadro's number. It's about as many as the number of grains of sand on all the beaches of the Earth, many times over.

It's set up so that a mole of carbon-12 atoms weighs 12 grams. That lets chemists, by weighing, count atoms. A mole of water is 18 grams, about a tablespoon, and it holds that vast number of molecules. Since 2019, the number has been fixed as an exact value, and the mole is now defined by it. The idea is named for Amedeo Avogadro, an Italian scientist who proposed in 1811 that equal volumes of gases hold equal numbers of molecules.

# Conservation of mass
@src Lavoisier, 1789

In a chemical reaction, nothing is created or destroyed. In 1789 Antoine Lavoisier carefully weighed everything before and after burning things in sealed vessels and showed that the total mass didn't change.

When something burns, it gains the weight of the oxygen it combines with. A log seems to lose most of its weight when it burns, but that's because the gases escape into the air. If you could catch every bit of the smoke and the ash, the weight would be the same. His work ended the old theory that burning releases a substance called phlogiston. Lavoisier is often called the father of modern chemistry, though he lost his head to the guillotine in 1794.

# Boyle's law
@src Boyle, 1662

If you squeeze a gas into half the space, its pressure doubles, as long as the temperature stays the same. Robert Boyle worked this out in 1662, with air trapped in a bent glass tube and mercury. He poured mercury in the open end and watched the trapped air get smaller.

It explains why a bicycle pump heats up when you use it, and why your ears pop in an airplane when the pressure of the air around you changes. It's also how your lungs work. When your chest muscles expand your chest, the pressure inside falls, and air flows in. It's among the oldest laws of chemistry, and it applies to everything from a scuba tank to a syringe.

# Brownian motion
@src Brown, 1827; Einstein, 1905

In 1827 the botanist Robert Brown noticed that tiny grains of pollen in water jiggle around constantly. He couldn't explain it, and he checked that it wasn't due to the pollen being alive, since dust from rocks did the same.

In 1905 Albert Einstein showed that it happens because invisible water molecules are bumping into the grains from all sides. The French physicist Jean Perrin confirmed it with experiments in the following years, which also convinced skeptics that atoms and molecules really exist. Until then, some distinguished scientists had doubted that atoms were real. A simple jiggle seen through a microscope became strong evidence for the unseen world of molecules.

# The first plastic
@src Baekeland, 1907

In 1907 the Belgian-American chemist Leo Baekeland invented Bakelite, the first fully synthetic plastic, from phenol and formaldehyde. Earlier plastics were made from natural materials, such as cellulose. Bakelite was made from scratch.

Unlike earlier plastics, it didn't melt once it was set, so it could be used for things that got hot, like telephone housings, radios and electrical parts. It was nicknamed "the material of a thousand uses." Baekeland had already become rich from inventing a photographic paper, and he did the work in a laboratory he had built behind his house. The material started the age of plastics, and its descendants are now everywhere. Today we make hundreds of millions of tons of plastic a year, and disposing of it has become one of the great environmental problems of our time.

# Forever chemicals
@src US EPA; ATSDR

PFAS are a large family of man-made chemicals that were used for decades in non-stick pans, waterproof fabrics and firefighting foam. They're called "forever chemicals" because their carbon-fluorine bonds, among the strongest in chemistry, mean they don't break down, so they build up in the environment and our bodies.

High levels have been linked to health problems including some cancers and reduced immune response. They have been found in rain, in water supplies and in the blood of nearly everyone tested. Regulators have been setting limits for them in drinking water, and manufacturers have been phasing out the worst ones. Cleaning them up is difficult. It's a case of a chemical that was useful in the short term and unexpectedly troublesome in the long term.

# Catalysts
@src Standard chemistry text

A catalyst speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up itself. Enzymes are the catalysts of living things, making the reactions of life happen fast enough at body temperature. In a car's exhaust system, the catalytic converter uses platinum and related metals to turn harmful gases into less harmful ones.

Without catalysts, many reactions in your cells would be too slow to keep you alive. A single enzyme can do its job millions of times a second. Industry depends on them, too. The Haber-Bosch process, which makes fertilizer, uses an iron catalyst, and oil refineries rely on them all day long. A catalyst doesn't change what is possible. It only changes how fast it happens, and that can make all the difference.

# The fire triangle
@src NFPA

Fire needs three things: fuel, heat and oxygen. Take away any one, and it goes out. That simple idea is the basis of every way of fighting a fire, from the smallest to the largest.

Water cools a fire, removing heat. A fire blanket or lid cuts off the oxygen. A firebreak removes the fuel. Fire safety experts now often use a fire tetrahedron, which adds a fourth element, the chemical chain reaction that keeps a flame going. Knowing this, you can choose the right method. For a grease fire in a pan, for example, put a lid on it, and never throw water, which would spread the burning oil.

# Rust
@src Standard chemistry text

Rust is what happens when iron slowly reacts with oxygen and water to form iron oxide, which is flaky and weak. Unlike the thin oxide layer on aluminum, which protects the metal below, rust flakes off and exposes fresh metal to the air, so the damage keeps going.

Salt speeds up the process, which is why cars rust faster where roads are salted, and near the sea. Stainless steel resists rust because the chromium in it forms a thin, invisible protective layer. The damage from corrosion costs the world's economies trillions of dollars a year. Paint, oil and coatings of zinc all work by keeping water and air away from the iron, and a small scratch left alone can eventually become a big problem.
