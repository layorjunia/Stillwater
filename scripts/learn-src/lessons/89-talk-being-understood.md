# We think we're clearer than we are
@src Keysar & Henly, Psychological Science, 2002

When we say something, we know what we mean, and so it seems clear. In 2002 Boaz Keysar and Anne Henly tested how well speakers judge their own clarity. Speakers were given ambiguous sentences, such as "the man chased the woman on the bicycle," and a picture to show which meaning they intended. They read the sentences aloud to a partner, who had to pick the meaning.

The speakers estimated that their partners understood them correctly about seventy-two percent of the time. In fact, the partners picked the right meaning only about sixty-one percent of the time, not much better than a coin toss. The speakers couldn't help hearing the sentence in the way they meant. It's called the curse of knowledge. When you want to be understood, it's best to check, and not to assume that you were clear.

# How well do we read each other's minds?
@src Ickes, Stinson, Bissonnette & Garcia, JPSP, 1990; Ickes, Journal of Personality, 1993

In 1990 William Ickes and his colleagues asked pairs of strangers to sit together for six minutes in a room, and chat while they were filmed. Afterward, each person watched the tape alone, and wrote down what they had been thinking and feeling at certain points. Then each watched again, and tried to guess what their partner had been thinking at those moments.

Strangers were right about one fifth of the time, in their guesses about thoughts and feelings. Close friends did a little better, about a third. And the accuracy rose only slightly with the length of the friendship. Ickes called it empathic accuracy. It's a task at which most of us are mediocre, even with those we are close to.

# The trip to Abilene
@src Harvey, Organizational Dynamics, 1974

Jerry Harvey, a professor of management, told a story about his own family. One hot afternoon in Texas, in a town called Coleman, the family was playing dominoes on the porch. His father-in-law suggested that they drive to Abilene, fifty-three miles away, to have dinner at a cafeteria. Harvey thought it was a crazy idea, but said that it sounded fine. The others agreed.

The trip was in a car without air conditioning, in a dust storm, with a temperature of a hundred and four degrees, and the food was bad. Back at home, they discovered that none of them had wanted to go. Each had gone along, because they thought the others wanted to. Harvey called it the Abilene paradox, the situation in which a group does something that no member wants, since each is afraid to speak up. It is a failure of communication, and not of decision.

# The feeling of another's knowing
@src Smith & Clark, Journal of Memory and Language, 1993; Brennan & Williams, Journal of Memory and Language, 1995

When someone answers a question, we judge how sure they are, not just from the words, but from the way they speak. In 1993 Vicki Smith and Herbert Clark asked people trivia questions, such as, who was the first man on the Moon. They noted the answers, and how they were said, and then asked the people how sure they were.

People who were unsure took longer to answer, used fillers such as um and uh, and ended with a rising tone, as if asking. In 1995 Susan Brennan and Maurice Williams played recordings of the answers to other listeners, who were asked to judge how likely it was that the speaker knew the answer. They used the same cues. A listener can tell what a speaker's own memory is saying. It's a case in which metacognition is visible in speech.

# Doubtful experts can persuade
@src Karmarkar & Tormala, Journal of Consumer Research, 2010

We usually assume that a confident speaker is more convincing. In 2010 Uma Karmarkar and Zakary Tormala tested what happens when a speaker says that they are uncertain. People read reviews of a product. Some were written by an expert, and some by a nonexpert. In some, the reviewer said that they were certain, and in others, that they were not sure.

When the reviewer was a nonexpert, certainty was more persuasive. But when the reviewer was an expert, and showed uncertainty, readers paid more attention, and were more persuaded than when the expert was certain. They seemed to think that if an expert is unsure, it must be a hard question, and they worked harder to understand the reasons. Admitting doubt can be a strength, if you are known to know.

# The confidence heuristic
@src Price & Stone, Journal of Behavioral Decision Making, 2004

If you have to decide whom to believe, who do you pick? In 2004 Paul Price and Eric Stone ran a series of experiments in which people received advice from several sources. In one, students were asked to estimate the answers to factual questions, and each advisor gave a range.

The students picked the advisors who gave the narrowest ranges, which signaled the greatest confidence, even when they were told that the advisors were no more accurate. It's called the confidence heuristic. We take confidence as a sign of knowledge. In real life, it's often a reasonable rule, but it makes us vulnerable to people who sound sure and are wrong. It's a reason to ask what a person's track record is, and not just how they sound.

# Words or tone?
@src Ishii, Reyes & Kitayama, Psychological Science, 2003

When someone says thank you in a flat voice, or says it's fine with an irritated tone, which do you pay attention to, the meaning of the words, or the sound? In 2003 Keiko Ishii, José Reyes and Shinobu Kitayama tested Americans and Japanese. Participants heard words with a pleasant or unpleasant meaning, such as congratulations or disgusting, spoken in a pleasant or unpleasant tone. They had to say whether the word's meaning, or the tone, was pleasant.

The Americans found it easier to attend to the words, and to ignore the tone. The Japanese found it easier to attend to the tone, and were slowed down by it when it conflicted with the words. The researchers suggested that in cultures that depend more on context, people have learned to listen to how things are said. It's a reminder that misunderstandings can arise from a difference in what people attend to.
