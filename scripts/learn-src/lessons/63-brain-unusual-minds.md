# The rats that pressed the lever
@src Olds & Milner, Journal of Comparative and Physiological Psychology, 1954

In 1953 James Olds and Peter Milner at McGill University were implanting electrodes in the brains of rats. One electrode ended up in the wrong place, in a region near the base of the brain. When the rat was given a mild electrical pulse there, it came back to the same corner of its cage, as if it wanted more.

The researchers put the animals in a box with a lever that delivered the pulse. The rats pressed it again and again, thousands of times an hour, for many hours. Some ignored food, even when they were starving. It seemed to be a pleasure center. We now know that the system is more about wanting than enjoying, and that dopamine is central to it. But the experiment was the beginning of the study of the brain's reward circuits, and the biology of addiction.

# Rat Park
@src Alexander, Coambs & Hadaway, Psychopharmacology, 1978
@conf mixed

It was long said that addictive drugs are so strong that any animal that tastes them will keep taking them. The evidence came from experiments in which rats, kept alone in small cages, would drink water laced with morphine until they were dependent. In the 1970s the psychologist Bruce Alexander in Canada asked whether the cage had something to do with it.

He built Rat Park, a large enclosure with room to roam, tunnels, toys and other rats. In his experiments, rats in the park drank much less of the morphine water than rats in isolation. He argued that addiction depends on environment, and not just on the drug. Later attempts to repeat the work had mixed results, and the original study was criticized for its design. But the idea that isolation and stress make addiction more likely is supported by much research.

# How habits are stored
@src Jog, Kubota, Connolly, Hillegaart & Graybiel, Science, 1999

When you learn to do something, such as driving a route or tying a shoe, it starts out as a series of steps that you think about. With practice, it becomes automatic. In the 1990s Ann Graybiel and her team at MIT recorded from neurons in the basal ganglia of rats learning to run a maze. They had to turn left or right at a signal.

At first, many neurons fired throughout the run. As the rats learned, the pattern changed. Neurons fired mainly at the start and at the end of the sequence. The brain had bundled the steps into a single unit, or chunk, with a beginning and an end. It's why a habit can run by itself once it's triggered, and why it's so hard to stop in the middle. Habits are stored in a different system from the one used for deliberate choices.

# A life without pain
@src Cox et al., Nature, 2006

Pain is unpleasant, but it protects us. A few people are born unable to feel it. In 2006 James Cox and his colleagues studied three families in northern Pakistan in which some children could not feel pain. One boy had worked as a street performer, putting knives through his arms and walking on hot coals. He died at the age of fourteen after jumping off a roof.

The researchers found that the people had mutations in a gene called SCN9A, which makes a channel in nerve cells that carries pain signals. Without it, the nerves can't send the message. People with this condition can badly injure themselves without knowing, and often have broken bones and burns. The discovery has led drug companies to look for painkillers that block the channel, without the side effects of opioids.

# The artist who remembers a city
@src Wiltshire, Floating Cities, 1991; Treffert, 2009

Stephen Wiltshire was born in London in 1974. He was diagnosed with autism, and did not speak until he was five. At school, he was drawn to art, and he began to draw buildings. He has become known for an ability to draw detailed landscapes of cities from memory, after a short look.

In one famous feat, he took a helicopter ride over Rome, lasting about forty-five minutes. Over the next several days, he drew a panorama of the city on a sheet that was more than ten feet long, with its buildings in accurate detail. He has done the same for Tokyo, New York and other cities. He is called an autistic savant, a person with a disability who has an extraordinary skill. How he does it is not fully understood, though it seems to depend on very detailed visual memory.

# A memory that doesn't forget
@src Parker, Cahill & McGaugh, Neurocase, 2006

In 2000 a woman in California wrote to the memory researcher James McGaugh to say that she could remember almost every day of her life since about the age of fourteen. She could give the date, the day of the week, what she did and what was on the news. The scientists tested her, and checked her claims against diaries and records. She was correct.

The condition is now called highly superior autobiographical memory. About a hundred people have been identified. They are not better at memorizing lists or numbers, and their memory for the events of their own lives is exceptional. Some people say that it is a burden, since they cannot stop reliving painful moments. Studies have found some differences in the brain, in areas related to memory, though the cause is unknown.

# Minds without pictures
@src Zeman, Dewar & Della Sala, Cortex, 2015

Close your eyes and imagine a red apple on a table. Most people can see it, more or less clearly, in the mind's eye. Some people see it vividly, and others only vaguely. For a few people, there's nothing. The apple doesn't appear at all. They know what an apple looks like, and can describe it, but they cannot form the picture.

In 2015 Adam Zeman and his colleagues named this condition aphantasia, from the Greek for imagination. It affects a few percent of people. Many don't know that others can see images until they find out in adulthood. People with aphantasia can do well in school and in their careers, and many are good at math and writing. They also tend to have fewer vivid dreams. It's an example of how people can differ in ways that they have never noticed.

# The voice in the head
@src Hurlburt, Heavey & Kelsey, Consciousness and Cognition, 2013
@conf mixed

Many people assume that everyone thinks in words, with an inner voice. In fact, people differ. The psychologist Russell Hurlburt has spent decades studying the inner experience of people, by giving them a beeper that sounds at random times. When it beeps, they note exactly what they were experiencing at that moment, and then they're interviewed about it.

He found that some people have an inner voice much of the time, and others rarely. Some think mostly in images, or in feelings, or have no particular awareness of their thoughts at all, only an idea that they know. In one set of samples, inner speech was present in about a quarter of the moments, with huge differences between people. The method has been criticized, because people's reports can be inaccurate. But the main finding, that inner experience varies, is widely accepted.

# The patient who denies her paralysis
@src Babinski, 1914; Ramachandran & Blakeslee, Phantoms in the Brain, 1998

After some strokes, a patient may be paralyzed on one side of the body, and yet insist that nothing is wrong. Asked to move the left arm, they might say that they have done it, though the arm hasn't moved. Shown the arm lying still, they may say that it belongs to someone else, or explain that they don't feel like moving it. In 1914 the neurologist Joseph Babinski called this anosognosia, meaning lack of knowledge of illness.

It isn't a simple matter of denial. It usually follows damage to the right side of the brain, in regions that are involved in monitoring the body, and the person seems truly unaware. Ramachandran described patients who invented elaborate excuses. The condition shows that awareness of our own state depends on brain systems that can fail, without our knowing that they've failed.

# The impostor delusion
@src Capgras & Reboul-Lachaux, 1923; Ellis & Young, British Journal of Psychology, 1990

A person with Capgras syndrome believes that someone close to them, such as a spouse or a parent, has been replaced by an identical impostor. The patient sees the face and recognizes it, but feels that it isn't the real person. It was first described in 1923 by the French psychiatrist Joseph Capgras.

One explanation, popularized by V. S. Ramachandran, is that the pathway that links the visual recognition of a face to the emotional response is damaged. The patient sees the right face, but doesn't feel the familiar warmth. The brain tries to make sense of the mismatch, and comes up with the idea of an impostor. Patients often recognize the voice on the phone, since the auditory pathway to emotion is intact. It's a striking case of how recognition has both a thinking part and a feeling part.

# The belief that you are dead
@src Cotard, 1880; Berrios & Luque, Comprehensive Psychiatry, 1995

In 1880 the French neurologist Jules Cotard described a patient who said that she had no brain, no nerves, no chest and no intestines, and that she was only a decomposing body. He called the condition the delusion of negation. It's now known as Cotard syndrome, or walking corpse syndrome.

People with it may believe that they are dead, or that they don't exist, or that their organs have rotted away. It's most often seen in severe depression, and sometimes after brain injury. Some patients stop eating, since they think there is no point. Treatment can involve antidepressants, antipsychotic drugs and electroconvulsive therapy, and it's often effective. The syndrome is rare, and it shows how deeply our sense of being alive depends on how the brain makes us feel.

# The hand with a will of its own
@src Goldstein, 1908; Biran & Chatterjee, Archives of Neurology, 2004

In alien hand syndrome, a person's hand behaves as if it belongs to someone else. It may reach out and grab an object, or unbutton a shirt that the person has just buttoned, or in some cases, even grab the person's own throat. The person is aware that the movement is happening, but says that they are not controlling it.

It was first described in 1908, in a woman whose left hand acted on its own. It's often associated with damage to the corpus callosum, the bundle of fibers that connects the two halves of the brain, or to the frontal lobes. In the film Dr. Strangelove, a character struggles with a hand that makes a Nazi salute. The syndrome suggests that the feeling of controlling our actions is separate from the actions themselves.

# The woman who couldn't see motion
@src Zihl, von Cramon & Mai, Brain, 1983

In the early 1980s a woman, called L.M. in the medical report, suffered a stroke that damaged a part of the brain at the back and side. After this, she lost the ability to see movement. The world, for her, was a series of still pictures. She could not pour tea, because the liquid seemed to be frozen like a glacier, and she could not tell when to stop pouring, as the cup overflowed.

Crossing a street was dangerous. A car would seem to be far away, and then suddenly close, without having appeared to move. She found it hard to follow conversations, since she couldn't see lips moving. The condition is called akinetopsia. It was important evidence that the brain has a specialized area, now known as MT or V5, which processes motion, separately from the other parts of vision.

# The diving bell and the butterfly
@src Bauby, Le scaphandre et le papillon, 1997

In December 1995 Jean-Dominique Bauby, the editor of the French magazine Elle, had a stroke at the age of forty-three. When he woke up, he was almost completely paralyzed. He could not speak or move, apart from his left eyelid. His mind was unaffected. It's called locked-in syndrome.

With the help of a speech therapist, he was able to communicate by blinking, using a system in which a helper recited the alphabet, ordered by how often letters are used, and he blinked at the right letter. In this way, he dictated a book about his experience, The Diving Bell and the Butterfly. It's said to have taken some two hundred thousand blinks. The book was published in March 1997, and he died just three days later.

# Awareness without a sign
@src Owen et al., Science, 2006

After a severe brain injury, some patients are in a vegetative state. They open their eyes and cycle through sleep and waking, but seem to show no sign of awareness. In 2006 Adrian Owen and his colleagues tested a twenty-three-year-old woman who had been in this state for five months after a car accident.

Inside a brain scanner, they asked her to imagine playing tennis, and then to imagine walking through the rooms of her house. Her brain lit up in the same areas, in the same way, as in healthy volunteers doing the same tasks. Though she could not move or speak, she appeared to understand and to follow the instructions. The study showed that some patients may be conscious, and has led to new ways of testing for awareness.

# Moving a cursor with a thought
@src Hochberg et al., Nature, 2006

In 2004 a man named Matthew Nagle, who had been paralyzed from the neck down after being stabbed, took part in a pioneering experiment. Surgeons put a small array of ninety-six electrodes, about the size of a baby aspirin, into the part of his motor cortex that controls the hand. Wires connected it to a computer.

After training, Nagle could move a cursor on a screen just by thinking about moving his hand. He could open email, play a simple video game, and control a robotic arm. John Donoghue led the team. Since then, people with similar implants have controlled robotic limbs and typed on a screen. In 2023 teams reported that implants had enabled people who could not speak to communicate at speeds approaching natural conversation.

# Deep brain stimulation
@src Benabid et al., The Lancet, 1991

In 1987 the French neurosurgeon Alim-Louis Benabid was operating on a patient with a severe tremor. To find the right spot in the brain, he stimulated it with a small electrical current. To his surprise, the tremor stopped. He realized that continuous stimulation at a high frequency could do the work of destroying the tissue.

He began to implant electrodes connected to a pacemaker-like device under the skin of the chest. The method is called deep brain stimulation. It was approved in the United States for the tremor of Parkinson's disease in 1997 and for the disease itself in 2002. More than a hundred thousand people have received the implants. It's also being tested for depression, obsessive compulsive disorder and epilepsy. How it works is not fully understood.
