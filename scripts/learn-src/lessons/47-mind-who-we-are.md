# What personality predicts
@src Roberts, Kuncel, Shiner, Caspi & Goldberg, Perspectives on Psychological Science, 2007

Do personality traits matter for how a life turns out? In 2007 Brent Roberts and his colleagues gathered the results of many long-running studies. They compared how well personality traits, family income and social class, and cognitive ability predicted three outcomes: how long people lived, whether they stayed married, and how well they did in their work.

Personality was a good predictor. For some outcomes, it was as good as socioeconomic status and mental ability, and in some cases better. Conscientiousness, the tendency to be organized, careful and persistent, was linked to a longer life and to success at work. Emotional stability and agreeableness were linked to lasting marriages. The effects of any one trait are modest. But they add up over a lifetime, and they suggest that how we tend to act has consequences.

# Conscientious children live longer
@src Friedman et al., JPSP, 1993

In 1921 the psychologist Lewis Terman started following more than a thousand gifted California schoolchildren. He collected information on them for the rest of his life, and the study was continued after his death. In the 1990s Howard Friedman and his colleagues used the records to ask whether personality in childhood predicted how long people lived.

Children whom their parents and teachers had rated as conscientious, meaning prudent, dependable and persistent, lived longer on average, across many decades. Cheerfulness as a child did not predict a longer life, as many had expected. Conscientious people tend to smoke less, drink less and take better care of themselves. They also tend to have more stable careers and relationships. The finding suggests that habits formed early can last for a lifetime.

# Why siblings are so different
@src Plomin & Daniels, Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 1987

Children who grow up in the same house have the same parents, the same neighborhood and often the same schools. You'd expect them to be alike. In 1987 Robert Plomin and Denise Daniels pointed out a puzzle. Studies of personality showed that adopted children who grew up in the same family were no more alike than strangers, and ordinary siblings were only moderately alike.

The reason is that genes and environment both matter, and that the environment that makes a child who they are is not the shared one. It's the part not shared, such as different friends, different teachers, accidents, and the way parents treat each child differently. The family they share explains little. The finding was a surprise, and it has been confirmed many times. It suggests that parents have less control over how a child's personality turns out than we think, though they matter a great deal in other ways.

# The story you tell about your life
@src McAdams et al., Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2001; Adler, JPSP, 2012

Each of us carries a story about our life. It has chapters, turning points and a main character. The psychologist Dan McAdams called it a narrative identity. In 2001 he and his colleagues asked adults to describe the key scenes of their lives, and they looked at the sequences. In some stories a bad event led to something good, which they called redemption. In others, a good event turned sour, which they called contamination.

People who told redemption stories, in which they had learned or grown from a hardship, tended to have greater well-being. Those who told contamination stories tended to be more depressed. In 2012 Jonathan Adler followed people in therapy, and found that a stronger sense of being the one in charge of their own story came before improvements in their mental health. Our story isn't fixed. How we tell it can change, and the change seems to matter.

# Selves you might become
@src Markus & Nurius, American Psychologist, 1986

Who you are now isn't the whole of your sense of yourself. In 1986 Hazel Markus and Paula Nurius proposed the idea of possible selves. These are our pictures of what we might become, including the ones we hope for, such as a successful professional, and the ones we fear, such as a lonely person. They act as guides, and give direction to what we do.

A possible self is most useful when it is detailed, and when we have a plan for reaching it. A hoped-for self that has no steps behind it may do little, and a feared self with no path away from it can be paralyzing. People who go through big changes, such as a divorce or a career change, often have to build new possible selves. It's a helpful exercise to write one down, and to add what would be the first step.

# An astrology test
@src Carlson, Nature, 1985

Can astrologers match a person to their birth chart? In 1985 the physicist Shawn Carlson designed a careful test. He recruited astrologers who were chosen by their peers, and helped them agree to the terms of the test beforehand. Each astrologer was given a birth chart and three personality profiles, one of which belonged to the person whose chart it was, and was asked to choose the right one.

If astrology worked, they would have chosen correctly much more often than one time in three. They did not. They were right about a third of the time, no better than guessing. The same astrologers had said before the test that they expected to be right most of the time. It was a fair test, designed with their help. The finding illustrates how a belief can feel true for reasons that have little to do with accuracy.

# Plants for the residents
@src Langer & Rodin, JPSP, 1976; Rodin & Langer, JPSP, 1977
@conf mixed

In 1976 Ellen Langer and Judith Rodin tried a small experiment in a nursing home in Connecticut. On one floor, the staff told residents that they were responsible for themselves. They could decide how to arrange their rooms, what to do with their time, and which night to see a movie. Each was offered a plant to look after. On another floor, the staff told residents that the staff would take care of everything, and gave plants but watered them for the residents.

Within weeks, residents who had been given responsibility were more alert and more active. In a follow-up eighteen months later, fewer of them had died. The study was small, and later work has found that the effects are not always so strong. But it fits a large body of research. A sense of control over everyday choices matters for well-being, especially when we are older, and it's easy to take it away by being too helpful.

# How you see getting older
@src Levy, Slade, Kunkel & Kasl, JPSP, 2002

What you believe about getting older may affect how long you live. In 2002 Becca Levy and her colleagues followed about six hundred people over the age of fifty in Ohio, for up to twenty-three years. At the start, they asked people to say how much they agreed with statements about aging, such as "Things keep getting worse as I get older."

People with more positive views of aging, who saw it as a time of wisdom and growth, lived on average seven and a half years longer than those with negative views. This held after accounting for age, gender, health and loneliness. It is an observational finding, and it can't prove that the beliefs caused the longer lives. But later experiments have found that positive messages about aging improve physical function. It's one reason to notice the stereotypes about aging that you have taken in.

# Remembering what you value
@src Cohen & Sherman, Annual Review of Psychology, 2014
@conf mixed

When someone criticizes us, or we receive bad news, we often become defensive. In the 1980s Claude Steele proposed that this happens when something threatens our sense of being a good person. His idea was that reminding people of what they care about elsewhere in life could reduce the need to defend. This is called self-affirmation.

In studies, people who spent a few minutes writing about a value that was important to them, such as family or music, became more open to information that threatened their views. In some school studies, a short writing exercise about values improved grades for students under pressure, and the benefit lasted. Later efforts to repeat the school findings have been mixed. The overall idea fits many results. When you feel under attack, remembering what matters to you can make it easier to listen.

# Be a runner, or run?
@src Bryan, Walton, Rogers & Dweck, PNAS, 2011
@conf mixed

Does the way a request is worded change what we do? In 2011 Christopher Bryan and his colleagues asked people about voting in an election. For some, the questions used a noun, such as "How important is it to you to be a voter?" For others, they used a verb, such as "How important is it to you to vote?" The meaning is nearly the same.

Those who were asked about being a voter were more likely to turn out and vote, according to official records. The researchers suggested that a noun speaks to who you are, and it's harder to turn down. Later studies have found weaker effects, and some have not repeated the result. But the idea is worth trying. If you want a habit to stick, think of yourself as someone who does it, and not only as doing it.
