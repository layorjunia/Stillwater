# Mean, median and mode
@src Standard statistics text

These are three ways of saying "typical," and they can give very different answers. The mean is the usual average, found by adding everything up and dividing. The median is the middle value when you line them all up, and the mode is the most common one.

If Bill Gates walks into a bar, the average wealth of the people in it shoots up, but the median hardly moves. For things like income or house prices, where a few huge values pull the mean, the median is usually the more honest summary. When you read that the "average" family earns some amount, it's worth asking which average is meant. A different choice can paint a very different picture of how people are doing.

# Standard deviation
@src Pearson, 1893

The average on its own doesn't tell you how spread out the numbers are. The standard deviation does. Karl Pearson gave it its name in 1893, and it's one of the most widely used numbers in all of statistics.

Imagine two classes that both average 70 on a test. In one, the standard deviation is 5, so almost everyone scored between 60 and 80. In the other it's 20, so you have students at 30 and others at 100. The averages match, but the classes are very different places to teach. Whenever you see an average, it's worth wondering about the spread around it, since a number with a wide spread tells you much less about any one case.

# What a confidence interval means
@src Hoekstra et al., 2014

Studies often report a "95% confidence interval." It sounds like there's a 95 percent chance the true value is inside the range, but that's not quite what it means. It means that the method used would capture the true value in 95 percent of repeated studies.

The difference is subtle, and surveys have found that many researchers get it wrong, including trained scientists. A practical reading is that the interval shows the range of values the data can't rule out. A narrow interval means a precise estimate, and a wide one means a lot of uncertainty. When a result is reported as a single number with no interval, it's fair to ask how much it could vary.

# The margin of error
@src Standard polling reference

News stories about polls often mention a margin of error, usually about plus or minus 3 percentage points for a poll of around 1,000 people. It reflects only random chance in picking the sample.

It doesn't cover badly worded questions, or people who didn't respond, which can matter more. If a poll shows 52 percent to 48 percent with that margin, the two sides are statistically tied, even if the headline says someone's ahead. Note that the margin applies to each number separately, so the gap between two candidates has an even larger margin. The next time you see a close poll, it's good to remember that it can't tell you who is leading.

# The Literary Digest fiasco
@src Squire, 1988

In 1936 the magazine Literary Digest mailed out ten million ballots and got back more than 2.4 million, and predicted that Alf Landon would beat Franklin Roosevelt in a landslide. Roosevelt won in one of the biggest landslides in history, carrying all but two states.

The magazine had drawn its sample from its own readers and from telephone and car-owner lists, which in the Depression skewed richer. George Gallup, polling a much smaller but representative sample, got it right. The lesson is that how a sample is chosen matters far more than how big it is. A huge sample that's biased is worse than a small one that's fair, and no amount of data can fix a lopsided way of collecting it.

# Effect size
@src Cohen, 1988

A study can find that something "works" and still tell you nothing about how much. Effect size measures how large the difference is. Jacob Cohen suggested rough benchmarks for a standardized difference: 0.2 is small, 0.5 is medium and 0.8 is large.

A "statistically significant" result with a tiny effect may be of no practical use, particularly in large studies where even trivial differences can be detected. For example, a diet that makes people lose a few ounces over a year might be "significant" with enough participants, but nobody would care. So when you read about a finding, it's good to ask two questions. Is it real? And how big is it? The second question is the one that tells you whether it matters.

# Statistical power
@src Button et al., 2013

A study with too few participants may fail to detect a real effect. That's low statistical power. A 2013 review of neuroscience studies estimated that the typical study had a power of only about 21 percent, which means it would miss most real effects.

Worse, low power also means that the results that do cross the threshold of significance tend to exaggerate the true effect. A small study that finds a big result is more likely to be a fluke or an overstatement than a large study. It's one reason why many findings from small studies don't hold up when they're repeated. If a surprising result comes from a few dozen people, it's wise to wait for a bigger one.

# How to make music make you younger
@src Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn, 2011

In 2011 Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson and Uri Simonsohn published a paper claiming that listening to the Beatles song "When I'm Sixty-Four" made people nearly a year and a half younger. It was absurd, and it was done on purpose.

It was a deliberate demonstration of how researchers, by making flexible choices about which data to include and which tests to run, can find "significant" results in nonsense. They then proposed rules to stop that kind of p-hacking, such as stating in advance how many participants you will use. The paper was a landmark in the movement to make research more reliable. It showed that a result can be statistically significant and still be completely meaningless.

# The dead salmon
@src Bennett et al., 2009

In 2009 the neuroscientist Craig Bennett put a dead Atlantic salmon in an fMRI brain scanner, showed it photographs of people in social situations, and asked it to determine their emotions. Without correcting for the thousands of statistical tests involved, the analysis found brain activity in the dead fish.

It was a joke with a serious point. If you run enough tests, some will come out "significant" by chance alone, and you have to correct for multiple comparisons. The study won an Ig Nobel Prize in 2012, an award for research that makes you laugh and then makes you think. Since then, brain-imaging researchers have taken the issue much more seriously. It's a good story to remember whenever someone says they found a link after testing many things.

# Pre-registration
@src Scheel, Schijen & Lakens, 2021

In pre-registration, researchers publicly record their hypotheses and methods before collecting data. That stops them from quietly changing the question after they see which results turn out well. It's like calling your shot before taking it.

A 2021 comparison of studies found that 44 percent of those published in a "registered report" format found a positive result, compared with 96 percent of standard published papers. It suggests that the usual literature is skewed toward positive findings, since negative results rarely get published. It's an important change, and one that makes it easier to trust the results. A finding that was predicted in advance is much more convincing than one that was noticed afterward.

# The meta-analysis
@src Glass, 1976

A meta-analysis pools the results of many studies on a question to give a clearer overall picture. The psychologist Gene Glass coined the term in 1976, when he combined hundreds of studies of psychotherapy to see whether it worked.

A larger body of evidence gives more precise answers than any single study. But it can only be as good as the studies it includes, and if negative results were never published, even the pooled answer can be biased. Good meta-analyses look for this problem, and they judge the quality of the studies they include. When you hear that "one study found" something, it's worth asking whether there's a meta-analysis, since a single study can mislead.

# Relative versus absolute risk
@src Gigerenzer, Calculated Risks (2002)

A headline says a drug "doubles your risk," which sounds alarming. But if the risk was tiny to begin with, doubling it may mean very little. A very small number doubled is still a very small number.

In 1995 the UK government warned that a new type of contraceptive pill doubled the risk of blood clots. The actual risk went from about 1 in 7,000 women to about 2 in 7,000. Many women stopped taking the pill, and there was a rise in unwanted pregnancies and abortions. The harm from the scare was larger than the harm from the pill. It's a good reason to ask, whenever you see a percentage change in risk, "From what to what?"

# Number needed to treat
@src Laupacis, Sackett & Roberts, 1988

One helpful way to express how well a treatment works is the number of people who need to take it for one person to benefit. If a drug cuts a risk from 2 percent to 1 percent, you could call that a 50 percent reduction. It sounds impressive.

But 100 people have to take it for one to be helped, and the others get the costs and side effects. The number makes the trade-offs clearer. A treatment with a number needed to treat of 5 is much more valuable than one with 500. It doesn't tell you whether to take a drug, but it gives you a fair way to weigh the benefit against the downsides. It's a question worth asking your doctor.

# Sensitivity and specificity
@src Standard medical statistics text

Any medical test can make two kinds of error. Sensitivity is how good it is at finding people who do have the condition, and specificity is how good it is at correctly clearing people who don't. A test can do very well on one and badly on the other.

A very sensitive test catches nearly everyone who's sick but may also flag many healthy people, which is why a positive result often needs a follow-up. When a condition is rare, most positive results can even be false alarms, even with an accurate test. That's an important reason why doctors don't screen everyone for everything. A positive result isn't the end of the story, and it's often worth asking, "How sure is this test?"

# Lead-time bias
@src Standard epidemiology text

Screening tests find diseases earlier, and the apparent survival time after diagnosis gets longer. But that can be an illusion. Suppose a cancer is found at age 63 without screening and the patient dies at 70, which is 7 years of survival.

With screening, it's found at 60, and the patient still dies at 70, so survival after diagnosis is 10 years, but the person didn't live any longer. All that has happened is that the clock started sooner. The fairer test of screening is whether fewer people die of the disease. That's why researchers look at deaths in the whole population, and not only at survival after diagnosis, when they judge whether a screening program helps.

# Always plot your data: Anscombe and the Datasaurus
@src Anscombe, 1973; Matejka & Fitzmaurice, 2017

In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe made up four small sets of numbers that have almost identical averages, spreads and correlations. Yet when you plot them, they look completely different: one is a neat line, one is a curve, one has a single outlier pulling the line, and one is a vertical stack of points. His point was that you should always graph your data, because summary numbers can hide what's really going on.

Decades later, the idea got a modern twist. In 2016 the data visualizer Alberto Cairo made up a data set that, when plotted, looks like a dinosaur, yet has the same averages, spread and correlation as a random scatter of dots. In 2017 researchers Justin Matejka and George Fitzmaurice built on it, showing a dozen other shapes, including a star and a bullseye, that all share the same summary statistics.

# Truncated graphs
@src Tufte, The Visual Display of Quantitative Information (1983)

A bar chart is supposed to show quantities in proportion to the height of the bars. If the vertical axis starts at 95 percent instead of zero, a difference between 97 and 99 can look like the second bar is several times as tall.

It's a common way to make small differences look dramatic, sometimes by accident. Always check where the axis starts. Edward Tufte called misleading graphs a violation of graphical integrity. For line charts, it's often acceptable not to start at zero, since the trend matters more than the size, but for bars it's a distortion. A quick glance at the numbers on the side of a chart can save you from being misled.

# The law of truly large numbers
@src Diaconis & Mosteller, 1989

When there are enough chances, even extremely unlikely things are bound to happen. The statisticians Persi Diaconis and Frederick Mosteller pointed out that if an event has a one-in-a-million chance on a given day, then with hundreds of millions of people in America, it should happen to hundreds of people every day.

So coincidences aren't strange. They're expected, when you consider how many opportunities there are. A person who dreams of a friend and gets a call the next morning feels something uncanny, but among millions of dreamers, it will happen to many. That doesn't make it any less memorable for the person it happens to. It just means that you can't take a rare event as proof of anything, if it could have happened to any of a million people.

# The Prussian cavalry
@src Bortkiewicz, 1898

In 1898 the Russian-Polish statistician Ladislaus Bortkiewicz looked at records of how many Prussian cavalry soldiers were killed by horse kicks each year, in 14 army corps over 20 years. The numbers were small, and each death seemed like a random accident.

He showed that the numbers fit a mathematical pattern known as the Poisson distribution, which describes rare events that occur independently at a steady average rate. It's a classic example of how even odd accidents follow statistical laws. The same pattern describes the number of calls arriving at a switchboard, the number of typos on a page and the number of meteorites that land in a field. Randomness, it turns out, has a shape.

# The lady tasting tea
@src Fisher, 1935

In the 1920s, at an English country research station, a woman claimed that she could tell whether the tea or the milk had been poured into a cup first. The statistician Ronald Fisher turned this into a famous experiment: eight cups, four of each kind, presented in random order.

If she's just guessing, the chance of getting all eight right is 1 in 70. The example became a model for randomization and significance testing. It shows the logic of a good experiment. Decide in advance what would count as evidence, take away every chance of bias by randomizing, and then ask how likely the result would be by luck alone. According to the story, she got all of them right.

# A/B testing
@src Bowman, 2009

When a website wants to know which version of a page works better, it can show version A to some visitors and version B to others, chosen at random, and compare the results. It's the same logic as a medical trial.

It lets companies settle arguments with data instead of opinions. In 2009 Google reportedly tested 41 shades of blue for a link bar, and a designer left the company partly because he felt that such testing was replacing design judgment. The case shows both sides. Testing can find what works, and it can also push toward small, safe changes. It's a powerful tool, but it only answers the questions you ask, and some of the best ideas are the ones nobody thinks of testing.

# Lottery odds
@src Multi-State Lottery Association

The chance of winning the Powerball jackpot is about 1 in 292 million. With three drawings a week, if you bought a ticket for every single drawing, you'd expect to wait nearly two million years to win once.

On average, a ticket is worth less than what you pay for it. People still play, and most of the pleasure is in the daydream. That's fair enough, as long as you think of it as the price of an entertainment and not as an investment. It helps to remember that you're far more likely to be struck by lightning than to win. If you do play, small amounts you can afford to lose are the sensible way.

# The Kelly criterion
@src Kelly, 1956

If you're offered a bet that's in your favor, how much of your money should you put on it? John Kelly, a physicist at Bell Labs, published a formula in 1956 for the answer. It tells you the fraction of your money to wager, based on your edge and the odds.

Bet too much, and a bad run can wipe you out, even though you have an edge. Bet too little, and you waste the edge. Many professional gamblers and investors use half the Kelly amount, to be safer, since the formula assumes you know your odds exactly, and in real life you don't. The idea is a good reminder that, in a favorable game, the most important thing is to stay in the game long enough for the edge to work.
