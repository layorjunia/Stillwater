# The house that orbits Earth
@src NASA, International Space Station records

The International Space Station is the largest object ever put into space. Its first module was launched in 1998, and since November 2000, it has been continuously occupied, with astronauts from many countries taking turns. It's about the size of a football field, and orbits about four hundred kilometers above the Earth, at around twenty-eight thousand kilometers an hour.

At that speed, it circles the planet about sixteen times a day. The astronauts see sixteen sunrises and sixteen sunsets every twenty-four hours. They live in free fall, so they float, and they must exercise for about two hours a day to keep their muscles and bones from weakening. The station was built by five space agencies, from the United States, Russia, Europe, Japan and Canada. It's often cited as an example of international cooperation.

# Robots on Mars
@src NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory, Mars Exploration Program

The first wheeled vehicle to drive on Mars was Sojourner, a rover about the size of a microwave oven, which landed in 1997. It traveled about a hundred meters. In 2004 two bigger rovers, Spirit and Opportunity, landed on opposite sides of the planet. They were designed to last ninety days. Opportunity kept going for nearly fourteen years, until a dust storm silenced it in 2018.

Curiosity, a rover the size of a car, landed in 2012, and found that Mars once had conditions that could have supported microbes. Perseverance arrived in 2021 with a small helicopter, Ingenuity. On April 19, 2021, Ingenuity made the first powered flight on another planet, lasting about forty seconds. It went on to make dozens more. The rovers have shown that Mars was once warmer and wetter.

# The hell of Venus
@src Soviet Venera program; Marov & Grinspoon, The Planet Venus, 1998

Venus is nearly the same size as Earth, and for a time, people imagined that it might be a tropical world. The truth is closer to hell. Its thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide traps heat in a runaway greenhouse effect, and the surface is hot enough to melt lead, about four hundred and sixty-five degrees Celsius. The air pressure is about ninety times that on Earth. The clouds are made of sulfuric acid.

On December 15, 1970, the Soviet probe Venera 7 became the first spacecraft to land on another planet and send back data. It survived on the surface for about twenty-three minutes. Later Venera landers took photographs of the rocky ground. None lasted for more than about two hours. Venus is a warning of what can happen to a planet's climate. It's also being studied for clues to the fate of Earth.

# Laika
@src Soviet Academy of Sciences; Malashenkov, 2002

On November 3, 1957, a month after Sputnik, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 2. It carried a dog, a small stray from the streets of Moscow, named Laika, which means barker. She was the first animal to orbit the Earth. The aim was to show that a living creature could survive in space, before sending a human.

The Soviets said at first that she had lived for several days. In 2002, it was revealed that she died within hours, from overheating and stress. There was no plan for her return. The mission raised protests from animal lovers around the world. Later animal passengers, such as dogs Belka and Strelka in 1960, did return safely. A monument to Laika was put up in Moscow in 2008.

# The first woman in space
@src Soviet space program records; Sharpe, Valentina Tereshkova, 2013

Valentina Tereshkova was born in 1937 in a village in Russia. She worked in a textile factory, and was a keen amateur parachutist. In 1962 she was selected, with four other women, from several hundred applicants to train as a cosmonaut. Her parachuting skill was valued, since cosmonauts landed by ejecting from their capsule and parachuting down.

On June 16, 1963, she launched in Vostok 6, and spent almost three days in space, orbiting the Earth forty-eight times. She became the first woman in space. The next woman did not fly until 1982, nearly twenty years later. The first American woman, Sally Ride, flew in 1983. Tereshkova later entered politics, and she remains a national figure in Russia. She has said that she would still like to fly to Mars.

# Stuck outside
@src Leonov, Two Sides of the Moon, 2004

On March 18, 1965, the Soviet cosmonaut Alexei Leonov left his spacecraft, Voskhod 2, and floated into space on a tether. It was the first spacewalk in history. For about twelve minutes, he drifted over the Earth, and he was amazed by the view. Then he found he had a problem. In the vacuum, his spacesuit had inflated like a balloon, and he couldn't bend his arms and legs to get back into the airlock.

He had to open a valve and let out some of the air in the suit, which was dangerous, since it might lead to a condition like the bends. He finally got in, head first, instead of feet first as planned. Then the spacecraft landed far off course, in a snowy forest in the Urals. The crew spent a night there, among wolves, before being rescued. Details were kept secret for years.

# Earthrise
@src NASA, Apollo 8, December 24, 1968; Chaikin, A Man on the Moon, 1994

In December 1968 Apollo 8 became the first crewed spacecraft to leave Earth orbit, and the first to go around the Moon. Frank Borman, Jim Lovell and Bill Anders were looking at the gray, barren lunar surface, when, on their fourth orbit, the Earth rose above the horizon. Anders grabbed a camera and took a photograph in color.

The picture, known as Earthrise, shows a blue and white globe, small and fragile, above a stark landscape. It's one of the most famous photographs ever taken. The nature photographer Galen Rowell called it the most influential environmental photograph. It helped to inspire the first Earth Day in 1970. On Christmas Eve, the astronauts read from the Book of Genesis in a broadcast watched by perhaps a billion people.

# The overview effect
@src White, The Overview Effect, 1987

Many astronauts have described a deep change in perspective, on seeing the Earth from space. The Earth looks tiny, without borders, and very fragile. The atmosphere, which seems so vast from the ground, is a thin blue line. Many have said that they felt a sense of awe, and a greater connection to all humanity. The astronaut Edgar Mitchell, of Apollo 14, called it an instant global consciousness.

In 1987 the writer Frank White gave this experience a name, the overview effect, after interviewing astronauts. Studies of astronauts' accounts suggest that many have come back with a stronger concern for the environment. Some researchers are looking for ways of producing something like it on Earth, using virtual reality, or the experience of awe. It's a reminder of how our view of the world depends on where we stand.

# The deep field
@src Williams et al., Astronomical Journal, 1996

In December 1995 the astronomer Robert Williams, who was then the director of the Space Telescope Science Institute, made an unusual decision. He used a part of his own time on the Hubble Space Telescope for a risky experiment. He pointed the telescope at a tiny, dark and apparently empty patch of sky, near the Big Dipper. It was about the size of a grain of sand held at arm's length.

For ten days, the telescope took hundreds of exposures of this patch. When they were combined, the image revealed about three thousand galaxies, of many shapes and sizes, some of them among the most distant ever seen. In what looked like nothing, there was a universe. A later Hubble Ultra Deep Field showed about ten thousand. They imply that there are hundreds of billions of galaxies in the observable universe.

# Meeting Pluto
@src Stern et al., Science, 2015

New Horizons was launched in January 2006. It was the fastest spacecraft ever launched at that time, and it took more than nine years to reach Pluto, which is about three billion miles from the Sun. On July 14, 2015, it flew past Pluto at about thirty thousand miles an hour, and took the first close-up pictures. The signals took four and a half hours to reach Earth.

Pluto turned out to be a surprising world. It has mountains of water ice as high as the Rocky Mountains, and a large, smooth, heart-shaped plain of nitrogen ice, named Tombaugh Regio, after the discoverer of Pluto. There are signs of glaciers, and a haze of blue in the thin atmosphere. A world that was thought to be a dead ball of ice was geologically active. New Horizons has since continued out into the Kuiper Belt.

# Landing on Titan
@src ESA, Huygens probe; Lorenz & Mitton, Titan Unveiled, 2008

Titan, the largest moon of Saturn, is the only moon in the solar system with a thick atmosphere. It's hidden by an orange haze, and for centuries, no one knew what lay below. In 1997 the Cassini spacecraft was launched toward Saturn, carrying a small probe, Huygens, built by the European Space Agency. After a seven-year journey, Huygens separated, and on January 14, 2005, it parachuted into Titan's atmosphere.

It took about two and a half hours to fall, and then it landed on a surface of damp, sandy ground, with pebbles of ice. It sent data for about ninety minutes. The pictures showed channels cut by liquid, and a shoreline. Titan has lakes and rivers of liquid methane and ethane, with a rainfall of methane. It is the only place other than Earth with liquid on its surface. It's the farthest landing ever made.

# Landing on a comet
@src ESA, Rosetta mission, 2014; Glassmeier et al., Science, 2015

In 2004 the European Space Agency launched Rosetta, a spacecraft that was to catch up with a comet. It took ten years, and four flybys of planets, to get to Comet 67P, also called Churyumov-Gerasimenko, which is about four kilometers across and shaped like a rubber duck. In November 2014, Rosetta released a small lander, Philae, to the surface.

The landing didn't go as planned. Harpoons that were supposed to anchor the lander failed, and it bounced twice, and came to rest on its side in the shadow of a cliff, where its solar panels got little light. It worked for about sixty hours on battery power, and sent back data, including the first sniff of organic molecules on a comet. In 2015 it woke up briefly. Rosetta was deliberately crashed onto the comet in 2016.

# Seven worlds around a small star
@src Gillon et al., Nature, 2017

In February 2017 astronomers announced that a small, cool star, TRAPPIST-1, about forty light-years away, has seven planets about the size of Earth. The star is much smaller and dimmer than the Sun, about the size of Jupiter. The planets orbit it very closely, all closer than Mercury is to the Sun. The year on the nearest takes only a day and a half.

Three of the planets lie in the habitable zone, where water could be liquid. They are so close together that, from the surface of one, the others would look as big as the Moon does to us. It's not known whether any has an atmosphere, or water. The James Webb Space Telescope is being used to look for them. TRAPPIST-1 is an example of how common small planets are around the commonest kind of star.

# A message on a golden record
@src Sagan et al., Murmurs of Earth, 1978

When the two Voyager spacecraft were launched in 1977, each carried a gold-plated copper disk, designed to be played like a record. A committee headed by Carl Sagan chose its contents. It was meant to give a picture of life on Earth to any extraterrestrial finder. The disk holds one hundred and fifteen images, greetings in fifty-five languages, sounds of nature, and ninety minutes of music.

The sounds include the songs of whales, thunder, a kiss and the sound of a mother's first words to her baby. The music includes Bach, Beethoven, a Peruvian panpipe song, and Chuck Berry's Johnny B. Goode. The record also had instructions for how to play it, and a map showing the position of the Sun, using pulsars. It may last for a billion years. Sagan said that it was a bottle in the cosmic ocean.

# A message to the stars
@src Drake, Arecibo message, 1974

On November 16, 1974, the astronomers Frank Drake and Carl Sagan used the huge radio telescope at Arecibo, in Puerto Rico, to send a message into space. It lasted less than three minutes. It was a string of 1,679 binary digits, zeros and ones, which can be arranged in a grid of 73 rows by 23 columns, to make a picture.

The picture showed the numbers one to ten, the atomic numbers of the elements that make up DNA, a diagram of a human figure, the Solar System, and the telescope itself. It was aimed at a cluster of stars, called M13, about twenty-five thousand light-years away. It will take that long to arrive, so we can expect no reply for fifty thousand years. It was mainly a demonstration of the telescope's power. Some scientists have worried about the wisdom of announcing ourselves.

# The Wow! signal
@src Ehman, Ohio State University Big Ear, 1977

On August 15, 1977, a radio telescope at Ohio State University, called Big Ear, was scanning the sky for signals from intelligent life. A volunteer, Jerry Ehman, was looking over the printouts of the data a few days later. He saw a series of letters and numbers, 6EQUJ5, which showed a signal that was far stronger than the background. He circled it, and wrote Wow! in the margin.

The signal lasted for seventy-two seconds, the time it took for the telescope to scan across that spot in the sky, and it came from the direction of the constellation Sagittarius. It was at a frequency of the hydrogen line, which some had suggested for communication. It was never heard again, despite many searches. Its origin is unknown. Possible explanations include a comet, or a natural source. It's the best candidate that we have for a message from space, and no one can say what it was.

# Space junk
@src NASA Orbital Debris Program Office; Kessler & Cour-Palais, Journal of Geophysical Research, 1978

Since Sputnik in 1957, thousands of satellites have been launched. Many are no longer working. They've joined spent rocket stages and fragments from explosions and collisions, in orbit around the Earth. Tens of thousands of objects larger than a softball are tracked by radar, and there are millions of smaller pieces, such as flecks of paint. At orbital speed, even a speck can damage a spacecraft.

In 1978 Donald Kessler, a NASA scientist, warned of a danger. If there are enough objects in orbit, collisions will produce more debris, which would cause more collisions, in a chain reaction. This is called the Kessler syndrome. A collision between two satellites in 2009 created thousands of fragments. The International Space Station has made emergency maneuvers to avoid debris. Cleaning up is hard, and is being studied.

# Hitting an asteroid
@src NASA DART mission; Thomas et al., Nature, 2023

Large asteroids have hit the Earth before, and will again. The question is whether we can do anything about it. In September 2022 NASA tested one method. The Double Asteroid Redirection Test, DART, sent a spacecraft the size of a vending machine to crash into a small asteroid called Dimorphos. Dimorphos is a moonlet, about the size of a stadium, that orbits a larger asteroid, Didymos. Neither poses a threat to Earth.

The impact was on September 26, 2022, at about fourteen thousand miles an hour. It was watched by telescopes on Earth. Afterward, astronomers found that Dimorphos's orbit around its partner had been shortened by about thirty-two minutes, much more than the minimum for success. It was the first time that humans had changed the motion of a celestial body. It showed that, with enough warning, we could nudge an asteroid away.
