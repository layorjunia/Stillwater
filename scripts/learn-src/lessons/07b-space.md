# Pulsars
@src Hewish et al., 1968

In 1967 a graduate student at Cambridge, Jocelyn Bell, noticed a strange signal in her radio telescope data. It was a pulse repeating every 1.337 seconds, as regular as a clock. Nothing natural was known to behave that way, and the team half-jokingly named it LGM-1, for "little green men."

It turned out to be a rapidly spinning neutron star, sweeping a beam of radio waves past Earth like a lighthouse. Her supervisor, Antony Hewish, shared the 1974 Nobel Prize for the discovery. She was not included, and many people have thought that this was unfair. Bell Burnell has handled it with grace, and she later donated a large prize of her own to help students from groups that are underrepresented in physics.

# Supernova 1987A
@src NASA

On February 23, 1987, astronomers saw a star explode in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy next to ours, about 168,000 light-years away. It was the closest supernova in about four centuries, and it could be seen without a telescope in the southern sky.

A few hours before the light arrived, detectors deep underground in Japan and the US caught a burst of neutrinos, ghostly particles produced in the explosion. The neutrinos had left the dying star's core before the shock wave had broken through to the surface. The two signals confirmed how such stars die, and the observation helped begin a new kind of astronomy, which looks at the universe by catching its particles.

# Gold from neutron star mergers
@src Abbott et al., 2017

In August 2017, detectors picked up gravitational waves from two neutron stars spiraling into each other 130 million light-years away. Telescopes around the world turned toward the spot and watched the glow that followed. It was the first time that a collision had been seen in both gravitational waves and light.

The glow showed signs of heavy elements being created, including gold and platinum. It helped answer a long-standing question about where the heaviest elements come from. Calculations suggest that one such merger can make heavy elements amounting to many times the mass of the Earth. If you're wearing a gold ring, its atoms were probably forged in the collision of two dead stars, long before the Sun was born.

# Mercury
@src NASA

The closest planet to the Sun is also the smallest, a little bigger than our Moon. A year there lasts just 88 days. With almost no atmosphere to hold in heat, its temperature swings wildly, from about 430 degrees Celsius in the day to about minus 180 at night.

Even so, scientists have found water ice in permanently shadowed craters near its poles, where sunlight never reaches. It's a surprise to find ice on the planet nearest to the Sun, and it shows how much a bit of shade can matter. Mercury also moves faster than any other planet, at about 47 kilometers per second, which is why the Romans named it after the swift messenger of the gods.

# Venus
@src NASA

Venus is a strange world. It spins backward and so slowly that a day, which lasts 243 Earth days, is longer than its year of 225 days. If you could stand on its surface, the Sun would rise in the west.

Its thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide traps heat in a runaway greenhouse effect, making it the hottest planet at about 465 degrees Celsius, hotter than Mercury, even though it's farther from the Sun. The pressure at the surface is about 90 times Earth's, similar to being almost a kilometer under the sea. The Soviet probes that landed there in the 1970s lasted only an hour or two before they were crushed and cooked. Venus is sometimes called Earth's twin because of its size, but it's a very unfriendly twin.

# Earth in motion
@src NASA

You're sitting still, but you're traveling. Earth orbits the Sun at about 30 kilometers per second, nearly 70,000 miles per hour. At the equator, the planet's spin carries you eastward at about 1,670 kilometers per hour.

You don't feel any of it, because the speed is smooth and steady and everything around you is moving along with you. We notice only changes in motion, such as speeding up, slowing down or turning, and not the motion itself. That's the same reason you can't tell that you're moving at all when you are on a smooth flight. Galileo made the argument four centuries ago, when people asked why we don't feel the Earth move. The answer is that nothing about being at rest is any different from moving steadily.

# Mars
@src NASA

Mars looks red because its surface is rich in iron oxide, which is rust. It has the biggest volcano known in the solar system, Olympus Mons, about 22 kilometers high, roughly two and a half times the height of Mount Everest.

Billions of years ago, Mars had rivers and lakes of liquid water, and rovers have found signs of old streambeds and lake sediments. Today it's cold and dry, with a thin atmosphere that can't hold much heat. The planet lost most of its air over time, perhaps because it's smaller than Earth and has no global magnetic field to protect it. Whether it ever had life is one of the biggest open questions in science, and rovers are collecting samples to bring back and study.

# Jupiter
@src NASA

Jupiter is by far the largest planet, with enough volume to hold more than 1,300 Earths. It's made mostly of hydrogen and helium, with no solid surface to land on. If you tried to descend, the atmosphere would just get denser and hotter.

Its Great Red Spot is a giant storm, wider than Earth, that has been observed for at least 150 years, though it has been shrinking in recent decades. Its strong gravity has probably shaped the solar system, and it has pulled in comets and asteroids. In 1994, pieces of the comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 crashed into it, and left dark scars that were visible from Earth. It spins faster than any other planet, completing a rotation in under ten hours.

# Saturn
@src NASA

Saturn is the least dense planet in the solar system, with an average density lower than water. In theory, it would float if you could find a bathtub big enough. It's a ball of gas, mostly hydrogen and helium, with a small, dense core.

Its famous rings are made mostly of water ice, in pieces from dust to house-size boulders, and they spread out over hundreds of thousands of kilometers, yet in places they're only about ten meters thick. If the rings were scaled down to the width of a football field, they'd be only a few millimeters thick. They may also be young, only a few hundred million years old, though scientists don't agree on that.

# Uranus
@src NASA

Uranus is tilted about 98 degrees, so it spins almost on its side, like a rolling ball. As a result, each pole gets 42 years of constant sunlight, followed by 42 years of darkness. It's the only planet whose seasons are that extreme.

William Herschel found it in 1781, the first planet discovered with a telescope. At first, he thought it was a comet. A giant collision early in the planet's history may have knocked it over, though no one is sure. Only one spacecraft, Voyager 2 in 1986, has ever visited it, so there's a great deal that we still don't know. It's a pale blue-green world, and the color comes from methane in its atmosphere.

# Neptune
@src Le Verrier, 1846

Neptune was found with mathematics before anyone saw it. After Uranus was discovered, astronomers noticed that its orbit wasn't quite as predicted, as if something were tugging on it. In 1846 Urbain Le Verrier in France, and John Couch Adams in England, each calculated where an unseen planet must be.

Johann Galle in Berlin pointed his telescope at the spot Le Verrier gave and found it that very night. It was a triumph for Newton's laws, since a planet had been found with pencil and paper. Neptune has the fastest winds in the solar system, over 2,000 kilometers per hour, even though it receives very little sunlight. It takes 165 years to go around the Sun, and it completed its first orbit since discovery in 2011.

# Pluto
@src IAU, 2006; NASA New Horizons

Pluto was called the ninth planet from its discovery in 1930 until 2006, when the International Astronomical Union voted to define a planet as something that has cleared the neighborhood around its orbit. Pluto hasn't, so it became a dwarf planet.

Many people were upset, and the debate still comes up. But the new definition arose because astronomers were finding other icy worlds out there, some almost as big as Pluto. In July 2015 NASA's New Horizons spacecraft flew by it and showed a surprisingly active world, including a large heart-shaped plain of frozen nitrogen. Pluto turned out to be more interesting than anyone had expected, whatever we call it. The name was suggested by an eleven-year-old girl in England.

# The Moon's origin
@src Hartmann & Davis, 1975

The leading explanation, developed in the 1970s, is that about 4.5 billion years ago a planet-sized body, which has been called Theia, struck the young Earth. The debris from the collision orbited the planet and gradually came together as the Moon.

Moon rocks brought back by Apollo astronauts have a chemistry very close to Earth's outer layers, which fits the idea. It's called the giant-impact hypothesis. It also explains why the Moon is so large compared with its planet, and why it has such a small iron core. Not every detail is settled, and the details are still being worked out, but most scientists consider it the best account that we have.

# Tides
@src NOAA

The Moon's gravity pulls more strongly on the side of Earth nearest it and less on the far side. That stretches the oceans into two bulges, one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side, and as Earth turns, coastlines pass through both, giving most places two high tides a day.

The Sun adds its own pull, about half as strong as the Moon's. When Sun and Moon line up, at new and full moon, the tides are higher, called spring tides, and the name has nothing to do with the season. When they pull at right angles, at the quarter moons, the tides are weaker. In the Bay of Fundy in Canada, the difference between high and low tide can exceed fifty feet.

# The Moon is drifting away
@src Apollo Lunar Laser Ranging

The Moon is moving away from Earth at about 3.8 centimeters a year, roughly the speed at which your fingernails grow. Scientists know this because Apollo astronauts left mirrors on the surface, and lasers fired from Earth bounce off them, letting us measure the distance to within a few millimeters.

As it recedes, Earth's rotation slows slightly, so our days get gradually longer. Long ago, a day was much shorter than it is now. Fossil corals show that 400 million years ago, the year had about 400 days. The effect is tiny from one year to the next, but it has added up over billions of years. It's a quiet reminder that the Earth and Moon are a connected system.

# Why we only see one side of the Moon
@src NASA

The Moon spins exactly once for every trip it makes around Earth, so the same face always points toward us. It's called tidal locking, and it happened because Earth's gravity slowly slowed the Moon's spin until the two matched.

It's not a "dark side," though. The far side gets as much sunlight as the near side, and so it goes through the same cycle of day and night. It was first photographed by a Soviet spacecraft in 1959, and humans first saw it with their own eyes when Apollo 8 flew around the Moon in 1968. The two sides are surprisingly different, with the far side having far fewer of the large dark plains.

# Total solar eclipses
@src NASA

By a remarkable coincidence, the Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times farther away, so they look nearly the same size in our sky. When the Moon passes exactly in front of the Sun, it can cover the disk completely and reveal the Sun's faint outer atmosphere, the corona.

During the brief darkness, the temperature drops, birds go quiet and the stars come out. Because the Moon is slowly drifting away, in about 600 million years it will be too small to cover the Sun fully, and total eclipses will end. We happen to live at the one time when they're possible. Ancient peoples feared them, and some used the records of eclipses to date historical events.

# Io
@src Morabito et al., 1979

Jupiter's moon Io is the most volcanically active place in the solar system, with hundreds of volcanoes. The reason is tidal heating. Jupiter and its other big moons tug on Io, squeezing and flexing it constantly, which heats the interior.

Its volcanoes spray sulfurous material hundreds of kilometers into space, and the surface is painted in yellows, oranges and reds. Voyager 1 spotted the first plumes in 1979, and an image analyst, Linda Morabito, noticed them. She saw a strange crescent at the edge of the moon in a picture that had been taken to help navigate the spacecraft. It was the first time that active volcanoes had been found anywhere beyond Earth.

# Europa
@src NASA

Europa, a moon of Jupiter, has a surface of ice, and under it is an ocean of salty liquid water, kept warm by tidal flexing. It may contain twice as much water as all of Earth's oceans, even though the moon is smaller than our own.

Because liquid water is one ingredient of life as we know it, scientists are very interested. The ice shell may be many kilometers thick, so any life would be hidden from view. NASA launched the Europa Clipper spacecraft in 2024 to fly past it dozens of times and study whether it could be habitable. It isn't built to search for life directly. It's meant to find out whether the conditions are right.

# Titan
@src ESA/NASA, Cassini-Huygens

Titan, Saturn's largest moon, is the only moon with a thick atmosphere, and the only other place in the solar system where we've seen stable liquid on the surface. But it's not water. It's lakes and rivers of liquid methane and ethane, at about minus 180 degrees Celsius.

In 2005 the Huygens probe landed there, and sent back pictures of a landscape that looks eerily like a shoreline, with rounded pebbles that are probably made of ice. It rains methane, and the weather creates channels and dunes. It's a world that works like Earth, but with different materials. NASA plans to send a helicopter called Dragonfly to explore it in the 2030s.

# Enceladus
@src NASA Cassini

Enceladus is a small, icy moon of Saturn, only about 500 kilometers across. In 2005 the Cassini spacecraft discovered jets of water vapor and ice particles shooting from cracks near its south pole.

Later flybys found salt, organic molecules and hydrogen in the spray, which suggests that there's a liquid ocean beneath the ice and active chemistry. Hydrogen could be a source of energy for life, in the same way as it is around some vents on Earth's sea floor. Enceladus is among the most promising places to look for life beyond Earth. Cassini even flew through the plumes, so scientists could taste the ocean without landing.

# The asteroid belt
@src NASA

In movies, the belt between Mars and Jupiter is crowded with rocks, and ships weave between them. In reality it's mostly empty space. There are millions of asteroids, but they're spread so far apart that spacecraft have passed through without any close calls.

The total mass of the belt is less than that of our Moon, and about a third of it is in the largest body, the dwarf planet Ceres. The belt is the leftover material of a planet that never formed, probably because Jupiter's gravity stirred it up. Most asteroids are small, rough chunks of rock or metal, and a few have been visited by spacecraft, some of which have returned samples to Earth.

# The dinosaur-killing impact
@src Alvarez et al., 1980

About 66 million years ago, an asteroid roughly 10 kilometers wide struck what is now the Yucatán Peninsula in Mexico. In 1980 the father-and-son team of Luis and Walter Alvarez noticed a thin layer of clay around the world rich in iridium, a metal common in asteroids, and suggested that it came from an impact.

In the 1990s, the buried Chicxulub crater was identified as the site. About three-quarters of species died, including all the large dinosaurs, though birds, which descend from small dinosaurs, survived. The impact threw dust and soot into the sky, and the world was cold and dark for a long time. It's a vivid example of a scientific idea that was doubted at first and then confirmed by new evidence.

# Halley's comet
@src Halley, 1705

Halley's comet comes back about every 76 years. It was last seen in 1986, and it's due again in 2061. For most of history, comets were seen as unpredictable omens that appeared from nowhere.

In 1705 Edmond Halley noticed that comets seen in 1531, 1607 and 1682 had similar orbits, and he used Newton's new laws to predict that it would return in 1758. It did, after his death, and it was the first comet shown to travel in a regular orbit. It was a major triumph for Newtonian physics. A person who is lucky enough to live a long life may see it twice, as Mark Twain famously did, being born and dying in the years when it was visible.

# Voyager 1
@src NASA

Launched in 1977 to study the outer planets, Voyager 1 is now the most distant human-made object, and it entered interstellar space in 2012. It's still sending data from over 24 billion kilometers away, on only a couple of hundred watts of power.

Attached to its side is a gold-plated record containing greetings in 55 languages, music and sounds from Earth, in case anyone ever finds it. The spacecraft's power is slowly fading, and engineers have been turning off instruments one by one to make it last. Its signal takes more than 22 hours to reach us. Even so, it will keep drifting through the galaxy long after the Sun has changed.

# The Pale Blue Dot
@src NASA; Sagan, Pale Blue Dot (1994)

In 1990, as Voyager 1 was leaving the solar system, Carl Sagan asked NASA to turn its camera back for one last look. In the resulting picture, from about 6 billion kilometers away, Earth is less than a single pixel, a tiny speck in a ray of sunlight.

Sagan wrote of it that everyone you love, everyone you know, and everyone who ever lived, lived their lives on that dot. It's become a symbol of our place in the universe, small and shared. Some people find it humbling, and others find it comforting, since our troubles look so small from there. It's worth a few minutes to look at the picture and think about it.

# The first humans on the Moon
@src NASA Apollo 11

On July 20, 1969, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed the lunar module Eagle on the Moon, while Michael Collins stayed in orbit above in the command module. The landing site was nearly missed, and Armstrong had to fly manually past some boulders with little fuel to spare.

They spent about two and a half hours walking on the surface, collecting samples and setting up experiments, and brought back 21.5 kilograms of lunar rock and soil. About 600 million people watched on television. Twelve people have walked on the Moon in all, the last in 1972. It was one of the most difficult engineering feats ever undertaken, and it was done with computers less powerful than a modern calculator.

# Sputnik and Gagarin
@src NASA History

On October 4, 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, a polished metal ball that beeped as it orbited Earth. It was the first artificial satellite and it started the Space Race.

Less than four years later, on April 12, 1961, Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space, and completed one orbit in 108 minutes. Many people in the US were shocked, and it spurred major investment in science education. Within a few years, the country had created NASA's Apollo program and a generation of new engineers. The race had its dark side, since the same rockets could carry weapons, but its effects on science and technology were large.

# The Hubble Space Telescope
@src NASA

Launched in 1990, Hubble orbits above the atmosphere, which blurs the view from the ground. Its first mirror was ground to the wrong shape, giving blurry images, until astronauts installed corrective optics in 1993.

After that it transformed astronomy. It helped measure how fast the universe is expanding, and its "Deep Field" images revealed thousands of galaxies in a patch of sky the size of a grain of sand held at arm's length. Each of those galaxies has billions of stars. Hubble has now been operating for more than thirty years, in large part because astronauts could visit it to repair and upgrade it five times.

# The James Webb Space Telescope
@src NASA

The James Webb telescope launched on Christmas Day 2021 and began science work in 2022. It's an infrared telescope with a mirror 6.5 meters across, made of 18 gold-coated segments, and it sits about 1.5 million kilometers from Earth.

A sunshield the size of a tennis court keeps it cold enough to detect faint heat. Because infrared passes through dust, it can see galaxies that formed in the first few hundred million years after the Big Bang, and it can study the atmospheres of planets around other stars. Its mirror folded up like origami to fit in the rocket, and then unfolded in space. It all had to work on the first try, and it did.

# Exoplanets
@src NASA Exoplanet Archive

Until 1995, we didn't know whether any other stars had planets. That year Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz announced a planet orbiting the Sun-like star 51 Pegasi, a gas giant that circles its star in just four days. They shared the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Since then, astronomers have confirmed more than 5,000 exoplanets, and it appears that most stars have planets. Many are very unlike anything in our own solar system, such as giant planets that orbit closer than Mercury does, and worlds somewhere between the sizes of Earth and Neptune. It's an astonishing change in thirty years. A question that was only speculation is now a catalog of thousands of worlds.

# The habitable zone
@src Kasting et al., 1993

The habitable zone is the range of distances from a star where a planet could have liquid water on its surface, not too hot and not too cold. It's sometimes called the "Goldilocks zone."

Being in it doesn't guarantee life or even water. Venus and Mars both lie close to the edges of the Sun's zone, and their very different atmospheres have made them hostile to life as we know it. The zone depends on the star, too. Around a dim red star, it's much closer in. It's a useful guide when deciding where to point our telescopes, but it's a starting point and not an answer.

# The Drake equation
@src Drake, 1961
@conf mixed

In 1961 the astronomer Frank Drake wrote a formula to organize the question of how many civilizations in our galaxy might be able to communicate with us. It multiplies factors like the rate of star formation, the fraction of stars with planets, the fraction of those where life arises, and how long civilizations last.

We know a few of the numbers and have to guess the rest, so answers range from "we're alone" to "millions." It's not a prediction so much as a way of seeing what we'd need to know. As we learn more, for example that planets are common, some of the factors become clearer. The most uncertain are still the ones about life itself, and how long a civilization lasts.

# The Fermi paradox
@src Hart, 1975
@conf mixed

Around 1950 the physicist Enrico Fermi was having lunch with colleagues, discussing extraterrestrial life, when he asked, "Where is everybody?" If the galaxy is so old and so large, and life is common, why haven't we seen any sign of it?

Suggested answers include that life is rare, that civilizations destroy themselves, or that we haven't looked in the right way. No one knows which, if any, is right. It's a puzzle, not a proof, since the premises are uncertain. Still, it's a good prompt for thought. Either we're surprisingly early, surprisingly rare or surprisingly careless about looking, and each of those would be interesting.

# Earth's age
@src Patterson, 1956

In 1956 the geochemist Clair Patterson measured the ratios of lead isotopes in a meteorite and calculated that the Earth is about 4.55 billion years old. His reasoning was that meteorites formed at the same time as the planets.

Later methods have refined the number slightly, to 4.54 billion, and it has held up. Patterson went on to discover that leaded gasoline was spreading lead around the planet, and he campaigned successfully to end its use. His careful work in a clean laboratory led him to find that lead was everywhere, and the discovery changed public health. A question about the age of the planet led to a safer world.
