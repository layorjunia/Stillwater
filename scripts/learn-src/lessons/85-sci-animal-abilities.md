# The fastest land animal
@src Wilson et al., Nature, 2013

The cheetah is the fastest land animal. It can accelerate from a standstill to sixty miles an hour in about three seconds, faster than most sports cars. Speeds of about sixty miles an hour or more have been recorded, though it can't keep them up for more than a few hundred meters. In 2013 Alan Wilson and his colleagues at the Royal Veterinary College fitted wild cheetahs in Botswana with collars that had GPS and motion sensors.

They found that the cheetahs rarely ran at top speed in hunts. They used their agility, and the ability to slow down and turn sharply, more than their top speed. A cheetah has a long, flexible spine, large nostrils, and claws that act like cleats. But it's not an endurance runner. After a chase, it needs to rest, and often loses its kill to lions or hyenas.

# Faster than a race car
@src Tucker, Journal of Experimental Biology, 1998; Ponitz et al., PLOS ONE, 2014

The fastest animal on Earth is not a runner. It's the peregrine falcon, which dives on its prey in a steep plunge called a stoop. Its speed in a dive has been measured at more than two hundred miles an hour. In 1998 Vance Tucker trained a falcon to dive from a height, with a small recorder on its back, and he measured speeds of about two hundred miles an hour.

The falcon has features that allow it to survive the dive. Small cones in its nostrils help it to breathe at high speeds, by controlling the flow of air. It has a third eyelid, a membrane, that clears its eyes. It strikes the prey, usually a bird, with its feet, and often kills it on contact. Peregrines were nearly wiped out in the mid-twentieth century by the pesticide DDT. They have since recovered.

# A giraffe's seven bones
@src Standard vertebrate anatomy

A giraffe's neck can be about six feet long, and it has just seven vertebrae, the same number as in the neck of a human. Almost all mammals have seven neck vertebrae, from the mouse to the blue whale. The exceptions are the sloths, which have between six and ten, and the manatees, which have six. Each of the giraffe's vertebrae is greatly elongated, and may be ten inches long.

Why so constant? One idea is that a change in the number of vertebrae could disrupt the genes that control the development of the nerves and the diaphragm. Mutations that cause an extra rib in the neck are linked to a higher risk of cancer in children, and such changes are often lethal. Evolution has been conservative. The giraffe has made a long neck out of the same parts that we have.

# The squid axon that explained the nerve
@src Hodgkin & Huxley, Journal of Physiology, 1952

In the 1930s the British biologist John Zachary Young found that squids have a very large nerve fiber, called a giant axon, which is up to a millimeter across, a hundred times wider than a human one. It controls the squid's escape response. It was large enough that an electrode could be placed inside it.

In the late 1940s Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley, at Cambridge, used it to study how nerves send signals. They worked out the electrical changes, and found that the signal, the action potential, is caused by the flow of sodium and potassium ions through the nerve membrane. They wrote equations to describe it in 1952. These explained the basis of all nerve signaling, in the human brain as well as in squids. They shared the Nobel Prize in 1963.

# The shrimp that snaps like a gun
@src Versluis et al., Science, 2000

The pistol shrimp is a small animal, only a few centimeters long, which lives in shallow seas. It has one claw that is much bigger than the other. It can snap it shut so quickly that it shoots out a jet of water at nearly a hundred kilometers an hour. The jet makes a bubble, which collapses with a bang. It can stun or kill small fish.

In 2000 Michel Versluis and his colleagues studied the effect. The collapsing bubble produces a sound of about two hundred decibels, louder than a gunshot, and a flash of light, and the gas inside briefly reaches a temperature of thousands of degrees. This is called cavitation. A group of pistol shrimp snapping can be so loud that it interferes with sonar. Naval submarines have used the noise as cover.

# The beetle that sprays boiling liquid
@src Eisner & Aneshansley, PNAS, 1999; Arndt et al., Science, 2015

The bombardier beetle defends itself with a chemical weapon. When threatened, it squirts a jet of a hot, caustic liquid from the tip of its abdomen, and it can aim it, over a wide angle. The liquid is boiling, at about one hundred degrees Celsius. It can kill a small insect, and it irritates the mouth of a predator.

The beetle makes it with two chemicals, hydroquinones and hydrogen peroxide, which it stores separately in a reservoir. When it's attacked, it squeezes them into a chamber with enzymes, where they react explosively. It pulses the spray, up to about five hundred times a second. In 2015 researchers used high speed X-ray imaging to look inside living beetles as they sprayed.

# A living battery
@src Catania, Science, 2014

The electric eel, which lives in the rivers of South America, is not an eel but a kind of knifefish. It can produce shocks of up to about six hundred volts, strong enough to stun a horse. About eighty percent of its body is made up of electric organs, which consist of thousands of cells stacked like the cells in a battery. It uses weak pulses to sense its surroundings, like radar.

In 2014 Kenneth Catania at Vanderbilt University found something surprising. When an eel attacks a fish that is out of the water, it leaps up, and presses its chin on the prey, and sends the current through the air, along the body of the prey, and back into the water. The shock goes through the prey, as if it were a wire. It's an unusual use of electricity, and it's an example of how clever predators can be.

# Steering by the Milky Way
@src Dacke et al., Current Biology, 2013

Dung beetles roll balls of dung away from the pile in a straight line, so that they will not be robbed by others. They can do this at night. In 2013 Marie Dacke and her colleagues in South Africa and Sweden wondered how they find their way when there's no Sun and no Moon. They put little cardboard hats on some of the beetles, so that they couldn't see the sky.

The beetles with hats wandered in circles. Those without them went straight. The beetles seemed to be using the stars. When the researchers took the beetles to a planetarium, and showed them a sky with only the Milky Way, they still rolled their balls in a straight line. It was the first evidence that an insect uses the Milky Way for navigation. It's a feat that is done by a brain no bigger than a grain of rice.

# Diving without breathing
@src Le Boeuf et al., Journal of Zoology, 1988; Robinson et al., PLOS ONE, 2012

The northern elephant seal spends most of its life at sea, and much of it underwater. Researchers fitted tags on seals from California to track their dives. The seals dived to depths of about five hundred meters on average, and the deepest dives went to more than fifteen hundred meters, nearly a mile. A dive lasted for twenty minutes or more, with just a few minutes at the surface between dives.

Some of the dives lasted for over an hour. The seal can do this because its blood holds a large amount of oxygen, and its muscles are full of a protein, myoglobin, which stores oxygen. Its heart slows down, and blood is sent mainly to the brain and heart. During a trip of several months, the seals spend ninety percent of the time underwater. They even seem to sleep while diving, drifting down in a spiral.

# The penguins who endure the winter
@src Williams, Penguins, 1995; Gilbert et al., Biology Letters, 2006

The emperor penguin is the only animal that breeds during the Antarctic winter. In March, the penguins walk up to sixty miles inland, to a place far from the sea. The female lays a single egg, and passes it to the male, who balances it on his feet under a fold of skin. She then walks back to the sea to feed.

The males stand in the dark, with temperatures down to minus forty degrees Celsius and winds of a hundred miles an hour. They go without food for about two months, and lose up to half of their body weight. To survive, they huddle together in a large group, and take turns moving from the cold edge to the warm center. When the females return, with food, the chicks hatch, and the males go to the sea to feed.

# The bird that circles the world
@src Jouventin & Weimerskirch, Nature, 1990

The wandering albatross has the longest wingspan of any bird, up to eleven feet. It spends most of its life at sea, and comes to land only to breed. It travels huge distances. In 1990 Pierre Jouventin and Henri Weimerskirch began to track albatrosses with satellite tags, and later work with GPS tags has shown that they can circle the Southern Ocean in a few weeks, in one case in about forty-six days.

The albatross uses almost no energy to stay in the air. It uses a technique called dynamic soaring, in which it takes advantage of the difference in wind speed between the surface of the sea and the air a few meters above. It glides on stiff wings, and can go for hours without a wingbeat. Its heart rate when flying is only slightly above the rate at rest. Many albatrosses mate for life, and some live for over sixty years.

# Smelling with a tongue
@src Schwenk, Science, 1994

When a snake flicks its tongue, it's not tasting the air, as is often thought. It's collecting chemical particles from the air, or from the ground. The tongue is forked, and when the snake pulls it back into its mouth, it presses the tips into two small pits in the roof of its mouth. They are the openings of the vomeronasal organ, also called Jacobson's organ, which analyzes the chemicals.

The fork has a purpose. In 1994 Kurt Schwenk argued that, like having two ears, having two tips allows the snake to sample two places at once. It can tell which side the scent is stronger on, and follow a trail, such as that of a mouse, by turning toward the stronger side. Snakes can also track a trail left by a mate. It's a form of stereo smell. Lizards, such as monitors, have forked tongues too.

# Rumbles below hearing
@src Payne, Langbauer & Thomas, Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology, 1986

In the 1980s the biologist Katy Payne was at the Portland zoo, where she noticed a throbbing feeling in the air near the elephants. She thought of the low sounds that whales make, which travel for long distances. She went back with recording equipment, and detected sounds that were below the range of human hearing, called infrasound. The elephants were communicating at frequencies of fourteen to thirty-five hertz.

Low sounds travel a long way. In studies in Africa, Payne and others found that elephants can hear each other's calls from several miles away. They may also sense vibrations through their feet, which are transmitted through the ground. Calls can be used to find mates, and warn of danger. The elephants were talking to each other all the time, and people had never heard them.

# The songs that cross the ocean
@src Garland et al., Current Biology, 2011

Male humpback whales sing long and complex songs, which can last for twenty minutes, and be repeated for hours. All the whales in a population sing the same song at a given time, and it changes gradually over the season. In 2011 Ellen Garland and her colleagues studied the songs of whales in the South Pacific, over eleven years.

They found that a new song would appear among the whales off eastern Australia, and then spread, within a year or two, to the whales in the next population to the east, and then on again, as far as French Polynesia, some six thousand kilometers away. The whales seem to learn the new song from one another. It's one of the best-known cases of cultural transmission in animals. The songs change as they pass, like a hit song that spreads from one country to the next.

# Air-conditioned mounds
@src Turner, The Extended Organism, 2000; King, Ocko & Mahadevan, PNAS, 2015
@conf mixed

Termites in Africa build great towers of mud, up to ten feet tall or more, which house colonies of millions. The mounds were long said to work as natural air-conditioners. The idea was that hot air from the nest rises through channels in the chimney, and fresh air is drawn in at the bottom, keeping the temperature steady at about thirty degrees, as the termites need.

The ideas inspired some architecture. A shopping complex in Zimbabwe, called Eastgate, was designed in 1996 with a cooling system said to be modeled on the mounds. Recent studies, however, have questioned the details. In 2015 researchers found that the mounds work more like a lung, with air moving in and out in a daily cycle, driven by the day-night changes in temperature. The mound is a remarkable structure. But how it works is still under debate.

# The feet that stick without glue
@src Lewis & Clark College and Stanford teams, Nature, 2000

A gecko can run up a smooth glass wall, and hang from the ceiling by one toe. It uses no glue, and its feet are not sticky. For centuries, scientists wondered how it did this. In 2000 a team of biologists and engineers, led from Lewis & Clark College in Oregon, showed that the answer lay in the tiny hairs on its toes. A gecko's foot has about half a million microscopic hairs, called setae, and each has hundreds of tips, called spatulae, only about two hundred nanometers wide.

The tips get so close to the surface that weak attractive forces between molecules, called van der Waals forces, hold them. Each tip's pull is tiny, but with billions of them, the total is enough to support the animal's weight many times over. The gecko releases its grip by changing the angle of the toe. Engineers have used the idea to make adhesive tapes and robots that climb walls.

# Older than trees
@src Janvier, Early Vertebrates, 1996; Stein et al., Nature, 2007

Sharks are very old. The first creatures that look like sharks appeared in the oceans more than four hundred million years ago, in the Silurian or Devonian periods. The earliest known trees, such as Archaeopteris, did not appear on land until about three hundred and eighty-five million years ago. So sharks are older than trees, and older than the dinosaurs, by a long way.

Sharks have survived five mass extinctions. Their skeletons are made of cartilage, which does not fossilize well, so most of what we know about early sharks comes from their teeth. Modern sharks vary widely, from the dwarf lanternshark, which is about eight inches long, to the whale shark, at more than forty feet. Many species are now threatened by overfishing. Some are killed for their fins, or caught by accident, in numbers of tens of millions a year.

# The blue blood that saves lives
@src Novitsky, Limulus Amebocyte Lysate, 1984; Gorman, Horseshoe Crabs, 2020

The horseshoe crab, a creature that has existed for about four hundred and fifty million years, has blue blood. It's blue because it uses a copper-based molecule, hemocyanin, to carry oxygen, and not the iron-based hemoglobin that makes our blood red. The blood has another feature. It clots when it comes into contact with bacterial toxins.

In the 1960s Frederik Bang and Jack Levin found that a substance in the crab's blood cells could detect even tiny amounts of bacterial endotoxin. It was developed into a test, called the LAL test. It's used to make sure that vaccines, injectable drugs and medical devices are free from contamination. Every year, many thousands of crabs are caught, bled, and returned to the sea, though some die. Synthetic alternatives, which do not need the crabs, are now being adopted.

# Ravens that plan ahead
@src Kabadayi & Osvath, Science, 2017

Do animals plan for the future? Humans are thought to be special in this, and in the past, evidence from animals was disputed. In 2017 Can Kabadayi and Mathias Osvath, at Lund University in Sweden, tested ravens. They taught the birds to use a tool, a stone, to open a box with a reward. Then they removed the box, and gave the ravens a choice of objects. One was a tool, among others that were useless.

Some time later, the ravens were given access to the box, in another room. The birds that had picked the tool carried it, and used it. When a raven was offered a choice of the tool or a smaller treat, it often chose the tool. The ravens seemed to understand that they would need the tool later, in a different place. Similar planning was found in apes. It shows that the ability to plan for the future is not unique to humans.

# Betty the crow
@src Weir, Chappell & Kacelnik, Science, 2002

New Caledonian crows live on a Pacific island, and they are known for using tools. They make hooks from twigs, and use them to pull grubs from holes. In 2002 Alex Weir and his colleagues at Oxford were studying a captive female, called Betty. She had been given a choice of a straight piece of wire and a hooked one, to fish a small bucket of food out of a tube.

The male crow took the hooked wire, leaving Betty with the straight one. Without having been trained, she picked up the straight wire, wedged one end in a crack, and bent it with her beak to make a hook. She then lifted the bucket. She repeated it in nine out of ten trials, and bent it in different ways. It was striking evidence of a bird's ability to manufacture a tool for a purpose.

# The grandmothers of the sea
@src Brent, Franks, Croft, Cant & Foster, Current Biology, 2015

Killer whales, or orcas, are among the few animals, besides humans, in which females live long after they stop reproducing. A female orca may stop having calves at about forty, and live to be eighty or ninety. Why? In 2015 Lauren Brent and her colleagues studied a population of orcas in the Pacific Northwest, which had been followed for over forty years.

They found that older females, the grandmothers, were the leaders of the groups when the whales were hunting for salmon. They tended to lead the group more as they grew older, particularly when the food was scarce. They seemed to hold knowledge about where to find the fish. The calves of mothers who had a living grandmother also had a better chance of surviving. It supports the grandmother hypothesis, that older females can help their families.
