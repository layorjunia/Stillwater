# The opera house that cost fourteen times too much
@src Flyvbjerg, Bruzelius & Rothengatter, Megaprojects and Risk, 2003

In 1957 a Danish architect, Jørn Utzon, won a competition to design an opera house for the harbor of Sydney, Australia. His design had a set of white sail-shaped shells, and it's now one of the most recognizable buildings in the world. The government estimated that it would cost about seven million Australian dollars, and be finished in 1963.

It opened in 1973, ten years late, and the final cost was about a hundred and two million dollars, more than fourteen times the estimate. The shells were difficult to build, and the engineers had to find a way of constructing them. Utzon resigned in 1966, after a dispute with the government, and never returned to Australia to see his building. The overrun was extreme, but not unusual. The planning fallacy applies to large public projects, as well as to our own tasks.

# Markets that predict elections
@src Berg, Forsythe, Nelson & Rietz, Handbook of Experimental Economics Results, 2008

In 1988 the University of Iowa started a small online market in which people could bet real money on the outcome of elections. In the Iowa Electronic Markets, a share in a candidate pays one dollar if that candidate wins the popular vote. The price of the share is the market's guess of the chance of winning, and traders profit if they are right.

Over several decades, the prices were closer to the actual results than polls, in most cases, especially when the election was weeks away. The reason is that the market gathers the knowledge of many people, who have a reason to be right, and it responds quickly to news. It's an example of the wisdom of crowds. Prediction markets have been used for forecasting many other things, though they can be distorted by a few traders with a lot of money, and by thin trading.

# How firefighters decide
@src Klein, Sources of Power, 1998

The psychologist Gary Klein wanted to study how experienced people make decisions under pressure. In the 1980s he interviewed fire commanders about their hardest calls. He expected that they would say that they compared options, weighing the pros and cons. They said that they didn't. In most cases, they said that they never made decisions at all, and just did what was obvious.

Klein found that the commanders recognized the situation as one of a type that they had seen before, and a course of action came to mind. They checked it by imagining how it would play out. If it seemed to work, they did it. If not, they changed it. He called it recognition-primed decision-making. It means that expert intuition is based on pattern recognition built from years of experience, and that it works best where the situation is familiar, and feedback is quick.

# The regret of the neighbor's lottery
@src Zeelenberg & Pieters, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 2004

In the Netherlands, a lottery draws a postal code, and everyone who lives there and has a ticket wins a prize. In 2004 Marcel Zeelenberg and Rik Pieters studied what happens when a person who doesn't have a ticket sees their neighbors win. The sight triggers a strong feeling of regret, since they would have won, if they had taken part.

The researchers found that the feeling of regret was greater than that of a person who plays and loses, and that it motivated people to take part. Regret is felt more strongly when the outcome of the option that we didn't choose is known, and when it was easy to choose. It's a force that marketers use, and that we use in our own choices. People often buy insurance, or tickets, to avoid the pain of regret.

# The cost of being able to change your mind
@src Gilbert & Ebert, JPSP, 2002

In 2002 Daniel Gilbert and Jane Ebert ran an experiment in a photography course at Harvard. Students took photographs, and then picked their two favorites to print. They were asked to choose one to keep, and the other would be kept by the researchers. For some students, the choice was final. Others were told that they could change their minds within five days, and swap.

When asked later how they felt about their photographs, the students who could not change their minds liked their pictures more than those who could. Yet, when asked beforehand, most people said that they would prefer to have the option of changing. The ability to switch kept the students from fully accepting their choice. The finding suggests that we like our choices better when we have no way out, and that keeping options open has a hidden cost.

# What frightens us
@src Slovic, Science, 1987

Why are people more afraid of some risks than others? In the 1970s and 80s Paul Slovic and his colleagues asked people to rate dozens of hazards, such as nuclear power, motor vehicles, pesticides and swimming. The ratings were compared with the statistics of deaths. Experts ranked the risks according to the number of deaths. Ordinary people ranked them differently.

Slovic found two main factors. One was dread, how much a risk is feared, as for something uncontrollable, catastrophic and fatal. The other was how unknown the risk is, to the people exposed, and to science. Nuclear power scored high on both, and was ranked as very risky by laypeople, while experts put it far lower. Risks like driving, with many deaths spread over time, caused less fear. It suggests that fear tracks the character of a risk, and not just its size.

# When to stop looking
@src Gilbert & Mosteller, Journal of the American Statistical Association, 1966; Seale & Rapoport, 1997

You are looking at apartments, or job candidates. You see them one at a time, and you must decide on the spot whether to take one. If you refuse, you can't go back. How many should you look at before you choose? This is called the secretary problem, and it has a surprising answer.

The best strategy is to look at the first thirty-seven percent of the options without choosing any, just noting the best so far. Then take the first one that is better than all of those. This gives about a thirty-seven percent chance of getting the very best. The number is one divided by e, the base of natural logarithms. In experiments, people tend to stop earlier than the optimal point. The rule assumes that you can only compare options, and that you can't go back, so it's a rough guide.

# Nudging the cafeteria
@src Thorndike, Sonnenberg, Riis, Barraclough & Levy, American Journal of Public Health, 2012

Can small changes in a cafeteria make people eat more healthily? In 2012 Anne Thorndike and her colleagues tried it at the cafeteria of Massachusetts General Hospital, which serves thousands of employees and visitors. They labeled food and drinks with colored dots. Green meant healthy, yellow meant less healthy and red meant unhealthy.

Three months later, they rearranged the display, putting the green items, such as bottled water, at eye level and in the most visible places. Sales of red items fell, and sales of green items rose. The effect continued for the following two years. There were no bans, and no change in prices. People could still choose anything. It's an example of choice architecture, and it suggests that an environment can steer us without removing our freedom.
