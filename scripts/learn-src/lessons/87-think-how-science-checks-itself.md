# Chocolate and Nobel Prizes
@src Messerli, New England Journal of Medicine, 2012

In 2012 a Swiss physician, Franz Messerli, published a short article in a leading medical journal. He plotted the chocolate consumption per person in twenty-two countries against the number of Nobel Prize winners per ten million people. The line was striking. Countries that ate more chocolate had more Nobel laureates, and the correlation was about 0.8, very strong.

Messerli did not claim that chocolate makes people win Nobel Prizes, though he joked that it might improve cognition. The article was partly a satire, to show how a correlation can appear without a cause. A rich country, with a good education system, may well eat more chocolate and produce more scientists. Switzerland was the top of the chart on both. It's a good example to keep in mind when reading a headline about a link between a food and a health outcome.

# The pipe smoker who doubted
@src Fisher, Nature, 1958; Stolley, American Journal of Epidemiology, 1991

Ronald Fisher was one of the greatest statisticians of the twentieth century. He invented much of modern experimental design. He was also a heavy pipe smoker. In the 1950s, as the evidence mounted that smoking caused lung cancer, he argued against it. He said that the data showed only a correlation. It was possible that something else, such as a gene, caused both a desire to smoke and a tendency to get cancer.

He was also paid as a consultant by the tobacco industry. Whether that colored his views is debated. His argument was logical, but was not borne out. Studies of twins, of people who quit, and of smokers' doses, as well as experiments in animals, built the case for causation. The episode illustrates the difficulty of proving cause without an experiment, and also how even brilliant people can be led by their habits.

# How to tell a cause
@src Hill, Proceedings of the Royal Society of Medicine, 1965

In 1965 the British statistician Austin Bradford Hill gave a talk in which he proposed a set of considerations for deciding whether an association is likely to be a cause. He had worked on the smoking and lung cancer studies. He called them nine viewpoints, but they are often called the Bradford Hill criteria.

They include the strength of the association, whether it's consistent in different studies and places, and whether the cause comes before the effect. Also important are a dose-response relationship, in which more exposure leads to more disease, a plausible mechanism, and whether stopping the exposure reduces the risk. Hill stressed that none of these is a hard rule, and that they are aids to judgment. They're still used in medicine and public health.

# The first modern clinical trial
@src Medical Research Council, British Medical Journal, 1948

In 1947 streptomycin, a new antibiotic, was available in Britain in tiny amounts. There was not enough to treat all the patients with tuberculosis. The Medical Research Council, with Austin Bradford Hill as its statistician, decided to use the shortage to do a proper trial. One group of patients was given streptomycin and bed rest, and another was given bed rest only.

The patients were assigned by random numbers, from sealed envelopes, so that the doctors could not choose who got the drug. The X-rays were read by experts who did not know which group a patient was in. After six months, many more of the patients on the drug had improved, and fewer had died. It's regarded as the first properly randomized clinical trial. The method has since been used to test almost every medical treatment.

# Where did 0.05 come from?
@src Fisher, Statistical Methods for Research Workers, 1925

In many sciences, a result is said to be significant if the probability of getting it by chance is less than five percent, which is a p-value of 0.05. Where did the number come from? In 1925 the statistician Ronald Fisher wrote a handbook for researchers, which suggested that it was convenient to take a probability of one in twenty as a limit for judging whether a deviation is significant.

It was a rule of thumb, meant to be flexible, and not a law of nature. Fisher said later that a scientist should look at the whole body of results, and not rely on a single threshold. But the number became a fixed rule, and a large part of the literature has been shaped by it. Many researchers now argue that a lower threshold should be used for new findings, or that p-values should be replaced by other measures.

# Why most findings may be false
@src Ioannidis, PLoS Medicine, 2005
@conf mixed

In 2005 John Ioannidis, a physician and scientist at Stanford, published an essay with a startling title, Why Most Published Research Findings Are False. He used a simple argument based on probabilities. If many of the hypotheses that are tested are false, or if studies are small, or if there are many ways of analyzing the data, then a large share of the positive results will be false alarms.

The essay became one of the most downloaded in the history of the journal. Its claim has been debated. Some statisticians argue that his assumptions were too pessimistic, and that the rate of false findings differs between fields. But later large efforts to repeat studies in psychology, and in cancer biology, found that many results did not hold. The paper helped to start the movement for open and reliable science.

# Hypothesizing after the results
@src Kerr, Personality and Social Psychology Review, 1998

Suppose that you run an experiment, look at the data, and find an interesting pattern. Then you write the paper as if you had predicted it from the start. In 1998 the psychologist Norbert Kerr gave this practice a name, HARKing, which stands for Hypothesizing After the Results are Known. He pointed out that it is common, and that it may seem harmless.

But it makes the evidence look stronger than it is. A pattern that is found by looking is often due to chance, since with enough comparisons, something will always look significant. A hypothesis that predicted it in advance deserves more trust. The remedy is pre-registration, in which researchers state their plan and predictions before seeing the data, and register it in a public place.

# Twenty-nine teams, one question
@src Silberzahn et al., Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 2018

In 2018 a group of researchers led by Raphael Silberzahn and Eric Uhlmann posed a question. Do soccer referees give more red cards to players with darker skin? They gave the same large dataset to twenty-nine research teams, with sixty-one analysts, and asked each to analyze it independently.

The teams chose different methods, and reached different conclusions. About two thirds found that dark-skinned players were more likely to get a red card, by a statistically significant margin, and a third found no significant effect. The estimates ranged from a slightly lower chance to nearly three times the chance. The study shows that results depend on the choices of the analyst, and not only on the data. It suggests that conclusions should be checked by more than one team.

# The Broad Street pump
@src Snow, On the Mode of Communication of Cholera, 1855

In 1854 a severe outbreak of cholera hit the Soho district of London. In ten days, more than five hundred people died, in a few streets. At the time, most doctors believed that cholera was caused by bad air. A local physician, John Snow, thought it was spread in water. He talked to residents, and marked each death on a map of the area.

The deaths clustered around a water pump on Broad Street. Snow found that people who lived farther away, but who had drunk from the pump, also got sick, while workers at a nearby brewery, who drank beer, did not. He persuaded the local officials to remove the pump handle. The outbreak was already waning, but his map was a landmark of epidemiology. A decade later, the evidence for germ theory made his view the standard.

# The grand experiment
@src Snow, 1855; Hempel, The Strange Case of the Broad Street Pump, 2013

Snow's study of the Broad Street pump is better known, but he considered another, larger study to be his best. In 1854, in the south of London, two water companies supplied houses on the same streets, in a mixed pattern. The Lambeth company drew its water from the Thames upstream of the city, where it was cleaner. The Southwark and Vauxhall company drew its water from below the city's sewage outflows.

Snow counted the cholera deaths in the houses supplied by each. The households that got water from Southwark and Vauxhall were about eight or nine times more likely to die of cholera than those supplied by Lambeth. Since the customers were similar, and the houses were mixed on the same streets, it was like an experiment that nature had set up. He called it a grand experiment. It's an early example of a natural experiment, a method that economists and epidemiologists still use.
