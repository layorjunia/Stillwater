# Do men interrupt women more?
@src Zimmerman & West, 1975; Anderson & Leaper, Sex Roles, 1998
@conf mixed

In 1975 Don Zimmerman and Candace West recorded conversations in public places, such as coffee shops, and counted interruptions. In the pairs of one man and one woman, nearly all of the interruptions came from the man. The finding was widely cited as evidence that men dominate conversation.

The original sample was small, and later work has been less dramatic. In 1998 Kristin Anderson and Campbell Leaper combined dozens of studies. They found that men did interrupt more than women, but the difference was small on average, and it was larger when the interruptions were intrusive rather than supportive. Context matters a lot, including who has power in the room. The question isn't whether men or women talk more rudely, but who gets to finish their sentences in which situations.

# Rudeness costs performance
@src Riskin et al., Pediatrics, 2015

In 2015 Arieh Riskin and his colleagues tested what rudeness does to a medical team. They had twenty-four neonatal intensive care teams run a simulated case of a very premature baby who was seriously ill. Before the exercise, an expert gave a short talk to each team. For half of the teams, the expert added a mild insult about the quality of care in their country. It was unrelated to the teams' work.

The teams that heard the rude remarks did worse at diagnosing the problem and at carrying out the procedures, as rated by judges who didn't know which teams had been insulted. They shared less information with each other and asked for less help. Even small amounts of rudeness can affect the thinking and the cooperation of a group. In work where mistakes are costly, it matters.

# Eyes closed, empathy up
@src Kraus, American Psychologist, 2017
@conf mixed

It seems obvious that we read other people better when we can see their faces. In 2017 Michael Kraus ran a series of experiments to test it. People either listened to someone talking, or watched them, or both, and then judged what the person was feeling. Then their guesses were compared with what the person had said they felt.

People were more accurate when they could only hear the voice than when they could also see the person. The explanation is that without a face to look at, listeners attended more to the tone, pitch and rhythm of the voice, which carry a lot of emotion. The effect was not huge, and it depends on the situation, since faces also help. But it suggests that on a phone call, or in a conversation where you look away, you may be getting more than you think.

# The feed experiment
@src Kramer, Guillory & Hancock, PNAS, 2014

In 2014 researchers at Facebook and Cornell published an experiment on nearly 700,000 users. For a week, the news feeds of some users showed fewer posts with positive words, and the feeds of others showed fewer with negative words. The researchers then looked at what those users wrote in their own posts.

People who saw fewer positive posts wrote slightly fewer positive words, and people who saw fewer negative posts wrote slightly fewer negative ones. The effects were tiny. But because the number of users was huge, the result was statistically clear. The study set off an argument about ethics, since users hadn't been asked for consent. It suggests that emotions can pass through text, with no face or voice, even if the effect on any one person is very small.

# Tu and vous
@src Brown & Gilman, The Pronouns of Power and Solidarity, 1960

Many languages have two words for you, such as tu and vous in French, or du and Sie in German. In 1960 the psychologists Roger Brown and Albert Gilman studied how they are used. In the past, they found, the choice reflected power. The person with higher rank used the familiar form to the lower, and got the formal form back.

Over the twentieth century, they argued, the pattern shifted to solidarity. People who feel close, or who see themselves as equals, use the familiar form with each other, and strangers use the formal one. Moving from one to the other is a social act. Suggesting that you switch to tu is a way of saying that you'd like to be closer. English lost the distinction, but we still use first names and titles in a similar way.

# Fast languages and slow ones
@src Coupé, Oh, Dediu & Pellegrino, Science Advances, 2019

Some languages sound much faster than others. Japanese and Spanish pack in many syllables per second, while Vietnamese and Thai seem slower. Does that mean that some languages communicate faster? In 2019 Christophe Coupé and his colleagues recorded 170 speakers of seventeen languages reading the same set of texts.

The faster languages used syllables that carried less information, and the slower ones used syllables that carried more. When the researchers multiplied the two, the rate at which information was delivered came out nearly the same across languages, at about 39 bits per second. The authors suggested that it may reflect a limit on how fast human brains can process speech. If so, every language has found its own way to the same speed.

# Huh? is a word everywhere
@src Dingemanse, Torreira & Enfield, PLoS ONE, 2013

When you can't hear what someone said, you probably say "huh?" In 2013 Mark Dingemanse and his colleagues recorded conversations in languages from all over the world, from Icelandic to Lao, Mandarin to Siwu in Ghana. They looked at what people say when they need someone to repeat themselves.

In all thirty-one languages that they studied, the word was a short syllable with a questioning rise in pitch, and in most of them it had a vowel like the one in "huh" or "eh." The researchers didn't think it was an instinct. They proposed that the word has settled in similar form in each language because conversation puts the same pressure on every language. You need a word that is quick, easy to say and clearly a question. It is a small case of languages arriving at the same solution.

# Why jumbled letters are not easy to read
@src Rayner, White, Johnson & Liversedge, Psychological Science, 2006

You may have seen the message that says that it doesn't matter in what order the letters in a word come, as long as the first and last letters are in place. It claims to come from research at Cambridge. There was no such research at Cambridge. The message was a hoax that went around the internet.

There is some truth in it. In 2006 Keith Rayner and his colleagues tested readers with sentences in which the middle letters of words were transposed. People could read them, since our brains are tolerant of small changes. But reading was slower. Scrambling the first letters of a word cost more than scrambling the ones in the middle. The brain does not read letter by letter, but it does use the letters, and scrambling them has a cost.

# Reasoning to win the argument
@src Mercier & Sperber, Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 2011
@series think
@conf mixed

Why does human reasoning have so many flaws? We look for evidence that supports what we already believe, and we are blind to our own mistakes. In 2011 Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber offered a surprising answer. They proposed that reasoning didn't evolve to help us find the truth alone. It evolved to help us argue, to persuade others, and to judge their arguments.

If that is right, the flaws look like features. One-sided thinking is useful when you're making your case, and the main check comes from other people who disagree. The theory has been debated, and not all researchers accept it. But it explains some findings, such as the fact that people are better at finding the weak points in other people's arguments than in their own. It suggests that we think best in conversation with others.

# Groups that argue get it right
@src Moshman & Geil, Thinking and Reasoning, 1998
@series think

The Wason selection task is a logic puzzle that most people get wrong. Four cards are laid out, and you must turn over the right ones to test a rule. When individuals tried it, only about one in ten got it right. In 1998 David Moshman and Molly Geil gave it to college students working alone, and to groups of five or six who could discuss it.

Three quarters of the groups found the correct answer. The authors concluded that it wasn't simply that one knowledgeable member told the others. It was that, as they argued, someone would say a wrong answer, others would object with a reason, and the group would work its way to the right one. Arguing in a group can fix errors that a single person can't see. It works best when people really engage with each other's reasons.

# Groups that take turns are smarter
@src Woolley et al., Science, 2010
@series think
@conf mixed

Is there such a thing as a group's intelligence, apart from the intelligence of its members? In 2010 Anita Woolley and her colleagues gave nearly seven hundred people a range of tasks, working in groups of two to five. The groups that did well on one task tended to do well on the others, which suggested a general factor, like IQ for groups.

The score didn't depend much on how smart the individual members were. It was higher in groups where the members were good at reading each other's feelings, where the talking was shared fairly evenly, and where there were more women. Later work has questioned how far the results hold up, and some studies find that the members' own abilities matter more. But the finding about even turn-taking is intriguing. A group does better when everyone gets to speak.

# When the crowd talks, it gets less wise
@src Lorenz, Rauhut, Schweitzer & Helbing, PNAS, 2011
@series think

The wisdom of crowds says that the average of many independent guesses is often very good. The key word is independent. In 2011 Jan Lorenz and his colleagues asked people factual questions, such as the length of a border or the number of murders in a city. Some people answered alone, and others saw what the rest of the group had guessed, and could revise.

When people saw each other's answers, the guesses became more alike, but not more accurate. The crowd lost its diversity, and so it lost the errors that canceled each other out. People also felt more confident, though they were no more right. It is a warning about group discussions and polls. If people hear others' views before they form their own, the group can end up confidently wrong. It can help to collect people's ideas privately before sharing them.

# Standing meetings
@src Bluedorn, Turban & Love, Journal of Applied Psychology, 1999

Do meetings go faster if no one can sit down? In 1999 Allen Bluedorn, Daniel Turban and Mary Sue Love tested this with more than a hundred groups of five people. Each group had to make a decision about a task. Half of the groups did it standing up, and the others sat around a table.

The standing groups finished their meetings about a third faster. The quality of their decisions was no worse than that of the groups who sat. People were less satisfied with the standing meetings, and the researchers put it down mostly to comfort. The meetings were short, about ten to twenty minutes, with one clear decision, so the results may not apply to long ones. But it suggests that much of the time that meetings take is not needed.

# Checking email less
@src Kushlev & Dunn, Computers in Human Behavior, 2015
@series mind

In 2015 Kostadin Kushlev and Elizabeth Dunn asked about a hundred and twenty adults to change how they dealt with email for a week. In one week, they were told to check it only three times a day. In another, they could check as often as they liked. The order was varied among the people. At the end of each week, they filled out questionnaires about stress.

During the weeks of limited checking, people reported less daily stress. Some people found it hard to hold back, and the benefit was not the same for everyone. But on average, fewer checks meant less stress. It's an easy test to run on yourself. Email is rarely as urgent as the habit of checking it suggests.

# Do audiobooks count?
@src Rogowsky, Calhoun & Tallal, SAGE Open, 2016
@series learn
@conf mixed

Is listening to a book less real than reading it? In 2016 Beth Rogowsky, Barbara Calhoun and Paula Tallal gave adults a text to read, to listen to, or to do both at once. Afterward, they tested how much they understood and remembered.

There was no significant difference between the groups in comprehension. Listening worked about as well as reading, at least for this material and for this group of adults. The study was fairly small, and other work finds differences with difficult texts, or for people who get distracted when they listen. But it suggests that the format matters less than the attention you bring to it. If you're walking the dog and listening, you're probably learning.

# Writing to learn
@src Graham & Hebert, Harvard Educational Review, 2011
@series learn

If you write about something you've read, do you understand it better? In 2011 Steve Graham and Michael Hebert combined the results of many experiments on writing and reading. They found that students who wrote about the texts they read understood and remembered them better than students who just read them or studied them in other ways.

The most helpful kinds of writing were those that made the student work with the ideas. Summarizing in your own words, answering questions about the text and taking notes were all better than copying. Writing makes you put ideas in order and reveals what you don't understand. It's a form of retrieval too. If you want to learn something, a good method is to close the book and write down what you remember.

# Why mama and papa
@src Jakobson, Why Mama and Papa?, 1960
@series words

In language after language, the words for mother and father sound alike. Mama and papa are close to the words in Mandarin, Hebrew, Swahili and Hindi, with small differences. It looks like it has to be a coincidence or a common origin, but the languages aren't related.

In 1960 the linguist Roman Jakobson offered an explanation that is still widely accepted. The first sounds that babies make easily are the simple ones, such as ma and pa and ba, made by opening the lips and making a vowel. Parents hear these sounds and take them as names, and the words stick. In this view the words come from the babies, and not from the parents. It's a rare case of a pattern shared across languages, with a simple cause.

# OK
@src Read, American Speech, 1963 and 1964
@series words

OK may be the most widely used word on earth, and it began as a joke. In the 1960s the scholar Allen Walker Read traced it to Boston in 1839. It was a time when newspapers had a fad for abbreviations, often with deliberate misspellings. O.K. stood for oll korrect, a funny way of writing all correct.

Most of the fad words died out, but OK survived. In 1840 it got a boost, when the supporters of President Martin Van Buren, who was from Old Kinderhook in New York, formed the O.K. Clubs. The word spread to other countries and languages, helped later by the telegraph. Today it needs no translation. A silly spelling of the 1830s became one of the best known words in the world.

# The few words we use all the time
@src Zipf, Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort, 1949
@series words

If you count the words in a long text and rank them by how often they appear, a pattern shows up. The most common word appears about twice as often as the second, three times as often as the third, and so on. The linguist George Zipf described this in 1949, and the pattern is known as Zipf's law.

It holds in nearly every language that has been tested, and it also appears in other things, such as the populations of cities. In English, a small number of short words, such as the, of, and and to, make up a large share of everything that we say. Most of the words in a dictionary are rare. Zipf thought that it came from a principle of least effort, in which speakers and listeners both want to keep the work low. The exact cause is still debated.

# A foreign language and clearer thinking
@src Keysar, Hayakawa & An, Psychological Science, 2012
@series think
@conf mixed

In 2012 Boaz Keysar, Sayuri Hayakawa and Sun Gyu An asked people to make decisions in a foreign language they had learned. One of the problems was a classic. A disease is expected to kill six hundred people, and a choice is described either in terms of lives saved or lives lost. In their own language, people tend to take a safe option when it is framed as a gain, and a risky one when it's framed as a loss.

In a foreign language, the framing effect shrank or disappeared. The explanation was that a foreign language has less emotional pull, so people think a little more slowly and more carefully. Some later studies found the effect for some kinds of decisions, and not for others. It suggests that thinking in a second language may add distance. When a decision is emotional, it could be worth restating it in plain, neutral words.
