# Reading character from the bumps on a skull
@series sci
@src Franz Joseph Gall, 1796; Edinburgh Phrenological Society, 1820

In 1796 a German physician, Franz Joseph Gall, began to develop a theory called phrenology. It held that the brain is the organ of the mind, and that different parts of the brain have particular functions. These ideas have a basis in reality. But Gall went further. He believed that the brain was made of separate organs, like muscles, that grew with use, and that the bumps on the skull showed which of them were large. By feeling the shape of a head, one could read a person's character.

The theory was extremely popular in the nineteenth century, especially from about 1810 to 1840. Its main British center was Edinburgh, where a phrenological society was set up in 1820. People had their heads read for advice about marriage, jobs and education.

It is now recognized as a pseudoscience. The central claim, that the contours of the skull predict personality, has been discredited by research. Its methods were doubted even in its own day. Yet it left a lasting mark, since the notion that mental functions are localized in the brain turned out to have truth in it. Phrenology got the right question and the wrong method.

# The healer whose force was tested with a blindfold
@series history
@src Franz Mesmer, animal magnetism, 1779; royal commission, 1784

Franz Mesmer was a German physician who in the 1770s put forward a theory that he called animal magnetism. He said that there is an invisible natural force in all living things, and that illness arose when it was out of balance. He claimed that he could direct it to heal. The word mesmerize comes from his name.

The idea drew many followers in Europe and the United States, and was an important influence in medicine for about 75 years from its beginnings in 1779. Hundreds of books were written about it. His treatments in Paris were dramatic, with patients sitting around a tub, holding iron rods, some falling into fits or trances.

In 1784 the French king named a commission to examine it, and its members included Benjamin Franklin and the chemist Antoine Lavoisier. They ran tests in which people were blindfolded, or were tricked about whether they were being treated. The effects appeared when people believed they were being magnetized, and not when they were. The commission concluded that the effects were due to imagination. The investigation is one of the first uses of controlled trials with blinding, and it was a milestone in the history of scientific method.

# The cure that lasted two thousand years
@series history
@src History of bloodletting; humoral theory; Ebers Papyrus

For well over two thousand years, a standard treatment for many illnesses was to remove some of the patient's blood. Bloodletting was done with a lancet, or with leeches. It was the most common procedure performed by surgeons from ancient times until the late nineteenth century. Passages in the Ebers Papyrus of ancient Egypt may refer to it.

It was based on the ancient theory of the humors. Blood and other fluids of the body, such as phlegm and bile, were thought to need to be in balance, and an excess of blood was supposed to be a cause of fever and other diseases. So doctors drew off a pint or more, and sometimes repeated it.

At the start of the nineteenth century, studies began to show that bloodletting was harmful. It was not used in a great many cases, but in Europe it remained fairly common until the end of the century. Modern medicine has dropped it for all but a few special conditions. Therapeutic phlebotomy is still used to treat illnesses in which there is too much iron or too many red blood cells, such as hemochromatosis. A practice that went on for twenty centuries was wrong in nearly every case, because no one measured the results.

# The biologist who won because Stalin liked him
@series history
@src Lysenkoism; Trofim Lysenko; Nikolai Vavilov

Trofim Lysenko was a Soviet agronomist who rose to power in the 1930s. He rejected the science of genetics, and the idea of natural selection, and favored a version of the theory that characteristics acquired in life can be inherited. He promised miracles in agriculture, such as winter wheat that could be planted in spring, and he was backed by the state.

Lysenko denounced geneticists as enemies of the people. His campaign, known as Lysenkoism, led to the dismissal or imprisonment of more than 3,000 mainstream biologists, and some were executed. The president of the Soviet agricultural academy, Nikolai Vavilov, who had once been Lysenko's mentor, was sent to prison and died there. Soviet genetics was effectively destroyed. Joseph Stalin personally edited one of Lysenko's speeches.

The teaching was imposed on other countries of the Eastern Bloc, and for some years, on China. It did not raise yields, and probably helped to cause shortages. It is the leading example of what can happen when politics decides what is true in science. Genetics, which was banned in the Soviet Union, flourished in the West, where the structure of DNA was found in 1953.

# The table-top fusion that nobody could repeat
@series sci
@src Fleischmann and Pons, 1989; University of Utah

On March 23, 1989, two electrochemists at the University of Utah, Martin Fleischmann and Stanley Pons, announced at a press conference that they had achieved nuclear fusion at room temperature. Fusion, the process that powers the Sun, normally needs temperatures of millions of degrees. They said that a small apparatus on a table, in which they passed an electric current through heavy water with an electrode of palladium, produced more heat than any chemical reaction could explain.

They also reported small amounts of neutrons and tritium, which are products of fusion. The news drew enormous attention. It raised hopes of cheap and plentiful energy. Many scientists around the world tried to repeat it, from the few details that were available.

Most could not. Some groups found no excess heat, and others found errors in the measurements. The neutrons and tritium that they had reported are also found in small amounts in nature. Within months, most physicists had concluded that the claim was mistaken. No accepted theory explains how cold fusion could occur. The episode is a lesson in the danger of announcing a result at a press conference before it has been checked by other scientists.

# The rays that only some people could see
@series sci
@src Prosper-René Blondlot, 1903; Robert W. Wood, 1904

In the early years of the twentieth century, physics was full of surprises. X-rays had been found in 1895, radioactivity in 1896, and the electron in 1897. Scientists expected still more kinds of radiation. In 1903 a respected French physicist, Prosper-René Blondlot, a professor at the University of Nancy, announced that he had found a new one. He called them N-rays, after Nancy.

Other scientists in France reported that they too could detect the rays. They were seen as a faint brightening of a small spark, or of a dim light, when a source of the rays was present. Dozens of papers were published. Blondlot was a distinguished man, who had won prizes from the French Academy.

Researchers outside France could not find them. In 1904 an American physicist, Robert W. Wood, visited Blondlot's laboratory. In a darkened room, he secretly removed a part of the apparatus, an aluminum prism, that was supposed to be essential. Blondlot went on seeing the rays. The N-rays were an illusion, caused by wishful observing of faint effects. They are a classic example of how an expectation can lead honest people to see what they want to see.

# The water that was not water
@series sci
@src Nikolai Fedyakin, 1961; Boris Derjaguin; polywater controversy, 1960s to 1973

In 1961 a Soviet physicist, Nikolai Fedyakin, measured the properties of water that had been condensed in very narrow quartz tubes. He seemed to have found a new form of water. It boiled at a higher temperature than ordinary water, froze at a lower one, and was far more viscous, about like a syrup.

A more senior scientist, Boris Derjaguin of Moscow, made it faster, though still in tiny quantities. By 1969 the press in the West had taken notice, and there were fears of a polywater gap between the United States and the Soviet Union. Fears were raised that if it touched ordinary water it might convert it all, with disastrous results. Many laboratories tried to make it.

Doubts began as early as 1970. By 1973, it had been found that polywater was nothing but water with various common substances in it. The tiny amounts that were being made were contaminated by compounds from the glass and from the experimenters themselves. Polywater is now remembered as an example of what is called pathological science, in which researchers fool themselves by studying effects that are at the limit of what can be seen.

# The remedy that was diluted to nothing
@series history
@src Samuel Hahnemann, 1796; homeopathic dilution

Homeopathy was invented in 1796 by a German physician, Samuel Hahnemann. Its central doctrine is that a substance that causes symptoms in a healthy person can cure similar symptoms in a sick one. It is summed up in the Latin phrase, similia similibus curentur, or like cures like.

Homeopathic remedies are made by repeated dilution. The chosen substance is diluted again and again, until the final product cannot be told from the diluent. Often not a single molecule of the original material can be expected to remain. Between the steps the preparation is hit or shaken, in the belief that this makes the water remember what was in it.

Everything that science knows about physics, chemistry, biochemistry and biology contradicts the idea. Clinical trials have generally found no objective effect beyond a placebo. The theory of disease that Hahnemann held, based on what he called miasms, does not fit the later discovery that viruses and bacteria cause many illnesses. Homeopathy was most popular in the nineteenth century, and is still sold today. The remedies are mostly harmless in themselves. The danger is that a person may use them in place of treatment that works.

# The medicine that was dissolved in poison
@series history
@src Elixir Sulfanilamide, 1937; Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, 1938

In 1937 a Tennessee company, the S. E. Massengill Company, brought out a liquid form of sulfanilamide, an antibiotic that was in great demand. The new liquid was called Elixir Sulfanilamide. The company's chief chemist, Harold Watkins, dissolved the drug in diethylene glycol, and added raspberry flavoring. Diethylene glycol is poisonous, but he did not know it. Its toxicity was not widely known.

The company did no tests on animals. Neither the law nor any regulation required it, because at that time there was no federal requirement that drugs be proven safe before they were sold. Selling started in September 1937. Within weeks, people who had taken it began to die of kidney failure. It is believed to have killed 107 people, many of them children.

The outcry led Congress in 1938 to pass the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, which greatly increased the powers of the Food and Drug Administration. It required that new drugs be shown to be safe before they could be sold. Before then, the only federal laws had been the Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906 and the Harrison Act of 1914. A modern system of drug safety was born from a disaster.

# The sedative that harmed ten thousand babies
@series history
@src Thalidomide, 1957 to 1962; Frances Oldham Kelsey; Kefauver-Harris Amendment

Thalidomide was first sold in 1957 in West Germany, as a sedative that could be bought without a prescription. It was promoted for anxiety, trouble sleeping and tension, and for morning sickness in pregnancy. It was thought to be safe, even for pregnant women.

It was not. It turned out to cause severe birth defects, such as babies born without arms or legs, or with short, malformed limbs, and problems with the eyes, heart and urinary tract. The drug was taken off the market in Europe in 1961. The number of babies who were badly harmed is estimated at over 10,000, and possibly 20,000. About 40 percent died around the time of birth. A single dose was enough to cause damage.

The United States was largely spared, because a new reviewer at the Food and Drug Administration, Frances Oldham Kelsey, had refused to approve it, asking for more data. She received an award from President Kennedy in 1962. That year, Congress passed the Kefauver-Harris Amendment, which required drug makers to prove that a drug is effective as well as safe. Thalidomide is still used, under strict control, to treat certain cancers and leprosy complications.

# The tonic that made a millionaire's jaw fall off
@series history
@src Radithor, 1918 to 1932; Eben Byers; William J. A. Bailey

In the early twentieth century, radium was thought to be a miracle. A craze arose for radioactive products, sold as tonics. The best known was Radithor, a small bottle of distilled water that held radium-226 and radium-228, introduced in 1918 by William J. A. Bailey, who was not a doctor. It was advertised as a cure for the living dead, and as perpetual sunshine, and was said to cure many ills, including impotence. It was expensive, and sold some 400,000 bottles between 1925 and 1930.

One of its customers was Eben Byers, a wealthy steel industrialist, a Yale graduate and a well-known amateur golfer. He drank about 1,400 bottles from 1927. He died in 1932 of cancers that were caused by the radium, and before his death, his jaw had to be removed.

Byers was buried in a lead-lined coffin. When his body was exhumed in 1965 for study, it was still radioactive. His death ended the radium fad, and led to tighter control of drugs and radioactive substances. The Food and Drug Administration had warned against the product, but at that time it had no power to ban it. A fashionable cure had been a slow poison.
