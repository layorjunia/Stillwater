# The wheel of fortune
@src Tversky & Kahneman, Science, 1974

In the early 1970s Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman asked people a question about the world. What percentage of the countries in the United Nations are in Africa? Before answering, each person watched a wheel of fortune that had been fixed to stop on either 10 or 65. They were asked to say whether the true answer was higher or lower than that number, and then to give their own estimate.

Those who saw 10 gave a median estimate of about 25 percent. Those who saw 65 gave about 45 percent. The number on the wheel had nothing to do with Africa, and people knew it was random. But it pulled their answers toward it. This is anchoring. We start from a number we've been given, even a useless one, and adjust, usually not far enough.

# Which hospital has more unusual days?
@src Tversky & Kahneman, Science, 1974

A town has two hospitals. In the larger one, about forty-five babies are born each day, and in the smaller one about fifteen. Roughly half of all babies are boys, but the exact percentage varies from day to day. For a year, each hospital records the days on which more than sixty percent of the babies born were boys. Which hospital has more such days?

Most people say that they would be about equal. In fact, the smaller hospital has more. A small sample strays from the average much more easily than a large one, since a few extra boys make a big difference to the percentage. People tend to ignore the size of a sample when they judge how typical a result is. It's one reason why a few cases, in a small group or a short stretch of time, can look like a pattern that isn't there.

# Same evidence, opposite conclusions
@src Lord, Ross & Lepper, JPSP, 1979

In 1979 Charles Lord, Lee Ross and Mark Lepper chose students who held strong views for or against the death penalty. Each student read about two studies, one that suggested that the death penalty deters crime and one that suggested that it doesn't. The studies were invented, and balanced in quality. The researchers asked what the students made of them.

Students accepted the study that agreed with their views as sound, and they found flaws in the one that didn't. After reading both, the two sides grew further apart. Each side felt that the mixed evidence supported them. This is called biased assimilation. Facts don't settle disagreements as easily as we expect, because we read them through what we already believe. Knowing this is a start, and asking how I would judge this study if it said the opposite helps.

# Wanting a different answer
@src Ditto & Lopez, JPSP, 1992

In 1992 Peter Ditto and David Lopez told students that they were being tested for an enzyme deficiency that could lead to serious illness. Each student put a drop of saliva on a test strip, and waited to see whether it turned green. Some were told that green meant the good result, that they did not have the deficiency. Others were told that green meant that they did.

The strip was just paper, and it never changed color. The students who hoped to see green waited a good deal longer for it than the students who hoped not to see it. Those who feared green soon decided that nothing was happening, and that all was well. Both groups looked at the same blank strip. We tend to give a welcome conclusion more chances to appear, and an unwelcome one fewer. The researchers called it motivated skepticism.

# The hungry shopper
@src Gilbert, Gill & Wilson, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 2002

When you go to the supermarket hungry, you buy more than you planned. In 2002 Daniel Gilbert and his colleagues tested this with shoppers outside a grocery store. They gave some of them a muffin to eat, and others got nothing. Everyone had a list of what they planned to buy, and afterward the researchers checked the receipts.

The shoppers who hadn't eaten bought more items that weren't on their lists. Their present hunger had spilled over into what they bought for the future. It's one case of a general problem. We are not good at imagining how we'll feel in a different state. A hungry person thinks that they'll be hungry all week, and a calm person can't picture the heat of anger. It's why decisions are better made when you're fed, rested and calm.

# The dating question
@src Strack, Martin & Schwarz, European Journal of Social Psychology, 1988

How happy are you with your life as a whole? It's a hard question. In 1988 Fritz Strack and his colleagues asked students two questions. One was about general happiness. The other was about how often they dated. The only difference was the order. Some students got the happiness question first, and some got the dating question first.

When the dating question came first, the answers to the two questions were strongly related, so students who dated more said that they were happier. When the happiness question came first, there was almost no relationship. The dating question had made them think about it as they judged their lives. We don't consult a store of knowledge when we answer a big question. We build an answer on the spot from whatever comes to mind. It's why our mood and the weather can sway our judgments.

# Is it harder to remember means it happens less?
@src Schwarz et al., JPSP, 1991

In 1991 Norbert Schwarz and his colleagues asked students to recall either six or twelve times when they had acted assertively. Then they asked them to rate how assertive they were. You'd expect that people who recalled more examples would rate themselves as more assertive.

The opposite happened. Those who had listed twelve examples saw themselves as less assertive than those who had listed six. Coming up with twelve was hard, and the difficulty felt like a sign that they weren't very assertive. We judge frequency by how easily examples come to mind, and not just by how many we have. The finding shows that the feeling of ease is a piece of information, and that we sometimes read it wrongly.

# If it's hard to read, it's hard to do
@src Song & Schwarz, Psychological Science, 2008

Suppose that you are given a recipe for Japanese sushi rolls, or a new exercise routine. In 2008 Hyunjin Song and Norbert Schwarz gave people instructions that were printed either in a clear font or in a difficult one that was hard to read. Then they asked how long the task would take, and whether they would be willing to do it.

People who got the hard-to-read version thought that the task would take nearly twice as long, and they were less willing to try it. The look of the instructions had changed their estimate of the effort needed. The feeling of difficulty from reading was mistaken for difficulty in doing. It's a practical tip. If you want someone to try something, make the instructions easy to read. And if you feel resistance to a plan, check whether the way it's written is part of the problem.

# The coin that people flip and then ignore
@src Batson et al., JPSP, 1997

In 1997 Daniel Batson and his colleagues asked students to assign themselves and another person to two tasks. One task offered a chance to win raffle tickets, and the other was boring and had no reward. The other person would never know who had decided. Some students were given a coin and told that they could flip it to decide, if they wanted to.

Most of the students who didn't have a coin gave themselves the good task. Of those who took the coin and flipped it, nearly all of them also gave themselves the good task, even though a fair coin should have given each about half the time. They used the coin as if it made the decision fair, and then ignored it when it came out wrong. Batson called it moral hypocrisy. We want to appear fair as well as to get what we want.

# Practicing what you preach
@src Aronson, Fried & Stone, American Journal of Public Health, 1991

How can you persuade people to do something that they know is good for them, such as using condoms? In 1991 Elliot Aronson, Carrie Fried and Jeff Stone studied college students. All were asked to make a video that would persuade high school students to use them. Some of them were then asked to think about times when they had failed to do so themselves.

Those who had made the video and also been reminded of their own lapses were much more likely to buy condoms at the end, and said that they planned to use them. They felt the contradiction between what they had preached and what they had done. This is called hypocrisy induction. It uses the discomfort of dissonance to change behavior. It works best when the person has made a public statement, and has been gently reminded of their own gaps.

# Moral licensing
@src Monin & Miller, JPSP, 2001; Blanken, van de Ven & Zeelenberg, 2015
@conf mixed

Do good deeds make us behave better, or worse? In 2001 Benoît Monin and Dale Miller had students first disagree with sexist statements, and so show that they weren't prejudiced. Then they asked them to choose someone for a job that is stereotypically male. Those who had shown their fair-mindedness were more willing to pick a man.

The idea is called moral licensing. After a good deed, we may feel that we've earned some credit, and so we allow ourselves a lapse. A review of studies in 2015 found that the effect exists, but is small, and may be exaggerated by the way studies get published. It's probably not a strong force. But it's a good question to ask. When you feel particularly virtuous, what are you allowing yourself to do?

# Claiming to know what doesn't exist
@src Paulhus, Harms, Bruce & Lysy, JPSP, 2003

How much do you know about the subjects you say that you know? In 2003 Delroy Paulhus and his colleagues gave people a list of names, events and terms, and asked them how well they knew each one. Some of the items were real, such as famous people or places. Some of them were made up.

Many people said that they knew some of the fake items. The more people thought they knew about the subject, the more fake items they claimed. It's called over-claiming. It happens because our sense of knowing is based on how familiar something feels. Anything that sounds right can seem familiar. It's a nice way to test your own knowledge. When you say that you know something, ask yourself whether you could explain it, and not just recognize it.
