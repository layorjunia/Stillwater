# The whole is more than the parts
@src Wertheimer, Psychologische Forschung, 1923

In the early twentieth century, a group of German psychologists, including Max Wertheimer, Wolfgang Köhler and Kurt Koffka, argued that we don't see the world as a collection of separate dots and lines. We see organized wholes. They called their school Gestalt psychology, from the German word for form. Their motto was that the whole is something other than the sum of its parts.

In 1923 Wertheimer listed the principles by which we group things. Items that are close together are seen as a group, which is proximity. Items that look alike are grouped, which is similarity. We fill in gaps to see a complete shape, which is closure. We prefer smooth, continuous lines. These rules work so automatically that we don't notice them. They are used by every graphic designer.

# Movement that isn't there
@src Wertheimer, Experimental Studies on the Perception of Movement, 1912

Two lights are placed a short distance apart. If one is switched on and then off, and then the other is switched on, at the right interval, you don't see two lights flashing. You see one light moving from the first position to the second. In 1912 Max Wertheimer studied this illusion on a train, and with a device in a hotel room in Frankfurt. He called it the phi phenomenon.

The phi phenomenon is the basis of apparent motion, in film and animation. A film is made of still pictures, shown at about twenty-four frames a second, and the brain turns them into smooth movement. It showed that perception is not just a record of what hits the eye. The brain builds motion between the pictures, and that was an important piece of evidence for the Gestalt view.

# Vase or faces?
@src Rubin, Synsoplevede Figurer, 1915

You might know the picture. It's of a white vase in the middle, and, if you look at the dark areas on each side instead, two faces looking at each other. It was designed by the Danish psychologist Edgar Rubin in 1915. You can see either the vase or the faces, but not both at once. The picture flips back and forth.

Rubin used it to study figure and ground. When we look at a scene, we divide it into the object that we focus on, the figure, and the background. The figure seems to be in front, and to have a shape, while the ground seems to extend behind it. The same lines can be seen in two ways, which shows that perception is an act of interpretation. The picture is still used to teach this idea.

# The cube that flips
@src Necker, London and Edinburgh Philosophical Magazine, 1832

In 1832 the Swiss crystallographer Louis Necker was looking at drawings of crystals under a microscope. He noticed that a drawing of a transparent cube, made of lines, would sometimes appear to change its orientation. The face that seemed to be at the front turned to the back, without any change on the page. The cube is now called the Necker cube.

The drawing is ambiguous, because a flat picture of a cube can be seen in two three-dimensional ways. The brain picks one, and holds it for a few seconds, and then switches to the other. The changes happen on their own, even when you try to hold one view. It's an example of bistable perception. It shows that what we see is the brain's best guess, and that when the evidence is equal, the guess can flip.

# Stare, and the world fades
@src Troxler, 1804

Look at the cross in the middle of a page, and keep your eyes still. After a few seconds, a faint colored blur in the corner of your vision will disappear, even though it's still there. It's called Troxler fading. It was described in 1804 by the Swiss physician Ignaz Troxler. The effect is strongest in peripheral vision.

The explanation is that the brain tunes out things that don't change. Neurons in the visual system respond to change, and if an image stays fixed on the retina, their response weakens. Normally our eyes make tiny movements all the time, which refresh the image. When you hold your gaze steady, the periphery fades. It's a good demonstration of the principle that the senses are built to detect differences, not constant stimulation.

# Colors that aren't there
@src Hering, 1878; Hurvich & Jameson, Psychological Review, 1957

Stare at a picture of a green, black and yellow flag for half a minute, and then look at a white wall. You will see a red, white and blue flag. This is a negative afterimage. The cells in your retina that respond to green get tired, and when you look away, the cells for the opposite color, red, dominate.

The effect fits the opponent process theory, proposed by the German physiologist Ewald Hering in 1878. He argued that color vision is organized in opposing pairs, red against green, blue against yellow, and black against white. This explains why we never see a reddish green or a yellowish blue. In 1957 Leo Hurvich and Dorothea Jameson gave it a quantitative form. Later work showed that the opponent system is built into the nerve cells that receive the signals from the cones.

# The room that changes your size
@src Ames, 1946; Ittelson, The Ames Demonstrations in Perception, 1952

In 1946 the American scientist Adelbert Ames built a strange room. Seen through a peephole, it looks like an ordinary rectangular room. But it is actually a trapezoid. One corner is much farther away than the other, and the ceiling and floor slope. When two people stand in the corners, the person in the far corner looks like a tiny person, and the one in the near corner looks like a giant. A person walking from one corner to the other seems to grow or shrink.

The illusion works because our brains assume that rooms are rectangular, and that the walls meet at right angles. Given that assumption, the only way to explain the images is to change the sizes of people. The room is used in films, such as the Lord of the Rings, to make actors seem to be different sizes. It's evidence of how strongly our assumptions shape what we see.

# Why the Moon looks big
@src Kaufman & Rock, Scientific American, 1962; Ross & Plug, The Mystery of the Moon Illusion, 2002

The Moon looks much bigger when it's near the horizon than when it's high in the sky. But it's the same size. A photograph taken at both times shows no difference, and you can check by holding a small coin at arm's length. This moon illusion has been noted since ancient times, by Aristotle and Ptolemy.

There's no agreed explanation. One popular theory is that the sky looks like a flattened dome, so the horizon seems farther away than the zenith, and the brain scales the size accordingly. But experiments have found problems with this. Other ideas involve the cues that we use to judge size and distance, and the way the eyes are positioned. Looking at the Moon upside down, between your legs, makes the illusion smaller. It's an old puzzle that has not been fully solved.

# The grill that burns without heat
@src Thunberg, 1896; Craig & Bushnell, Science, 1994

In 1896 the Swedish physician Torsten Thunberg discovered a strange effect. If he placed a person's hand on a set of metal rods, with every other rod warm, and the ones in between cool, the person felt a burning pain. Yet each rod, on its own, would be harmless. It is called the thermal grill illusion.

In 1994 A. D. Craig and Catherine Bushnell studied it in the laboratory. They found that the burning sensation arose from a clash between the signals in the pathways that carry warmth, cold and pain. When both warm and cold nerve fibers are active, the brain's circuits for pain are fooled. It suggests that pain is not just a direct report of damage. It's a construct that comes from combining several signals, and can be created when they conflict.

# Supertasters
@src Bartoshuk, Duffy & Miller, Physiology & Behavior, 1994

Some people find broccoli and black coffee intensely bitter, and others find them mild. In the 1990s Linda Bartoshuk, a psychologist at Yale, found that about a quarter of people are supertasters. They have more taste buds on the tongue than average, and they find bitter tastes, such as of a chemical called PROP, much stronger. About a quarter are nontasters, who find it nearly tasteless, and half are in between.

Supertasters often dislike bitter vegetables, and tend to find spicy food, fat and alcohol more intense. Some studies link it to eating fewer vegetables. It is partly genetic, and is more common in women. Children are often more sensitive than adults. If you hate Brussels sprouts, it may be that your tongue is more sensitive. It's a reminder that we may not experience the same food in the same way.

# Why cilantro tastes like soap to some people
@src Eriksson et al., 2012

Many people love the fresh herb cilantro, also called coriander. Some people say that it tastes like soap, or like bugs. It's one of the most divisive foods. In 2012 Nicholas Eriksson and his colleagues at the genetic testing company 23andMe asked thousands of people about their reactions, and compared them with their DNA.

They found a variation near a gene called OR6A2, which codes for a receptor in the nose that is sensitive to aldehydes, chemicals that are found in cilantro and in some soaps. People with a certain version were more likely to say that it tasted like soap. The link is not very strong, and it's likely that other genes and experiences matter too. For some, repeated exposure makes the taste more tolerable. The effect varies between populations.

# The fruit that makes lemons sweet
@src Kurihara & Beidler, Science, 1968

A small red berry from West Africa, called the miracle fruit, has a very unusual effect. If you chew it, and then eat something sour, like a lemon or vinegar, it tastes sweet. The effect lasts for half an hour or so. The people of West Africa have long used it to sweeten sour foods and drinks.

In the late 1960s Kenzo Kurihara and Lloyd Beidler found that the active substance is a protein, now called miraculin. It sticks to the sweet receptors on the tongue. In a neutral mouth, it does nothing. But when acid is present, it changes shape, and activates the receptors, so the acid is tasted as sweet. The berries have been used to help people undergoing chemotherapy, whose sense of taste is altered. It's a good case of how taste depends on the receptors and on chemistry.

# A thousand kinds of smell receptor
@src Buck & Axel, Cell, 1991

For a long time, no one knew how the nose detects smells. In 1991 Linda Buck and Richard Axel, working at Columbia University, found a large family of genes that code for odor receptors. In mice, there are about a thousand of them, and it's the largest gene family in the mammal genome. Each receptor is found on a different type of nerve cell in the nose.

A single odor molecule can activate several receptors, and each receptor responds to several odors. The brain recognizes a smell by the pattern of activation, like a chord on a piano. Humans have about four hundred working receptor genes. Buck and Axel shared the Nobel Prize in 2004. The discovery explained how we can tell apart thousands of different smells, with a limited number of receptors, and why some people cannot smell certain things.

# Does smell guide choice of a mate?
@src Wedekind, Seebeck, Bettens & Paepke, Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 1995
@conf mixed

In 1995 the Swiss biologist Claus Wedekind asked men to wear the same cotton T-shirt for two nights, without using any scented products. Then he asked women to smell the shirts, and rate them for pleasantness. The women also had their genes tested. They were particularly interested in the genes for the immune system, the major histocompatibility complex.

The women tended to prefer the smell of shirts worn by men whose immune genes differed from their own. It was suggested that smell helps us to choose partners whose children would have a wider range of immune defenses. The study was small, and later work has been mixed, with some studies finding the same, and others not. The effect, if it exists, appears to be weak. The idea that humans have a hidden chemical sense of compatibility remains unproven.

# Sleep paralysis
@src Sharpless & Barber, Sleep Medicine Reviews, 2011

Some people have woken up and found that they can't move, or speak. They are conscious, and feel a pressure on the chest. They may see or hear something frightening, such as a shadowy figure in the room. It lasts a few seconds, or a few minutes, and then ends. This is sleep paralysis, and it's far more common than most people think. About one in twelve people has had it.

During dream sleep, the brain keeps the muscles from moving, so that we don't act out our dreams. In sleep paralysis, the person wakes up while this paralysis is still in effect. The hallucinations are dreams intruding on waking. It's linked to irregular sleep, stress, and sleeping on the back. In folklore around the world, it has been blamed on demons, witches, and a visiting Old Hag. Knowing that it's harmless can make it less frightening.

# Signals from inside a dream
@src LaBerge, Nagel, Dement & Zarcone, Perceptual and Motor Skills, 1981

A lucid dream is a dream in which you know you're dreaming, and may be able to control it. For years, scientists doubted that they happened. In 1981 Stephen LaBerge at Stanford found a way to prove it. He knew that in dream sleep, the eyes move, while the rest of the body is paralyzed. So he asked lucid dreamers to move their eyes in a pattern, left, right, left, right, as a signal when they realized that they were dreaming.

In the sleep lab, the researchers saw the signals on the recording of the eye movements, while the person was in dream sleep. The dreamers confirmed later that they had been lucid. The experiment showed that a sleeper could send a message from inside a dream. People can learn to have lucid dreams through techniques such as checking whether they are dreaming during the day. It's used for research on dreams, and to treat nightmares.

# Babies hear every language
@src Werker & Tees, Infant Behavior and Development, 1984

A newborn can tell the sounds of any language in the world apart. In 1984 Janet Werker and Richard Tees tested babies from English-speaking families. They played them speech sounds from Hindi and from a Native American language, Nlaka'pamux, that English speakers find hard to distinguish. The six-month-old babies could hear the difference.

By the age of ten to twelve months, the babies had lost the ability. They had become attuned to the sounds of their own language, and tuned out the ones that don't matter. Babies raised in Hindi-speaking homes kept the ability. The brain prunes what it doesn't use. It's the reason that adults find it so hard to hear and say some sounds of a foreign language, and why early exposure helps.

# Babies learn language from people
@src Kuhl, Tsao & Liu, PNAS, 2003

In 2003 Patricia Kuhl and her colleagues tested whether babies can learn a foreign language just by hearing it. American babies of nine months were given twelve sessions of play with a native speaker of Mandarin Chinese, who read to them and talked to them. Another group heard the same material, but from a recording, or in a video. A third group had no Mandarin.

The babies who played with the live speaker learned to tell apart Mandarin sounds, as well as babies in Taiwan. The babies who saw or heard the recording learned nothing, as if they had never heard it. A person's presence, and the give and take of interaction, seem to be necessary. It's a reminder that language is learned in social situations, and not just by listening. A baby learns from people.

# A song of joy, in any culture
@src Fritz et al., Current Biology, 2009

Does music convey the same emotions to everyone? In 2009 Thomas Fritz and his colleagues went to Cameroon, to visit the Mafa, a people who live in the mountains in the north of the country, and who had no exposure to Western music, radio or television. They played the Mafa piano music, which was written to sound happy, sad or scary, and asked them to point to a face that matched.

The Mafa picked the right emotion more often than chance, for all three. The fast, major-key pieces sounded happy, and the slow, low pieces sounded sad. Western listeners did the same for Mafa music. It suggests that some emotional cues in music, such as tempo and loudness, are universal. Others, such as the idea that minor keys are sad, may be learned. Music is partly universal and partly cultural.

# Newborns find the beat
@src Winkler, Háden, Ladinig, Sziller & Honing, PNAS, 2009

Do we learn rhythm, or are we born with it? In 2009 István Winkler and his colleagues in Hungary and the Netherlands tested newborn babies, who were two or three days old. They played them a rock drum pattern, with a bass drum on the downbeat, and other drums in between. Sometimes, the downbeat was left out. They recorded electrical activity from the babies' brains.

When the beat was missing, the babies' brains showed a response, a sign of surprise. They seemed to expect the beat. It suggests that the ability to sense a regular pulse is present at birth, and doesn't need to be learned. Rhythm is basic to music and dance, and to speech. The study also gives a hint of why babies like to be rocked and sung to.

# Is harmony universal?
@src McDermott, Schultz, Undurraga & Godoy, Nature, 2016

Western listeners generally find some combinations of notes pleasant, such as a major chord, and others harsh, such as notes a semitone apart. It's often said that this preference is built into the brain, because the sounds of the pleasant chords have a simple mathematical relationship. In 2016 Josh McDermott and his colleagues tested the idea with the Tsimane', an Amazonian people who live in villages in Bolivia, with little exposure to Western music.

The Tsimane' showed no preference for consonant chords over dissonant ones. They rated them about the same. They did, however, show a preference for sounds that were not rough, as Westerners do. It suggests that the liking of consonance is learned, through exposure to music in which it's common, and not purely biological. It's a case where a feature that seemed universal turned out to be cultural.

# The spotlight of attention
@src Posner, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1980

In 1980 Michael Posner published a simple experiment that changed the study of attention. People looked at a dot in the center of a screen, and pressed a key as soon as a light appeared on the left or the right. Before each light, an arrow gave a hint about where it would appear. Most of the time, the arrow was right.

When the hint was correct, people responded faster. When it was wrong, they were slower. Their eyes stayed fixed on the dot, so it wasn't a matter of looking. Attention can be moved to a place without moving the eyes. Posner compared it to a spotlight, which illuminates one area and leaves the rest in the dark. The task is still widely used. It's the basis for much of what we know about how attention works in the brain.
