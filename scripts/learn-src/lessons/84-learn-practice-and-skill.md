# The plateau in learning
@src Bryan & Harter, Psychological Review, 1897 and 1899

In the 1890s two psychologists at Indiana University, William Lowe Bryan and Noble Harter, studied how telegraph operators learned to send and receive Morse code. They collected records of the speed of learners over many weeks. The curve of progress did not rise smoothly. It rose quickly at first, and then flattened for a while, before rising again. The flat stretch is called a plateau.

The researchers suggested that the plateau occurs when a learner has mastered the individual letters, and has to start to learn whole words and phrases. The brain has to reorganize its skills at a higher level, and that takes time, without visible progress. It's a good thing to know when you are learning a skill, such as a language or an instrument. A plateau is not a sign of failure. It's often a stage before a new level.

# The curve of practice
@src Newell & Rosenbloom, Cognitive Skills and Their Acquisition, 1981; Crossman, Ergonomics, 1959

How quickly do we improve with practice? In 1959 Edward Crossman studied cigar makers in a factory in Cuba. He had records of how many cigars each worker made per hour, over years. They improved rapidly in the first weeks, and then more slowly, but they kept improving for years. It was a smooth curve, with each doubling of practice producing a similar proportional gain in speed.

In 1981 Allen Newell and Paul Rosenbloom showed that the same pattern fits many skills, from typing to reading mirror writing to solving problems. It's called the power law of practice. The early gains are big, and later gains are small but continue. It tells us that to keep making progress at an advanced level, much more practice is needed. And it suggests that a beginner shouldn't be discouraged. The first few hours bring the biggest returns.

# Practicing at different distances
@src Kerr & Booth, Perceptual and Motor Skills, 1978

In 1978 Robert Kerr and Bernard Booth tested a simple idea about practice. Children of eight and twelve years old learned to throw a beanbag at a target on the floor. All of them practiced the same number of throws. One group threw from a distance of three feet. The other group threw from two feet and four feet, but never from three.

After twelve weeks, they were tested at three feet. The children who had practiced at two other distances did better than those who had practiced at three, the one in the test. They were also better when they were given a new task. The variety seems to teach the learner a general rule, and not just a single response. When practicing a skill, such as a tennis serve or a speech, it helps to vary the conditions.

# Random practice
@src Shea & Morgan, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory, 1979

In 1979 John Shea and Robyn Morgan had people learn three different arm movements, each of which involved knocking down a set of wooden barriers as fast as possible. One group practiced in blocks, with one movement repeated many times, and then another. The other group practiced in a random order, with a different movement each time.

During practice, the blocked group did better and improved faster. The random group seemed to be doing worse. But in a test ten days later, the results were reversed. The random group performed better, and especially in a new situation. This is called contextual interference. Switching tasks makes practice harder, which forces the learner to retrieve the plan each time, and that builds a stronger memory. It's the same principle as interleaving in study.

# Less feedback, better learning
@src Winstein & Schmidt, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 1990

You would think that a learner should get feedback after every attempt. In 1990 Carolee Winstein and Richard Schmidt tested this. People learned to move a lever in a particular pattern, and were told after each trial how close they were. One group got feedback after every trial. Another group got it on only half of the trials, chosen at random.

During practice, the group with constant feedback did a little better. But in a test the next day, without any feedback, the group that had been given feedback half of the time did better. When feedback is given every time, the learner may come to depend on it, and not learn to judge their own performance. Self-assessment is part of the skill. A coach who talks less may be better than one who comments on everything.

# Think about the effect, not the body
@src Wulf, International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology, 2013; Chua et al., Psychological Bulletin, 2021

When a golfer is learning to swing, should they think about the movement of their arms, or about where they want the ball to go? Gabriele Wulf, a researcher in motor learning, has studied this question for over twenty years. She has found that people perform better and learn faster when their attention is directed to the effect of the movement on the environment, such as the path of a club, than when it's directed to their own body.

The instruction to think about the arms is called an internal focus. The instruction to think about the club, or the target, is an external focus. In dozens of studies, from basketball to balance to singing, the external focus has helped. A meta-analysis in 2021 confirmed the effect. The reason may be that thinking about the body interferes with automatic control. It's a practical tip for any skill that is physical.

# Does chess make you smarter?
@src Sala & Gobet, Educational Research Review, 2016
@conf mixed

Chess is often promoted as a way of improving children's thinking, and it has been introduced in schools in many countries. In 2016 Giovanni Sala and Fernand Gobet combined the results of twenty-four studies of chess instruction in schools. They looked at whether it improved math, reading and general thinking skills.

They found modest effects on math, reading and thinking skills, but the effects were smaller in studies that used a good control group, in which the children in the comparison group had other activities of equal interest. The authors concluded that there is some benefit, but that it is probably due to the extra attention or the motivation, and not to a special effect of chess. It's another example of a failure of far transfer.

# Is practice everything?
@src Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, Psychological Science, 2014
@conf mixed

The idea that anyone can become an expert with enough practice has been popular. In 2014 Brooke Macnamara, David Hambrick and Frederick Oswald combined the results of eighty-eight studies of the relationship between deliberate practice and performance, in music, games, sports, education and professions.

They found that practice mattered a lot, but not as much as often claimed. It accounted for about a quarter of the differences in performance in games like chess, about a fifth in music, and about a fifth in sports. In education, it explained only about four percent, and in professions less than one percent. Much of the variation was due to other factors, such as when people began, their abilities, and their situations. Practice is necessary, but it's not the whole story.

# The sight-reader's memory
@src Meinz & Hambrick, Psychological Science, 2010

Pianists differ in how well they can play a piece of music at first sight. It's a skill that depends on practice, and on other things. In 2010 Elizabeth Meinz and David Hambrick tested a group of pianists, ranging from beginners to advanced. They measured how much they had practiced, how well they sight-read, and their working memory, the capacity to hold information in mind.

Practice was a strong predictor, and accounted for about half of the differences in sight-reading. But working memory made an independent contribution, of about seven percent. Among people with the same amount of practice, those with a bigger working memory did better, even at the highest levels of practice. The study suggests that talent has a role, and that even in a skill that depends on practice, individual abilities matter.

# How long to master chess?
@src Gobet & Campitelli, Educational Psychology, 2007

The ten thousand hours rule says that it takes about ten thousand hours of practice to become an expert. In 2007 Fernand Gobet and Guillermo Campitelli tested the rule with chess players. They surveyed the hours of serious practice that players had put in before they reached master level, in Argentina and in Britain.

The average was around eleven thousand hours, but the variation was huge. Some of the players reached master level in about three thousand hours, and others had practiced for more than twenty-three thousand hours and had still not got there. Some players never reached the level, despite many years of practice. There's no magic number. Practice matters a great deal, but people differ in how quickly they improve.
