# The ticket you paid for
@src Arkes & Blumer, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 1985

In 1985 Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer ran a field experiment at a university theater. People buying season tickets were randomly given the full price, a discount, or a bigger discount, without knowing that the price had been varied. Then the researchers counted how many plays each group attended.

In the first half of the season, the people who had paid the full price went to more plays than those who had paid less. Their money was spent, and they didn't want to waste it. Later in the season, the difference faded. This is the sunk cost effect. What you've already spent, and can't get back, should not affect what you do next, but it does. The sensible question is not what have I put in. It is, from here, what is the best use of my time and money?

# The bullet holes that weren't there
@src Wald, 1943; Mangel & Samaniego, JASA, 1984

During the Second World War, military analysts studied the bombers that came back from missions. They plotted where the planes had been hit by bullets, and the obvious plan was to add armor to the places with the most holes. The statistician Abraham Wald saw the flaw. These were the planes that had survived.

The places without holes were where a hit was fatal, because the planes that were hit there never made it home. So the armor belonged on those parts, such as the engines. This is survivorship bias, which means looking only at the cases that made it through and forgetting those that didn't. It's behind many misleading lessons, like studying successful companies for what makes them successful when the failed ones did the same things.

# The pilots who got praised
@src Galton, 1886; Kahneman & Tversky, 1973

Daniel Kahneman once taught flight instructors in the Israeli air force. They told him that when they praised a cadet after a very good landing, the next landing was usually worse, and when they scolded one after a bad landing, the next was usually better. They concluded that scolding works and praise doesn't.

Kahneman saw that the pattern could be explained by chance. Any performance has some luck in it. A very good landing is likely to be followed by an average one, and a very poor landing is likely to be followed by an average one, whatever anyone says. Francis Galton had named this effect in the 1880s, regression to the mean. It's easy to miss. We invent causes for what is just a return to normal, and it leads us to credit a cure for an illness that was about to improve anyway.

# Berkeley and the missing women
@src Bickel, Hammel & O'Connell, Science, 1975

In 1973 the University of California at Berkeley looked at its graduate admissions for signs of bias against women. The overall numbers looked damning. Around 44 percent of the men who applied were admitted, against about 35 percent of the women. A group of statisticians, led by Peter Bickel, looked at the numbers department by department.

Within most departments, women were admitted at about the same rate as men or at higher rates. The reason was that women had applied in greater numbers to departments that were hard to get into, such as English, and men had applied more to departments with high acceptance rates. This is called Simpson's paradox, where a trend in the whole disappears or reverses when the data is split into groups. It's a warning that a single average can hide what's really happening.

# Linda the bank teller
@src Tversky & Kahneman, Psychological Review, 1983

In 1983 Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman described a woman named Linda. She was thirty-one, single, outspoken and bright, and she had studied philosophy and had taken part in protests as a student. People were asked which was more likely. Was Linda a bank teller, or was Linda a bank teller who is active in the feminist movement?

Most people, including many with training in statistics, said that the second was more likely. But that can't be right. Every feminist bank teller is a bank teller, so the second group can't be larger than the first. This is the conjunction fallacy. A detailed story seems more believable than a plain one, though each added detail makes it less likely to be true. It's one reason why vivid scenarios can lead us astray.

# I knew it all along
@src Fischhoff, JEP: Human Perception and Performance, 1975

In 1975 Baruch Fischhoff gave people a passage about a little known historical event, the war between the British and the Gurkhas in the early 1800s. Some of them were told how it ended, and the others weren't. Then they were asked how likely they would have thought each possible outcome was, if they hadn't known the answer.

The people who knew the result thought that it had been much more likely than the people who didn't. And they didn't realize that knowing the outcome had changed their view. This is hindsight bias. After something happens, it seems obvious. It makes us overconfident in our ability to predict, and it makes us too hard on people who made decisions before the result was known. Writing down your predictions beforehand is a good defense.

# The planning fallacy
@src Buehler, Griffin & Ross, JPSP, 1994

Why do projects take longer than we expect? In 1994 Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross asked students writing their final theses to predict when they would finish. On average they predicted about 34 days. They actually took about 55 days. Only around a third finished by the date that they predicted.

The problem wasn't a lack of experience. Even when they were asked for their worst case, students' estimates were too optimistic. We tend to picture how a plan will go if all goes well, and we underweight the interruptions and delays that always arise. The authors called it the planning fallacy. A good fix is to look at how long similar projects have taken in the past, and to start from that, and not from your own picture of the plan.

# Imagine it has already failed
@src Mitchell, Russo & Pennington, 1989; Klein, Harvard Business Review, 2007

How do you find the weak spots in a plan that you're excited about? In 1989 Deborah Mitchell, J. Edward Russo and Nancy Pennington found that people came up with more reasons for an outcome if they imagined that it had already happened. Asked to explain a future event as if it were a fact, they generated about thirty percent more reasons than when they were asked to consider what might happen.

Gary Klein turned this into a business practice in 2007, called the pre-mortem. Before a project begins, the team imagines that it has failed badly, and each person writes down why. It gives people permission to raise worries without seeming negative, and it surfaces risks that the group has been quietly sensing. It costs about ten minutes and may save months of trouble.

# Judging by the outcome
@src Baron & Hershey, JPSP, 1988

Suppose that a surgeon recommends an operation with a small risk, and the patient dies. Was it a bad decision? In 1988 Jonathan Baron and John Hershey gave people stories about decisions with the same information but different outcomes. They found that people rated the decision as worse when the outcome was bad, though the decision-maker had known no more or less in either case.

This is outcome bias. A good result can come from a poor decision, and a bad result can come from a good one. Chance plays a big part. If we judge only by results, we may reward people for luck and punish them for bad breaks, and we learn the wrong lessons. A better question is whether the choice was reasonable given what was known at the time.

# Doubling a tiny risk
@src Gigerenzer et al., Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2007

In October 1995 British health authorities warned that a type of contraceptive pill, called third generation, doubled the risk of blood clots compared with older pills. The news was widely reported. Many women stopped taking it. In the following year, the numbers of conceptions and abortions rose, and so did births.

The warning was about relative risk. Doubling a very small risk gives a still small risk, and the extra clots were a handful of cases per ten thousand women, while an unplanned pregnancy is a much more common event. Later studies suggested that the increase in risk was smaller than first thought. Gerd Gigerenzer and others argue that health information should be given in absolute terms, such as how many people out of a thousand, and not as percentages of change.

# Doctors and the mammogram question
@src Gigerenzer et al., Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2007

Here is a question that was put to 160 gynecologists. About one percent of women who are screened have breast cancer. A mammogram will find about nine in ten of those who do. It also gives a false alarm in about nine percent of those who don't. A woman has a positive result. What's the chance that she has cancer?

The correct answer is about one in ten. The most common answer from the doctors was ninety percent. When the same information was given as natural frequencies, such as ten women out of a thousand have cancer, nine of them test positive, and about eighty-nine out of the rest also test positive, most could see that the answer is about nine out of ninety-eight. The way that numbers are presented changes how well even experts reason about them.

# The studies that didn't repeat
@src Open Science Collaboration, Science, 2015

In 2015 a large group of researchers, called the Open Science Collaboration, tried to repeat one hundred published psychology studies. They followed the original methods as closely as they could, and used larger samples. Of the original studies, ninety-seven percent had reported statistically significant results. Of the repeats, only about thirty-six percent did.

The size of the effects also dropped, to about half of what was first reported. The result set off a period of reform in psychology, with changes such as preregistering plans, sharing data and publishing replications. It doesn't mean that psychology is worthless, and many findings held up well. It does mean that a single exciting study is not enough, and that it's wise to wait for the result to be repeated.

# The song that made people younger
@src Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn, Psychological Science, 2011

In 2011 Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson and Uri Simonsohn reported an experiment in which people who listened to the Beatles song "When I'm Sixty-Four" became nearly a year and a half younger than those who listened to a control song. The finding was statistically significant, and it was also impossible.

The authors had done it on purpose. They wanted to show how researchers can get a false result from ordinary choices, such as stopping data collection when the result looks good, trying a few different ways of analyzing the data, or dropping a condition. Each choice seems reasonable, but together they can make a false finding more likely than not. The paper helped lead to the practice of stating your analysis in advance, so that the same data can't be made to say anything.

# The file drawer
@src Rosenthal, Psychological Bulletin, 1979; Franco, Malhotra & Simonovits, Science, 2014

Imagine twenty teams test a treatment that doesn't work. By chance, one of them gets a positive result, and publishes it. The other nineteen put their results in a drawer. Anyone reading the published literature sees one study and one success. In 1979 Robert Rosenthal called this the file drawer problem.

In 2014 Annie Franco and her colleagues tracked a group of studies from the start, using records of experiments that had been funded. They found that studies with strong results were much more likely to be written up and published than studies with null results, and that many null results were never even written up. It's a reason why published findings can overstate the truth, and it's why registered trials are valuable.

# When a measure becomes a target
@src Goodhart, 1975; Strathern, 1997

In the 1970s the economist Charles Goodhart observed that a statistical measure tends to lose its usefulness once it is used for control. The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern later put it in a form that is now well known. When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

The reason is that people adapt. If schools are judged by test scores, they may teach to the test. If hospitals are judged by waiting times, they may find ways to make the clocks look better. If a company rewards the number of calls handled, calls will get shorter. The measure and the real goal drift apart. It's why it helps to use several measures, and to ask whether a number is still telling you what you care about.

# The silver medal
@src Medvec, Madey & Gilovich, JPSP, 1995

Who is happier, an Olympic silver medalist or a bronze medalist? Logically it should be silver. In 1995 Victoria Medvec, Scott Madey and Thomas Gilovich analyzed video of athletes at the 1992 Summer Olympics, and had people rate their expressions as they received their medals.

Bronze medalists looked happier than silver medalists. The researchers suggested that the reason is who each athlete compares with. The silver medalist thinks about the gold that they just missed. The bronze medalist thinks about how close they came to finishing with nothing. What we feel often depends less on what we got than on what we imagine could have happened.

# How does a zipper work?
@src Rozenblit & Keil, Cognitive Science, 2002

Rate how well you understand how a zipper works, or a flush toilet, or a cylinder lock. Most people give themselves a middling to high score. Now write out the explanation, step by step. In 2002 Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil did just that, and found that people's ratings of their understanding dropped, often sharply, after they tried to explain.

They called this the illusion of explanatory depth. We feel that we understand things because we're familiar with them and can use them, and we mistake that for knowing how they work. Trying to explain brings the gaps to the surface. It's a handy test of your own understanding, and a good reason to be gentle with others who have strong opinions about things they have never had to explain.

# Explaining a policy
@src Fernbach et al., Psychological Science, 2013
@conf mixed

In 2013 Philip Fernbach and his colleagues asked people about political policies, such as a merit-based pay system for teachers, or a sanction on Iran. People gave their views and rated how well they understood the issue. Then some were asked to explain in detail how the policy would work, and others to give reasons why they supported it.

Those who tried to explain how the policy worked rated their own understanding lower, and their opinions became less extreme. Giving reasons did not do the same. The idea is that having to explain exposes the illusion that we know. Some later attempts to repeat the effect have found it smaller or less consistent. But it points to a good question to ask, in a debate or in your own head. Instead of asking why do you believe that, ask how would it work?

# Defending a choice you never made
@src Johansson, Hall, Sikström & Olsson, Science, 2005

In 2005 Petter Johansson and his colleagues showed people pairs of photographs of faces and asked them to choose the more attractive one. Then the experimenter handed over the chosen photo and asked the person to say why they preferred it. On some trials, the experimenter secretly swapped it for the other photo, using a bit of sleight of hand.

Only about a quarter of these swaps were noticed. Most people accepted the face they hadn't chosen, and gave reasons for liking it, such as saying that they liked her smile. The effect is called choice blindness. It suggests that we don't always have access to the reasons for our choices, and that we are good at making them up afterward. What we say about why we did something may be an explanation and not a record.

# The winner's curse
@src Bazerman & Samuelson, Journal of Conflict Resolution, 1983

In the 1980s Max Bazerman and William Samuelson ran auctions in classrooms. They put jars of coins on display, such as 800 pennies, and students bid for them. The coins were worth eight dollars. Each student had their own estimate of the amount, some of them too high and some too low.

The winner was nearly always the person with the highest estimate. As a result, the winning bid averaged more than ten dollars, so the winners lost money. This is the winner's curse. In any auction where an item has the same unknown value for everyone, the highest bidder is probably the one who has overestimated the most. Oil companies had seen the same effect in real auctions for drilling rights. The remedy is to bid less than your own estimate, and to bid lower when there are more bidders.

# Clever Hans
@src Pfungst, Clever Hans, 1907

In the early 1900s a horse in Berlin, called Clever Hans, became famous. His owner said that he could do arithmetic. Asked to add three and four, Hans would tap his hoof seven times. A commission of experts investigated and found no sign of fraud, and the owner seemed to be honest.

The psychologist Oskar Pfungst then tested him more carefully. Hans could only answer correctly when the questioner knew the answer and could be seen. Without that, he failed. The horse was reading small, unconscious movements in the questioner's face and posture, which told him when to stop tapping. The case is a classic lesson about experimenter effects. It's the reason why good studies are blinded, so that the people running them can't pass on cues without knowing it.

# Twelve sailors and the scurvy
@src Lind, A Treatise of the Scurvy, 1753

In 1747 the Scottish naval surgeon James Lind took twelve sailors with scurvy on a ship at sea. He divided them into six pairs, and gave each pair a different treatment. The treatments were cider, a mixture of acids, vinegar, seawater, a paste of spices, and two oranges and a lemon a day.

The two sailors who got the citrus fruit recovered quickly, and one was fit for duty within about a week. The others improved little. It was a small trial, and it didn't cure the practice of the navy overnight, but it is often called one of the first controlled trials. Lind kept the groups alike in other ways and compared the treatments side by side. It's the basic idea behind a modern experiment.

# The law of small numbers
@src Tversky & Kahneman, Psychological Bulletin, 1971

In 1971 Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman asked people, including trained scientists, questions about samples. They found that people expect even small samples to look like the population they come from. A handful of results is expected to show the same pattern as a big set.

They called it the belief in the law of small numbers, a joke on the real law of large numbers. In small samples, chance produces many odd results. A study of twenty people can easily show something that isn't there, or miss something that is. The scientists in the study also set sample sizes too small, and they were too confident in results from small studies. It's a good reason to be wary of any conclusion based on a few cases.
