# Intellectual humility
@src Leary et al., Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2017

Intellectual humility means recognizing that your beliefs might be wrong and that your knowledge has limits. In 2017 Mark Leary and his colleagues built a questionnaire to measure it, with statements like "I question my own opinions" and "I accept that my beliefs could be mistaken." Then they tested what the scores went along with.

People who were higher in intellectual humility were less likely to overestimate how much they knew. They paid more attention to the quality of an argument, and less to whether it agreed with them. And they were more open to hearing views that were different from their own. Humility here isn't weakness or a lack of conviction. It's the habit of holding a belief firmly enough to act on it, and loosely enough to change it.

# Open-minded thinking and better forecasts
@src Haran, Ritov & Mellers, Judgment and Decision Making, 2013

In 2013 Uriel Haran, Ilana Ritov and Barbara Mellers wanted to know which habits of mind make people good at estimating things. They tested several candidates, including need for cognition, grit, and the tendency to look for the best option rather than a good enough one. Across three studies, only one of them predicted accuracy.

It was actively open-minded thinking, the habit of looking for evidence that goes against your view, taking other people's opinions seriously, and revising your beliefs. The reason seemed to be that open-minded people gathered more information before deciding. They kept looking. This fits with the findings on top forecasters, who treat their beliefs as guesses to test. It's a mindset that can be practiced by asking, before deciding, what would change my mind?

# Consider the opposite
@src Lord, Lepper & Preston, JPSP, 1984

People who hold strong views tend to read evidence in a lopsided way. They accept studies that support their side and pick holes in those that don't. In 1984 Charles Lord, Mark Lepper and Elizabeth Preston asked whether a simple instruction could reduce the bias. They gave people evidence on the death penalty, and told some of them to be as fair and unbiased as they could.

That instruction did little. But another instruction worked better. When people were asked to consider at each step how they would have reacted if the very same study had come out on the other side, their bias shrank. Telling people to be fair was not enough, but giving them a specific mental step was. It's a technique you can use on yourself, whenever you meet a result that you like.

# Reasons you might be wrong
@src Koriat, Lichtenstein & Fischhoff, JEP: Human Learning and Memory, 1980

Most people are overconfident. When they say that they are ninety percent sure, they're right a lot less than nine times in ten. In 1980 Asher Koriat, Sarah Lichtenstein and Baruch Fischhoff tried to find out why, and whether it could be fixed. They had people answer general knowledge questions, and say how confident they were in each answer.

When people were asked to write down a reason why their answer might be wrong, before giving their confidence, they became better calibrated. Listing reasons in favor didn't help. It seems that we usually think only of supporting reasons, and so we feel more sure than we should. A single question, "what is one reason I could be wrong?", is a cheap and effective way of making yourself less overconfident.

# Weather forecasters know how sure to be
@src Murphy & Winkler, Applied Statistics, 1977

Most professions are poor at knowing how right they are. Weather forecasters are an exception. In 1977 Allan Murphy and Robert Winkler studied American forecasters' predictions of rain. When forecasters said that there was a seventy percent chance of rain, it rained on about seventy percent of those days. When they said twenty percent, it rained about a fifth of the time.

The forecasters are well calibrated, and the reason is practice with feedback. They make many predictions about the same sort of event, and they find out right away whether they were right. They also keep score, and use formal rules for stating probabilities. Most of us make predictions rarely, and seldom check them. The lesson is that calibration can be learned. If you write down your forecasts and the odds, and then look back, you improve.

# People who like to think
@src Cacioppo & Petty, JPSP, 1982

Some people enjoy thinking hard and others avoid it. In 1982 John Cacioppo and Richard Petty gave this trait a name, the need for cognition, and designed a questionnaire to measure it. It includes statements such as "I prefer complex to simple problems" and "Thinking is not my idea of fun."

People who scored high spent more effort on arguments and were more persuaded by good ones, whereas those who scored low were swayed by cues such as the speaker's looks or the number of arguments. The trait is not intelligence. It is a matter of how much you choose to engage. Everyone can raise or lower their effort depending on the situation, and noticing which mode you're in is part of thinking about thinking.

# Studying what is just out of reach
@src Metcalfe & Kornell, JEP: General, 2003

When you have a lot to learn, where should you spend your time? In 2003 Janet Metcalfe and Nate Kornell watched how adults used time to study lists of vocabulary items. The words varied in difficulty. People spent less time on the easy ones, which they knew, and also less on the hardest ones.

They put their time into the items that were closest to being learned, those in what Metcalfe calls the region of proximal learning. It's an efficient strategy for the most part, because the items just out of reach pay off best for the time spent. But it needs accurate judgments of what you know. If you wrongly think you know something, you'll skip it. It's another reason to test yourself before deciding what to study next.

# How experts attack a math problem
@src Schoenfeld, Mathematical Problem Solving, 1985

In the 1980s the mathematician Alan Schoenfeld videotaped students and professors working on unfamiliar problems. A typical student read the problem, picked an approach in under a minute, and stuck with it, often for the whole time, even when it wasn't working. Most never stopped to ask whether they were making progress.

Mathematicians behaved differently. They spent time making sense of the problem, and they paused regularly to ask what they were doing, why, and how it would help. If it wasn't working, they changed course. Schoenfeld argued that this self-monitoring was the main difference, more than knowledge of facts. He taught his students to ask these questions out loud, and their problem solving improved.

# The cycle of a good learner
@src Zimmerman, Theory Into Practice, 2002

In 2002 the educational psychologist Barry Zimmerman summarized decades of research on students who learn well on their own. He described a cycle with three phases. Before a task, they set a goal, make a plan and think about whether they can do it. During the task, they keep their attention on it and watch their progress. Afterward, they judge how it went and decide what to change.

Students who do well tend to go through the whole cycle, while weaker students often skip the first and last steps, going straight in and moving on afterward. The cycle repeats, so each round of reflection improves the next plan. The model is a practical outline. Before you study, ask what you want to accomplish. During, check whether it's working. After, ask what you will do differently.

# Friends know some things better
@src Vazire, JPSP, 2010

Who knows you best, you or the people close to you? In 2010 Simine Vazire proposed that it depends on the trait. For things that are mostly inside, such as how anxious or worried you feel, you have an advantage, because other people can't see it. For traits that are visible and loaded with value, such as how smart, talented or likeable you are, other people may be more accurate.

The reason is that we have a hard time being objective about the traits we most want to be good at. A friend can see how you behave, without the wish to look good. The idea has support from many studies of self and peer ratings. It suggests a practical step. If you want to know how you come across, or how good you are at something, ask a few people you trust, and listen.

# Trusting your own mind more than your actions
@src Pronin & Kugler, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 2007

When we judge other people, we look at what they do. When we judge ourselves, we look at what's going on inside our heads. In 2007 Emily Pronin and Matthew Kugler called this the introspection illusion. We think that our own thoughts and motives are a window into what we are really like, and that people can't see into us as well as we can see into ourselves.

In their studies, people said that they should rely on introspection when deciding whether they were biased, but they didn't think that it was a good guide for others. Since many biases work outside awareness, looking inside says little. People only admitted their own biases after learning that introspection can be wrong. It's a good reason to treat your feeling of being fair as weak evidence, and to look at your behavior as well.

# Naive physics
@src McCloskey, Caramazza & Green, Science, 1980

Imagine a ball rolling through a curved tube lying flat on a table. When it comes out at the end, what path does it take? In 1980 Michael McCloskey and his colleagues asked college students, and a good many of them drew a curved path. In fact, the ball moves in a straight line, since nothing is pushing it sideways.

The students had learned physics, but they still held an older intuition, that a moving object keeps a memory of the push that made it curve. McCloskey called these beliefs naive physics. They resemble ideas held in medieval times, before Newton. It shows that learning a formula doesn't always replace an old model in our minds. To change a mental model, we usually need to test it against the facts and see it fail.

# To fix a misconception, face it
@src Guzzetti et al., Reading Research Quarterly, 1993

How do you get someone to give up a wrong idea? In 1993 Barbara Guzzetti and her colleagues combed through studies of science teaching, looking for what changed students' misconceptions. A clear winner was something called the refutation text. It states the common misconception, says that it is wrong, and then explains the correct idea.

A text that gives only the correct idea tended to work less well, because the old idea stays in place alongside it. Seeing the wrong idea named, and then seeing why it fails, seems to help people replace it. It's a good tip for teaching, and also for correcting yourself. If you want to unlearn something, it helps to write down the wrong version and why it doesn't work, and not just the right one.

# The feeling of rightness
@src Thompson, Prowse Turner & Pennycook, Cognitive Psychology, 2011

When you give a quick answer to a problem, how do you decide whether to stop or think again? In 2011 Valerie Thompson and her colleagues asked people to give their first answer to a reasoning problem, and then rate how right it felt. They then gave them time to reconsider.

When the first answer felt very right, people spent little time on it and seldom changed it. When it felt less right, they thought longer and changed their answers more often. The feeling of rightness works as a signal to the slower part of the mind. The trouble is that wrong answers can feel right, especially when they are easy to come up with. Knowing that the feeling is a signal, and not a proof, is a way of catching errors.

# We prefer the worse way to study
@src Kornell & Bjork, Psychological Science, 2008

In 2008 Nate Kornell and Robert Bjork had people learn to recognize the styles of different painters. Some people studied each artist's paintings in a block. Others saw the paintings of different artists mixed together. The mixed order is harder, since you keep switching between styles. Afterward, they tested everyone on new paintings by the same artists.

People who studied the mixed order did better. But most people, in a questionnaire afterward, said that the block was as good as or better than the mixed. They believed in the method that felt easier, and which was less effective. We tend to judge our learning by how smooth it feels in the moment. When a method feels hard, it may be working. It helps to look at results, not at feelings.

# A smooth lecture feels like learning
@src Carpenter, Wilford, Kornell & Mullaney, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 2013

In 2013 Shana Carpenter and her colleagues showed students two versions of a video lecture on a scientific topic. In one, the lecturer spoke smoothly, with eye contact and no notes. In the other, the lecturer hesitated, looked down and used notes. The content was identical. Afterward, they asked the students how much they thought they'd learned, and tested them.

The students who watched the fluent lecturer thought that they had learned more. But there was no difference in how much they remembered. The fluency of the speaker had changed their feeling of learning and not their learning. It's a reminder that a good presenter can make material seem easy, and that a hesitant one doesn't mean poor content. The test of whether you have learned is what you can do afterward.

# The doorway effect
@src Radvansky, Krawietz & Tamplin, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 2011
@conf mixed

You walk into a room to get something, and when you arrive you can't remember what. In 2011 Gabriel Radvansky and his colleagues tested whether doorways are to blame. People did tasks in a virtual world or the real one, carrying objects, and had to remember what they were carrying. They forgot more after passing through a doorway than after walking the same distance within a room.

The proposed explanation is that the mind files experiences into events, and a doorway marks the end of one. Information from the previous room becomes harder to reach. Some later attempts to repeat the effect have found it to be weaker or inconsistent. But it's a pleasing idea, and it suggests a simple tip. If you've forgotten, go back to the place where you first thought of it.

# Why talking it over helps
@src Smith et al., Science, 2009

In large university courses, instructors often ask a question and have students vote on it, then discuss it with a neighbor, and then vote again. The second vote is usually better. Is this because the students who know the answer persuade the rest? In 2009 Michelle Smith and her colleagues tested it in a genetics course.

After discussion, students answered a new question on the same idea, alone. Their scores went up, and they went up even for groups in which no one had known the right answer at the start. So the students weren't simply copying the others. They were working out the idea together. Explaining and challenging each other's answers seems to produce understanding that none of them had alone. It's a good reason to study with someone.
