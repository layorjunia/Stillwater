# Facing it works
@src Foa & Kozak, Psychological Bulletin, 1986; Craske et al., 2014

Avoiding what frightens us feels safe, but the fear tends to stay. Exposure therapy goes the other way. A person faces the feared thing, such as a crowded place, a memory or a physical sensation, in small steps and with support, until the fear falls on its own. In 1986 Edna Foa and Michael Kozak described this as a process of getting new information that doesn't fit the fear.

Exposure is among the best supported treatments for phobias, panic, obsessive compulsive disorder and PTSD. More recent work by Michelle Craske and her colleagues has changed the way it's understood. The old fear isn't erased. A new memory is learned beside it, that the feared thing did not lead to disaster. That helps explain why practice in different places and at different times makes the benefits last.

# The fear of the fear
@src Clark, 1986

A panic attack can feel like a heart attack, and the first one is often terrifying. In 1986 the psychologist David Clark proposed an explanation for why some people go on to have more of them. They notice a bodily sensation, such as a racing heart or dizziness, and read it as a sign of immediate danger. The fear makes the sensation stronger, and that seems to confirm the danger.

The result is a loop, with the person afraid of the very sensations that fear produces. This helps explain why panic can seem to come from nowhere. Treatments built on this idea teach people that the sensations are harmless, and let them experience those sensations on purpose until they lose their power. The sensations are unpleasant but not dangerous in themselves, and an attack usually peaks within minutes. New chest pain is still worth having checked by a doctor.

# Safety behaviors keep fear alive
@src Salkovskis, 1991

Many anxious people have rituals that make them feel safe. They sit near the exit, carry a bottle of water, avoid eye contact, or rehearse every sentence before they speak. In 1991 Paul Salkovskis called these safety behaviors, and pointed out that they have a hidden cost. When nothing bad happens, the person credits the safety behavior and not the fact that the situation was safe.

So the belief that danger is near is never tested. Treatment often involves dropping the safety behavior and seeing what happens. A person with social anxiety might speak without rehearsing, or stay in the room without checking the exit. They find that the feared outcome mostly doesn't happen, or that they can cope with it. The crutch helps in the moment and keeps the fear in place.

# Why avoidance feels like a solution
@src Mowrer, Harvard Educational Review, 1947

In the 1940s the psychologist O. Hobart Mowrer offered a two part explanation for why fears last. The first part is that a fear is learned, when something frightening becomes linked to a place or a sign. The second is that avoiding the feared thing brings immediate relief, and relief rewards the avoidance. So the person learns to avoid it again.

This is why a fear can last for decades. Each time you steer around it, you feel better and you lose a chance to find out that the thing was not so dangerous. The relief is real, but it comes at a cost, because your world shrinks. It's also why gradual approach is the usual way out. Each small step that you take without disaster teaches you something that avoidance never can.

# The worries that never came
@src LaFreniere & Newman, Behavior Therapy, 2020
@conf mixed

People who worry a lot often feel that their worrying protects them, as though thinking about a bad thing might stop it from happening. In 2020 Lauren LaFreniere and Michelle Newman tested what happens to worries over time. In a small study of people with generalized anxiety disorder, participants wrote down what they were worried about, and then checked later what had really happened.

About nine in ten of the worries did not come true. And for many of those that did, people said that they had coped better than they had feared. Seeing this in black and white seemed to help some people worry less. The study was small, and it was about people in treatment, so it doesn't prove that worrying is always pointless. But keeping a record of your worries and checking them later is a simple test that anyone can try.

# The triad of depression
@src Beck, Depression: Clinical, Experimental and Theoretical Aspects, 1967

In the 1960s the psychiatrist Aaron Beck noticed a pattern in what his depressed patients said. They saw themselves as worthless, they saw the world as unfair or empty, and they saw the future as hopeless. He called these three views the cognitive triad. They didn't seem like symptoms of a low mood alone. They seemed to be part of what kept the mood low.

Beck's idea was that these views could be examined, like any other belief. If you think that you are a failure, you can ask what the evidence is, and what you'd say to a friend. This was the beginning of cognitive therapy, which has since been tested in hundreds of trials. It doesn't work by pushing out negative thoughts. It works by treating them as ideas to check, and not as facts.

# Antidepressants are modest but real
@src Cipriani et al., Lancet, 2018

Do antidepressants work? In 2018 Andrea Cipriani and his colleagues combined the results of more than five hundred trials with over a hundred thousand people. They compared twenty-one drugs with placebo and with each other. Every one of the drugs was more effective than placebo for adults with major depression, though the advantage was modest on average.

The drugs differed a little in how well they worked and in how well people tolerated them. The authors stressed that the best choice depends on the person. Some people respond a lot, and others hardly at all, and an average can hide that. In trials of these drugs, many people also improve on a placebo, which is one reason the measured advantage looks small. The sensible message is that antidepressants help many people, and that treatment is best decided with a doctor.

# The chemical imbalance story
@src Moncrieff et al., Molecular Psychiatry, 2022
@conf mixed

For decades, many people were told that depression is caused by a chemical imbalance, a shortage of serotonin in the brain. In 2022 Joanna Moncrieff and her colleagues reviewed the research on this idea. They concluded that there was no consistent evidence that depression is caused by low serotonin levels or activity.

The review was widely discussed, and critics said that it was too narrow, and that the field has long since moved beyond a simple serotonin story. Other scientists think that serotonin may play a role in some people, or in the way that drugs work. What nobody has shown is that a person's depression comes from a single known shortfall. If you take medicine for depression, this debate is not a reason to stop. Talk to your prescriber before making any change.

# Moving helps mood
@src Singh et al., British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2023

Does exercise help with low mood and anxiety? In 2023 Ben Singh and his colleagues gathered the results of nearly a hundred reviews of studies on physical activity and mental health. They found that physical activity helped to reduce symptoms of depression, anxiety and distress, with a moderate effect on average, across many groups of people.

The effect was seen for walking, jogging, strength training and yoga. Shorter programs, of up to twelve weeks, tended to show larger benefits than longer ones, and higher intensity showed more benefit in some analyses. The researchers noted that the quality of many of the studies was limited. But the result agrees with other work. Exercise is not a replacement for therapy or medicine when you need them. It is a useful addition that costs little and has few downsides.

# Beds are for sleeping
@src Bootzin, 1972

If you often lie awake in bed, your brain may learn to link the bed with being awake. In 1972 Richard Bootzin proposed a treatment based on this idea, called stimulus control. The rules are simple. Go to bed only when you feel sleepy. Use the bed only for sleep, and not for work, scrolling or watching television. If you can't fall asleep after about twenty minutes, get up and do something quiet until you feel sleepy again.

It sounds strange to leave your bed when you want to sleep, but the goal is to break the connection between bed and frustration. Stimulus control is one part of cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, and it has been tested in many trials. It works best when it's kept up for a few weeks. It also asks you to keep a regular time for getting up, even after a poor night.

# The first treatment for insomnia isn't a pill
@src Qaseem et al., Annals of Internal Medicine, 2016

In 2016 the American College of Physicians published guidelines for treating long-term insomnia in adults. After reviewing the evidence, it recommended that all adults with chronic insomnia first try cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, often called CBT-I. Medicines were to be considered only if that didn't work, and after a discussion of benefits and risks.

CBT-I is a short course, usually a few weeks, that combines a regular sleep schedule, stimulus control, limits on time in bed, and a way of dealing with worried thoughts about sleep. Studies find that it often works as well as sleeping pills in the short term and better in the long term, because the benefits last after the treatment stops. It can be done with a therapist, or with a well made program or book.

# ADHD runs in families
@src Faraone & Larsson, Molecular Psychiatry, 2019

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder is sometimes described as the result of poor parenting or too much screen time. The evidence points in a different direction. In 2019 Stephen Faraone and Henrik Larsson reviewed studies of twins and families and concluded that ADHD is highly heritable, with about three quarters of the differences between people explained by genes.

That puts it among the more heritable conditions in psychiatry. It doesn't mean that the environment doesn't matter, or that a gene alone causes it. Many genes, each with a small effect, are involved, along with other influences, such as prematurity and early exposures. The finding matters because it changes how a family and a person can think about it. ADHD is a difference in how the brain manages attention and impulses. It is not a failure of character.

# No link between vaccines and autism
@src Taylor, Swerdfeger & Eslick, Vaccine, 2014

In 1998 a British doctor, Andrew Wakefield, published a paper in The Lancet that suggested a link between the MMR vaccine and autism. It was based on twelve children, and it set off a long scare. The paper was found to have serious problems, including undisclosed conflicts of interest and unethical methods, and The Lancet retracted it in 2010.

Since then the question has been studied many times. In 2014 Luke Taylor and his colleagues combined the results of studies that included more than a million children. They found no relationship between vaccination and autism, and none between the ingredients in vaccines and autism. The scare did real damage, since falling vaccination rates were followed by outbreaks of measles. Autism has strong genetic roots, and its signs often become visible at about the age when children receive certain vaccines, which is why the timing fooled people.

# Talking right after a disaster
@src Rose, Bisson, Churchill & Wessely, Cochrane Review, 2002

After a disaster or an accident, it seems natural to gather the survivors and have them go through what happened in detail, in one session. This was once standard practice, and it was called psychological debriefing. The idea was that talking early would prevent later problems such as PTSD.

In 2002 Suzanna Rose and her colleagues reviewed the trials. They found no evidence that a single debriefing session reduced the risk of PTSD, and in some of the trials, people who took part did worse. This doesn't mean that people shouldn't talk. It means that pressing people to relive an event right away may not help. The guidance now is to offer practical support, to keep people safe, and to be available, and to let each person choose when to talk.

# Perfectionism is rising
@src Curran & Hill, Psychological Bulletin, 2019

In 2019 Thomas Curran and Andrew Hill combined 164 samples of college students in the United States, Canada and Britain. The students had filled out the same questionnaire about perfectionism between 1989 and 2016. Scores went up in a steady line for three kinds of perfectionism, the kind you demand of yourself, the kind you demand of others, and the kind you feel others demand of you.

The largest increase was in socially prescribed perfectionism, the belief that other people expect you to be perfect. The authors linked this to more competitive schools and workplaces, and to social media. Perfectionism isn't just high standards. It is tied to anxiety, depression and burnout. The rise is a reason to think about the standards that you set for yourself, and who you imagine is judging them.

# Are young people more narcissistic?
@src Twenge et al., 2008; Trzesniewski, Donnellan & Robins, 2008
@conf contested

In 2008 Jean Twenge and her colleagues reported that scores on a widely used narcissism questionnaire had risen among American college students since the 1980s. They suggested that a culture of self-esteem and social media was making young people more self-centered. The idea was taken up by the press, and it became a familiar claim about younger generations.

The same year, Kali Trzesniewski, Brent Donnellan and Richard Robins analyzed other, larger sets of data, and they found no increase. Other researchers have since looked at the question with different methods and have found small changes or none. It's a case where experts looked at much the same numbers and drew different conclusions. It's a good reminder to be cautious about stories that every new generation is worse than the one before.

# Don't think of a white bear
@src Wegner et al., JPSP, 1987
@conf mixed

In 1987 Daniel Wegner and his colleagues asked people to say whatever came to mind for five minutes, and told them not to think of a white bear. They rang a bell every time they did. People thought of the bear more than once a minute, on average. Then they were told to think of a white bear if they wanted, and those who had tried to suppress it thought of it more than the people who had been free to think of it all along.

This was called the rebound effect, and it has been used to explain why trying to push away an unwanted thought can make it more frequent. Later studies found that the rebound is not always seen, and that the size of the effect varies. But the idea that suppression can backfire has influenced therapies that teach people to notice thoughts and let them pass, and not fight them.

# Almost everyone has strange thoughts
@src Radomsky et al., 2014

Have you ever had a sudden thought about hurting someone you love, or jumping from a high place, or doing something awful in public? Most people have. In 2014 Adam Radomsky and his colleagues asked hundreds of people in thirteen countries about unwanted intrusive thoughts. Almost everyone, over nine in ten, said that they had them.

For most people, these thoughts pass without any trouble. The difference in people with obsessive compulsive disorder is not that they have the thoughts. It's what they make of them. If you believe that a thought means something about who you are, you may try to control it, and that can make it worse. Knowing that intrusive thoughts are common can take away a good deal of their power.

# A placebo you know is a placebo
@src Kaptchuk et al., PLoS ONE, 2010
@conf mixed

Placebos are thought to work only if people believe they are real medicine. In 2010 Ted Kaptchuk and his colleagues tried something different. They gave eighty people with irritable bowel syndrome either no treatment or pills that were clearly labeled as placebos, made of an inert substance, and told them that placebos can produce changes through mind-body effects.

After three weeks, the people taking the placebo pills were more likely to say that they had adequate relief, 59 percent compared with 35 percent of the others. The study was small and not blinded, so it can't tell us how much of the change was the pills and how much the hope and attention. Later trials of open label placebos have been mixed, with some benefit for pain and fatigue. It's an intriguing sign of how much expectation matters.

# Side effects that come from expecting them
@src Wood et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2020

Some people stop taking statins, which lower cholesterol, because of aches and other symptoms. In 2020 Fiona Wood and her colleagues tested whether the pills themselves were responsible. They recruited sixty people who had stopped taking statins, and had them go through months of a statin, a placebo and no tablet, in random order, without knowing which was which.

The symptoms were just as bad during the months on a placebo as during the months on a statin, and both were worse than the months with no tablet. The effect of expecting side effects is called the nocebo effect. The symptoms were real, but they didn't come from the drug. When the results were explained, about half of the people went back on their statin. Don't stop a medicine on your own, but if side effects trouble you, talk to your doctor about it.

# Seeing stress as helpful
@src Crum, Salovey & Achor, JPSP, 2013
@conf mixed

Stress has a bad reputation, but is it the stress that harms us, or what we believe about it? In 2013 Alia Crum, Peter Salovey and Shawn Achor measured how much people thought that stress is helpful, as against harmful. In studies of employees at a financial firm, those who saw stress as enhancing reported fewer health problems and better work performance, even at high stress.

In a second part of the study, a short video that presented stress as useful changed people's views, and that changed some of their responses. The research is correlational in part, and later studies of mindset interventions have found modest and varied effects. But the idea is a hopeful one. If your pounding heart and tense body are your body preparing for a challenge, you may be able to use that energy, instead of fearing it.

# Asking about suicide
@src Dazzi, Gribble, Wessely & Fear, Psychological Medicine, 2014

Many people are afraid to ask a friend directly whether they are thinking about suicide. They worry that it could put the idea in their head. In 2014 Tarquinio Dazzi and his colleagues reviewed thirteen studies that had looked at this question. None of them found that asking about suicide increased suicidal thoughts, and some suggested that talking about it could bring relief.

Later reviews have reached a similar conclusion, though the studies were mostly about thoughts rather than actions. The practical lesson is that if you are worried about someone, ask them plainly and kindly, listen without judging, and help them connect with support. In the United States, anyone in crisis can call or text 988 at any hour. Asking shows that you care, and it opens a door that may be hard for them to open.

# The coffee that feels like panic
@src American Psychiatric Association, DSM-5, 2013

If you have felt a racing heart, trembling hands and a knot of worry after too much coffee, you were not imagining it. Caffeine is a stimulant. In large amounts, it can cause jitteriness, a fast heartbeat, nervousness and trouble sleeping. These are the same sensations that people feel in anxiety. The standard psychiatric manual lists a caffeine-induced anxiety disorder for severe cases.

People who are prone to anxiety or panic can be especially sensitive. For them the sensation can set off the fear of the fear, which makes it feel like an attack. It is worth checking how much caffeine you take, including tea, energy drinks and pills, when you are feeling anxious. Cutting back gradually, and not all at once, can avoid headaches, and for some people it makes a clear difference.
