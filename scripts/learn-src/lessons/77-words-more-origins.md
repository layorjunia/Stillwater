# Pandemonium
@src Milton, Paradise Lost, 1667; Oxford English Dictionary

The word pandemonium now means wild noise and confusion, but it was invented as the name of a place. In 1667 the English poet John Milton published his epic poem Paradise Lost. In it, the fallen angels build a capital for hell, a great palace, and he called it Pandæmonium. He made the name from two Greek words, pan, meaning all, and daimon, meaning spirit or demon. It was the place of all the demons.

The poem was very popular, and the word took on a life of its own. By the eighteenth century, people were using it for any wild, noisy place, and later for any uproar. Milton invented hundreds of words, and it's one of the most successful. It's a case of a word that began as one man's coinage and became ordinary speech.

# Utopia
@src More, Utopia, 1516

In 1516 the English lawyer and writer Thomas More published a short book about an imaginary island, which he called Utopia. In it, a traveler describes a land where there is no private property, everyone works six hours a day, and religion is tolerated. It's a satire as well as a vision, and More used it to criticize the faults of his own society.

The word is made from two Greek words. It could be ou-topos, meaning no place, or eu-topos, meaning good place. More probably intended the pun. The word is now used for any ideal society, and gave rise to dystopia, an imagined society of misery. More was later beheaded by Henry VIII for refusing to accept the king as head of the Church. He is now regarded as a saint by the Catholic Church.

# Quixotic
@src Cervantes, Don Quixote, 1605 and 1615

Don Quixote is the title character of a novel by the Spanish writer Miguel de Cervantes, published in two parts in 1605 and 1615. He is an elderly gentleman who has read so many tales of chivalry that he loses his mind. He sets out on a horse, called Rocinante, in rusty armor, with a squire, Sancho Panza, to right wrongs and to win glory.

In the most famous scene, he mistakes windmills for giants, and charges at them. From his name, the adjective quixotic means idealistic in a foolish, impractical way. The word has been in English since the early 1700s. The novel is often called the first modern novel. It's also one of the most translated books, and has been in print for over four hundred years.

# Malapropism
@src Sheridan, The Rivals, 1775

In the play The Rivals, written by Richard Brinsley Sheridan in 1775, a character called Mrs. Malaprop tries to sound educated, but keeps using the wrong word. She says that someone is the very pineapple of politeness, when she means pinnacle. She speaks of an allegory on the banks of the Nile, when she means alligator. Her name comes from the French mal à propos, meaning inappropriate.

The word malapropism now means the mistaken use of a word that sounds like another, with a comic effect. It differs from a simple mistake, in that the word is a real one, but not the one meant. Many of Mrs. Malaprop's errors have a kind of logic. Famous people have made them, too, and they can turn up in everyday talk, when we reach for a word that we don't know well.

# Spoonerism
@src Oxford English Dictionary

A spoonerism is a slip in which the sounds of two words are swapped, such as saying a blushing crow, instead of a crushing blow. The word is named after the Reverend William Archibald Spooner, who was warden of New College, Oxford, from 1903 to 1924. He was known for making slips of the tongue of this kind.

Many of the stories told about him, such as that he said you have hissed all my mystery lectures, are probably invented by students. He himself said that he could recall only one, in which he had announced the hymn, Kinquering Congs Their Titles Take. But the name stuck, and the word is in the dictionary from 1900. Such slips happen to all of us, and are studied by linguists because they show how the brain plans speech.

# Hobson's choice
@src Oxford English Dictionary

A Hobson's choice is a choice that is no choice at all, take it or leave it. It's named after Thomas Hobson, who kept a livery stable in Cambridge, England, in the seventeenth century. He rented out horses to students at the university. To keep his best horses from being overworked, so the story goes, he insisted that each customer take the horse that was nearest the door.

A customer could take that horse, or none. Hobson died in 1631, and John Milton, who was a student in Cambridge then, wrote two humorous poems about him. The phrase Hobson's choice is recorded by the 1640s. It's often confused with a dilemma, in which there are two choices that are both bad. In a true Hobson's choice there is only one option, offered as if it were a choice.

# Gerrymander
@src Boston Gazette, 1812

In 1812 the governor of Massachusetts, Elbridge Gerry, signed a bill that redrew the electoral districts of the state. The new boundaries were designed to favor his party, the Democratic-Republicans. One of the districts, north of Boston, had an odd, twisting shape. A newspaper, the Boston Gazette, published a cartoon of the district, with wings and claws added. They said it looked like a salamander.

Someone called it a Gerry-mander, and the name stuck. To gerrymander is to draw the boundaries of an electoral district in order to benefit one party or group. The practice has been used by both parties for more than two hundred years, and with the help of computers today, it can be done very precisely. Courts and reformers continue to debate how to limit it. Gerry himself was not the author of the plan. He is said to have disliked it.

# Sideburns
@src Oxford English Dictionary

The word sideburns is a backward form. It comes from the name of General Ambrose Burnside, a Union general in the American Civil War. He was famous for his whiskers, which grew in front of his ears, and joined a thick mustache, though the chin was clean-shaven. People called them burnsides.

Over time, the two parts of his name were reversed, to side-burns, perhaps because the whiskers grew at the side of the face. The word sideburns has been in use since the 1880s. Burnside's military record was mixed. He led the Union forces in the disastrous Battle of Fredericksburg in December 1862. He later became governor of Rhode Island and a senator. He is remembered for his whiskers more than for his battles.

# Teddy bear
@src Theodore Roosevelt Center; Berryman cartoon, Washington Post, 1902

In November 1902 President Theodore Roosevelt went on a hunting trip in Mississippi. After several days, he had not found a bear. His guides caught a black bear, tied it to a tree, and invited him to shoot it. Roosevelt refused, saying that it would be unsportsmanlike. The incident was reported in the press, and the cartoonist Clifford Berryman drew it in the Washington Post, showing a small, cute bear.

A shop owner in Brooklyn, Morris Michtom, saw the cartoon. He and his wife made a stuffed bear, and put it in the window, with a note saying that it was Teddy's bear, after receiving permission from the president. It was a great success. In Germany, the toy company Steiff was making plush bears at the same time. The name teddy bear has been in use ever since. Roosevelt's nickname, Teddy, which he disliked, became a toy.

# Units named for people
@src Bureau International des Poids et Mesures; Oxford English Dictionary

Many units of measurement are named after scientists. The volt is named for the Italian physicist Alessandro Volta, who invented the battery. The ampere, the unit of current, honors the French physicist André-Marie Ampère. The ohm, the unit of resistance, is named for the German physicist Georg Ohm. Watt, for the Scottish engineer James Watt, is the unit of power, and the joule, for James Joule, a brewer's son in England, is the unit of energy.

The newton, the unit of force, honors Isaac Newton, and the hertz, a unit of frequency, honors Heinrich Hertz, who discovered radio waves. The pascal, for pressure, honors Blaise Pascal, and the tesla, for magnetic fields, honors Nikola Tesla. By convention, the name of the unit is written in lower case, even though it comes from a name, but the symbol is a capital letter. Each is a way of keeping a scientist's name alive.

# Nightmare
@src Oxford English Dictionary

The word nightmare has nothing to do with a horse. The second part, mare, is an old English word for a kind of evil spirit. In Anglo-Saxon folklore, a mare was a female demon that sat on a sleeper's chest, causing a feeling of suffocation and terror. The belief may be linked to the experience of sleep paralysis.

By the Middle Ages, the word nightmare meant the spirit, and then the bad dream itself. The idea of a horse came later, perhaps because of the sound of the word. In German, the word for nightmare is Albtraum, an elf's dream, and in French, cauchemar, which includes the same root. In many cultures, a spirit was blamed for a frightening dream. The word is a fossil of an ancient belief.

# Soldier
@src Oxford English Dictionary

A soldier was originally someone who was paid. The word comes from the Latin solidus, which was a gold coin of the later Roman Empire, introduced by the emperor Constantine in the fourth century. The coin was used to pay troops. The Latin word gave the Old French soudier, a man who fights for pay, and then the English word soldier, in the thirteenth century.

The same coin gave other words. The Italian soldo, the French sou and the Spanish sueldo, meaning salary, come from it. The English word solid, from the same root, means whole, as in a coin of full value. In English, an old abbreviation of pounds, shillings and pence used the letter s for solidus. A soldier was a man who earned his solidi.

# Cappuccino
@src Oxford English Dictionary; Capuchin order records

A cappuccino is named after a religious order. The Capuchin friars, founded in Italy in the sixteenth century, wore a brown habit with a pointed hood. The Italian word for hood is cappuccio. The friars were called Cappuccini, the little hooded ones, and the order's name came into English as Capuchin.

By the 1700s, the color of the coffee, brown with milk, in Vienna and Italy was said to match the color of the friars' robes. A drink was named kapuziner in Vienna. The modern cappuccino, made with espresso and foamed milk, developed after the espresso machine was invented in the early twentieth century. A type of monkey, the capuchin, was also named for the hood-like crown of hair on its head.

# Bikini
@src Oxford English Dictionary; Hawkins, The Bikini, 2021

On July 1, 1946, the United States military exploded an atomic bomb at Bikini Atoll, a ring of coral islands in the Pacific. It was part of a series of tests, and the people who lived there had been moved off. Four days later, on July 5, a French designer, Louis Réard, presented a tiny two-piece swimsuit at a fashion show in Paris.

He called it the bikini, hoping that it would cause an explosive reaction, like the bomb. Models were reluctant to wear it, and he had to hire a nude dancer. It was banned in some countries and on some beaches. It became popular in the 1960s. The name of the atoll in the Marshallese language is thought to mean the place of coconuts. The islanders were never able to return to live there in safety.

# Jeans and denim
@src Oxford English Dictionary

Two clothing words come from French cities. Jeans comes from Gênes, the French name for Genoa, in Italy. In the sixteenth century, sailors from Genoa wore a sturdy cotton or wool-cotton cloth. The cloth was called jean in English. The word for a pair of trousers made of it became jeans.

Denim comes from de Nîmes, meaning from Nîmes, a town in southern France where a tough cloth, serge de Nîmes, was made. In the 1870s Levi Strauss, a merchant in San Francisco, and a tailor, Jacob Davis, patented the use of metal rivets to strengthen the pockets of work trousers. Jeans have since become the world's most popular clothing. Two words that began as place names are on millions of people.

# Tuxedo
@src Oxford English Dictionary; Smithsonian

A tuxedo is a man's formal dinner jacket, usually black. The name comes from a place, Tuxedo Park, a private community for the rich in New York State, about forty miles from New York City. It was founded in 1885 by Pierre Lorillard IV, a tobacco heir. The name of the area itself came from a Native American word, probably from the Lenape language.

The story goes that in 1886, at the park's fall ball, a young man appeared in a short dinner jacket, without tails, which was an English style. Others copied it, and the jacket was named for the place. It became standard formal wear in the United States. In Britain, it's called a dinner jacket. The Lenape name probably means place of the bear, or crooked river, in some accounts.

# Mayday
@src Oxford English Dictionary; Mockford, 1923

Mayday is the international call for help in an emergency, by radio. It was created in 1923 by Frederick Stanley Mockford, a senior radio officer at Croydon Airport near London. He was asked to find a word that would be understood by pilots and ground staff in both English and French. Much of the traffic was between London and Paris.

He proposed mayday, from the French m'aider, an abbreviation of venez m'aider, meaning come and help me. The word is spoken three times in a row, to avoid mistakes. It was adopted by international agreement in 1927. It has nothing to do with the spring festival. The distress signal in Morse code, SOS, is a pattern of three dots, three dashes and three dots, and doesn't stand for any words, though some people say it means save our ship.

# Karaoke and karate
@src Oxford English Dictionary

Both karaoke and karate begin with the Japanese word kara, meaning empty. Karate is made of kara and te, hand, and it means empty hand, a style of fighting that uses no weapons. The name was adopted in Okinawa in the early twentieth century. Before that, the art was known by another word, which meant China hand, because of its origins.

Karaoke is made of kara and oke, a shortening of the Japanese word for orchestra, okesutora. It means empty orchestra, a recording of music without a singer. It is usually said that karaoke was invented in Japan in the early 1970s, by a musician named Daisuke Inoue, who made a machine that played recorded accompaniment for people to sing along. He never patented it. Karaoke has become popular all over the world.

# Tsunami
@src Oxford English Dictionary

The word tsunami is Japanese. It's made up of tsu, meaning harbor, and nami, meaning wave. A harbor wave. Fishermen coming back from the sea would find their harbor destroyed, though they had not noticed any large wave on the open ocean. The reason is that a tsunami, in deep water, is long and low, a swell perhaps a few feet high, but moving as fast as a jet plane. As it reaches shallow water, it slows, and rises.

In English, such waves used to be called tidal waves, though they have nothing to do with the tides. The word tsunami was adopted by scientists, and became widely known after the Indian Ocean tsunami of 2004. It's now the standard word. Japan has the longest written record of these waves, which goes back more than a thousand years.

# Words from the Taíno
@src Oxford English Dictionary; Rouse, The Tainos, 1992

When Christopher Columbus landed in the Caribbean in 1492, the people he first met were the Taíno, who spoke an Arawakan language. It was the first language of the New World that Europeans met, and a number of its words entered Spanish and then English. The list includes hurricane, from huracán, the name of a god of storms. It includes canoe, hammock, barbecue and tobacco.

The word barbecue comes from barbacoa, a wooden frame that was used for cooking, and for sleeping. Potato may come from batata, the Taíno name for the sweet potato. Savannah, maybe, and iguana, are also thought to be Taíno. The Taíno population declined catastrophically within decades of the arrival of the Spaniards, from disease and violence. The words they gave are among the few things of their language that survive.

# Words from the Aztecs
@src Oxford English Dictionary; Karttunen, An Analytical Dictionary of Nahuatl, 1983

Nahuatl was the language of the Aztecs, and it's still spoken by about a million and a half people in Mexico. When the Spanish arrived in the sixteenth century, they took many words from it, and some of them traveled into English. Tomato comes from the Nahuatl tomatl. Chocolate is thought to come from xocolatl, which may mean bitter water, though its origin is debated. Avocado comes from ahuacatl.

The word ahuacatl also meant testicle, probably because of the shape of the fruit, and the pairs that hang together on a tree. The Spanish changed it to aguacate, and the English changed it to avocado, which sounded like the word for lawyer, abogado. Other Nahuatl words are chili, coyote, tamale and guacamole, which is made of the words for avocado and sauce. The foods came to the world with their names.

# Safari
@src Oxford English Dictionary

The word safari is Swahili, and it means a journey. It comes from the Arabic safar, which also means travel. In East Africa, it was used for the long caravans of traders who traveled from the coast to the interior. The word came into English in the late nineteenth century, through accounts of hunting trips by British explorers, such as Richard Burton.

Hunting safaris were popular with wealthy Europeans and Americans, among them Theodore Roosevelt, who went to Africa in 1909, with his son, and shot about five hundred animals. Today, most safaris use cameras instead of guns. Safari has also come to mean any kind of long trip to see wild animals, and in computing, the name of a web browser. A word for a trade journey became a word for tourism.

# Words from Hindi
@src Yule & Burnell, Hobson-Jobson, 1886; Oxford English Dictionary

The British ruled much of India for about two centuries, and as a result, English has borrowed many words from Hindi and other languages of the subcontinent. Shampoo comes from the Hindi chāmpo, from a verb meaning to press or knead, a kind of massage with oils. Bungalow comes from bangla, meaning of Bengal, a type of low house. Pajamas comes from a Persian-Hindi word meaning leg garment.

Jungle comes from jangal, meaning wild or uncultivated land. Loot comes from lūṭ, meaning plunder. Thug is from thag, a swindler, or a member of a gang of robbers. Many other words, such as veranda, cot, dungarees and juggernaut, have also come into the language. A famous dictionary of such words, Hobson-Jobson, published in 1886, is named for an English version of the cry of Muslim mourners, Ya Hasan, Ya Husain.
