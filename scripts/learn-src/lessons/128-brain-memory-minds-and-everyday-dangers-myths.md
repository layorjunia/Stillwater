# The memory that works like a camera does not exist
@series mind
@src Research on eidetic imagery and exceptional memory

Many people say that they have a photographic memory, or wish that they did. The idea is of a mind that can take a snapshot of a page or a scene, and recall it later with such precision that the mental image is like a photograph. No one has ever been shown to have that ability. In careful tests, people who claimed it turned out to have excellent memories that were built by mnemonic techniques, and not by a natural camera in the head.

There is a real phenomenon that is called eidetic memory. A small number of young children can see an image in their mind's eye for a minute or two after the object has been taken away, and can describe details with unusual accuracy. It fades, and it is rare in adults.

There are individuals with exceptional memories, such as the champions of memory contests, and a few with remarkable recall for events of their lives. None has a memory that works like a camera. Memory is a process that rebuilds, and even the best memories contain errors and gaps. The idea of a photographic memory is attractive because it suggests that remembering is a matter of seeing, and learning requires no effort.

# Are you left-brained or right-brained?
@series brain
@src Nielsen, Zielinski, Ferguson, Lainhart & Anderson, PLOS ONE, 2013

A popular idea says that people are either left-brained, which is logical and analytical, or right-brained, which is creative and intuitive, depending on which half of the brain is dominant. It appears in magazines and in many classrooms. The research does not support it.

Some functions are indeed lateralized, meaning that they depend more on one side. Language, for example, is usually dominated by the left hemisphere. The broad claims in popular psychology about the two halves are often inaccurate or exaggerated. In 2013 a team led by Jared Nielsen, at the University of Utah, analyzed brain scans of 1,011 people aged 7 to 29, taken while they were resting. They looked for signs that some people had stronger connections on one side of the whole brain.

They found no evidence of a left-brain or right-brain type. The lateralization of connections was a local property of particular networks, and not a global trait of the person. The two hemispheres work together, and are joined by a thick bundle of fibers. It is a single study, but it agrees with a large body of earlier work. The two sides of the brain are partners.

# The disease that does not split the mind
@series mind
@src Eugen Bleuler, 1908; psychiatric classification

Schizophrenia is often confused with having a split or multiple personality. A film or a headline may say that someone with schizophrenia has two personalities. It is not what the word means. Schizophrenia is a condition marked by continuous or recurring episodes of psychosis, in which a person loses touch with reality.

Its main symptoms include hallucinations, most often hearing voices, delusions, paranoia, and disorganized thinking. Other symptoms are withdrawal from others, a reduced expression of emotion, and a loss of drive. The word was coined by the Swiss psychiatrist Eugen Bleuler, in the early twentieth century, from Greek roots that mean to split and mind. He had in mind a splitting of mental functions, such as thought, feeling and will, which seemed to him to come apart in the illness.

It did not mean a splitting of the personality. A split or multiple personality, in which a person seems to have more than one identity, is a different condition, now called dissociative identity disorder. The confusion matters, because it feeds the false idea that people with schizophrenia are dangerous or unpredictable. Most are not violent, and are more often victims than perpetrators.

# Can a buried memory come back?
@series mind
@conf contested
@src Loftus; research on repressed and recovered memories

The idea of repressed memory is that a person may bury a painful event deep in the mind, forget it for years, and then recall it accurately, perhaps in therapy. It is a popular idea, and it has been used in films and in courtrooms. It is hotly debated among psychologists.

Many researchers say that the evidence for it is weak. People who go through terrible events usually remember them, sometimes all too well. Cases in which an experience is truly forgotten and then recovered in full are rare and hard to verify. In contrast, there is strong evidence that false memories can be created. Experiments by Elizabeth Loftus and others have shown that people can come to remember events that never took place, if they are given suggestions.

This concern became serious in the 1980s and 1990s, when some therapists used techniques that were thought to dig out lost memories, and some patients came to remember abuse that others said did not happen. Families were torn apart, and some accusations led to convictions that were later overturned. Not everyone accepts that repression is a myth, and some clinicians believe in it. The safe lesson is that the vividness of a memory is not a sign that it is true.

# The brain does not finish at twenty-five
@series brain
@src Neuroscience of brain development across the lifespan

One of the most repeated facts about the brain is that it is not fully developed until the age of 25. Sometimes the age given is 21 or 30. It is used to talk about teenagers and decisions. The idea has some truth in it, but it is misleading as stated. The brain does not reach full maturity at any particular age.

The prefrontal cortex, at the front of the brain, does continue to change through the teens and twenties, and its connections become more efficient. But changes in structure, including the growth of the fatty sheath on nerve fibers, called myelin, go on with some consistency across the whole of life, to its end. There is no day when the construction is finished.

Different abilities also peak at different times. Some kinds of quick processing are at their best in the early twenties, and others, such as vocabulary and some forms of judgment, may keep growing into middle age or later. The number 25 comes from studies that stopped at about that age, and not from a finding that things stop there. A brain is a lifelong work in progress, which is a good reason to keep learning.

# Three poisons and one scare
@series sci
@src Toxicology of nicotine, chloroform, tetrahydrozoline and potassium-40

Smokeless tobacco, such as chewing tobacco and snuff, is sometimes thought to be a safe substitute for cigarettes. It is not. It contains nicotine, and is highly addictive. It causes dental disease and cancers of the mouth, esophagus and pancreas, and it is linked to heart disease. It can also harm pregnancies, leading to stillbirth, early birth and low birth weight.

In crime fiction a villain puts a cloth soaked in chloroform over a victim's face, and the victim drops at once. In real life it takes at least five minutes of breathing the vapor from a soaked cloth to render a person unconscious. Most real criminal cases involving chloroform also involve another drug, such as alcohol or diazepam. The idea has been popularized by writers of crime stories.

A prank that spread after the film Wedding Crashers was putting Visine, the eye drops, into someone's drink to cause diarrhea. It does not do that. It is neurotoxic, and swallowing it can cause serious harm. The scare runs the other way. Bananas contain a radioactive form of potassium, potassium-40, but the amount is far too small to harm anyone. No one could eat enough bananas to get radiation poisoning, because the dose does not add up in the body.

# What is in the Bermuda Triangle
@series history
@src Vincent Gaddis, Argosy, 1964; Lawrence Kusche, The Bermuda Triangle Mystery: Solved, 1975

The Bermuda Triangle is an area of the western Atlantic, usually drawn with corners at Miami, Bermuda and Puerto Rico. It is said to be a place where ships and planes vanish in unusual numbers, through some mysterious force. The belief is widespread. The evidence does not support it.

The name was popularized in 1964 by a writer, Vincent Gaddis, in an article for the magazine Argosy. Others wrote books, and the legend grew. In 1975 a librarian and pilot, Lawrence Kusche, went back to the original sources, such as newspaper reports and ship records. He found that many accounts were exaggerated or could not be checked, and that a large share of the incidents had happened well outside the Triangle. Some of the stories left out a storm that was known to have occurred.

The insurers at Lloyd's of London have said that they do not charge higher rates for ships that pass through it, since there is no unusual number of losses. The United States Coast Guard says that in its reviews of losses in the area, it has found nothing that suggests any cause other than physical ones. It is a busy area, with heavy traffic and sudden storms, and accidents are bound to occur. The mystery lies in the storytelling.

# Things that fall from planes, trains and shelves
@series sci
@src Aviation sanitation practice; railroad safety; battery engineering

A piece of ice that falls from a passing airplane is sometimes called blue ice, and it is said that airplanes drop their toilet waste in flight. They do not. The waste of a commercial aircraft is kept in tanks, and emptied by service vehicles on the ground. Blue ice is caused by an accidental leak from the tank. The blue color comes from the disinfectant that is used in aircraft toilets.

It is also said that a penny placed on a railway track can derail a train. It cannot. A bit of debris as small as a brick can be a serious danger, and is a major cause of derailments, but a penny is too small, and is flattened. Even so, it is a dangerous thing to do. The wheels can throw flattened coins and other debris out at high speed, and some people have been hit by trains when trying it.

An old belief is that a car battery will lose its charge if stored on a concrete floor. This is false. A battery stored on concrete does not discharge faster than on any other surface. Most modern batteries are sealed. If the case cracks, or the battery tips over, the acid can corrode a concrete floor, which is a different problem. Many such rules are left over from the time when batteries had cases made of porous materials.

# Two things that movies get wrong about weapons
@series sci
@src Ballistics; mine warfare

In action films, a person hit by a shotgun blast is thrown backward across the room. It does not happen in real life. The momentum of the shot is no greater than the recoil that is felt by the person firing the gun, and that is too little to knock anyone over. If a shot could throw a person backward, then the recoil would knock the shooter back in the same way.

Another scene is of a person who steps on a land mine and hears a click, and must stand still, while a friend works to disarm it, with a pause in the plot. In real life a land mine explodes immediately when stepped on. It does not wait to go off until the foot is lifted. The idea of a mine that is armed on the step and triggered by a release is a creation of fiction, and gives a chance for suspense.

Both are cases in which an effect that would be dull or too final is changed to suit a story. The physics of a shotgun is the physics of a simple collision, and a mine that is stepped on does what it was built to do. The films show a world of drama that follows its own laws.

# More lanes, more traffic
@series think
@src Duranton & Turner, American Economic Review, 2011; Downs, 1962

It seems obvious that if a road is crowded, building more lanes will clear it. Often it does not. Building new roads, or widening existing ones, does not always reduce congestion. Sometimes it can make it worse. The reason is that the number of drivers is not fixed. When a road is improved, more people use it.

Economists Gilles Duranton and Matthew Turner studied the effect in American cities. In a 2011 paper, called The Fundamental Law of Road Congestion, they found that the distance driven on interstate highways rises in proportion to the number of lane kilometers. When more roads are built, people drive more. The added traffic comes from current residents who make more trips, from more commercial traffic, and from people who move in.

They also found little sign that adding lanes to one kind of road drew traffic away from others, or that public transit reduced the distance driven. Their conclusion was that building more roads is unlikely to relieve congestion by itself. The idea of the law goes back to the economist Anthony Downs, who proposed it in 1962. It is a reminder that people respond to changes, and that a fix can create the demand that it is meant to remove.
