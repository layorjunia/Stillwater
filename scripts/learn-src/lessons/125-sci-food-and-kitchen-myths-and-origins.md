# Searing does not seal in the juices
@src Food science; culinary research

Many cooks believe that searing meat in a hot pan seals in its juices. It does not. Searing makes meat lose some moisture. The reason to sear is the brown crust. The browning produces flavor and changes the color and texture, through a set of reactions named for the French chemist Louis-Camille Maillard.

A similar idea is that braising adds moisture to meat. It does not either. Braising, which means cooking meat slowly in a covered pot with some liquid, causes meat to lose moisture too. What makes a braised dish seem moist is that the slow, gentle heat breaks down the tough connective tissue, including collagen, and turns it into gelatin. That lubricates the muscle fibers, and makes them tender.

Both ideas come from the same wish, which is to find a trick that keeps meat moist. In fact the juiciness of a piece of meat depends mostly on how far it is cooked. Meat loses water as it heats, so a steak cooked to a lower temperature is moister than one cooked to a higher temperature. The sear is for taste, and should not be expected to do more.

# Food rules that are more flexible than they sound
@src Food safety guidance; U.S. expiration date regulation; honey and bread storage

The shells of mussels and clams that stay shut after cooking are often thrown away, because of a rule that says they are unsafe. A mussel that does not open can still be fully cooked and safe to eat. It is a rule of thumb that errs on the side of caution, and nothing that cooking has made unsafe.

A famous claim says that Twinkies, the American snack cakes, last for decades. They do not. Their shelf life is around 25 days, and the official figure is 45 days. They usually stay on a store shelf for 7 to 10 days.

In the United States, the dates printed on packaged foods, such as best by and use by, are in most cases a manufacturer's suggestion for best quality, and not a safety deadline. A few states regulate some dates, and federal law requires them for infant formula. Properly stored, packaged foods can be eaten after the date. Crystallized honey is not spoiled either. It is the result of glucose and pollen, and gentle warming melts it. And bread kept in a refrigerator goes stale faster, though the cold does slow mold.

# Three kitchen beliefs that are wrong
@src Capsaicin research; tryptophan content of foods; coffee roasting

It is said that the seeds are the hottest part of a chili pepper. They are not. The seeds contain only a little capsaicin, the compound that causes the burning sensation. The highest concentration is in the placental tissue, the pith or white ribs, to which the seeds are attached. The seeds seem hot because they are in contact with it.

Another belief is that turkey makes you sleepy because it is rich in tryptophan, an amino acid involved in making the sleep-related hormone serotonin and melatonin. Turkey is not especially high in tryptophan, and does not make people drowsier than other foods. The sleepiness after a Thanksgiving or Christmas dinner usually comes from eating a large meal.

A third is that dark roast coffee has more caffeine than light roast. It does not always. When beans are roasted, they swell and become less dense. So if you measure coffee by the scoop, a light roast, which is denser, puts more bean, and so more caffeine, into the scoop. Dark roasts taste stronger because of the roast, which has little to do with the caffeine.

# Four food words that mean something other than you think
@src Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau rules; Japanese cuisine; botany of Pimenta dioica

Bourbon is thought of as a Kentucky whiskey. It does not have to be made there. The legal requirement is only that it be made in the United States, and it is also distilled in states such as New York, California, Wyoming and Washington. Kentucky does make the majority.

Sushi is commonly taken to mean raw fish. It does not. The word refers to the rice, which is prepared with vinegar. Most sushi has some seafood, but fish is not a defining ingredient. A kappamaki roll, with cucumber, has no fish at all.

Allspice is not a mixture of spices. It is a single spice, the dried berry of a Caribbean tree, Pimenta dioica, also called pimento or Jamaican pepper. It got its name because it seems to combine the flavors and aromas of cinnamon, nutmeg, cloves and black pepper.

A well-seasoned cast-iron pan will not be harmed by mild dish soap. The idea that it will may be left over from the days when soap was strong lye. Each of these facts is a small correction of a food belief that people repeat as a rule.

# The syndrome that came from a letter
@series sci
@src Kwok, New England Journal of Medicine, 1968; placebo-controlled MSG trials

Monosodium glutamate, or MSG, is a salt of glutamic acid, an amino acid found in many foods, such as tomatoes and cheese. It was first isolated from seaweed broth by a Japanese chemist, Kikunae Ikeda, in 1908, who named the taste it gave umami. It is used as a flavor enhancer in cooking.

In 1968 a doctor, Robert Ho Man Kwok, wrote a short letter to the New England Journal of Medicine, asking about a numbness and weakness that he felt after eating in Chinese restaurants. The press took it up, and the idea of Chinese restaurant syndrome spread across the United States. MSG was blamed for headaches and other discomfort.

But when scientists tested it in placebo-controlled trials, where people did not know whether they had MSG or a dummy, they did not find that it caused the symptoms. Some people report that they are sensitive to it, but this has not been shown in trials. The fear seems to have been fed by a prejudice that associated Chinese food with something suspicious. MSG is also present in many Western foods, with no such reputation.

# Four food origin stories that are not true
@src Macaroni Journal; histories of French cuisine; medieval spice trade records

Steak tartare, a dish of raw minced beef, is said to have been invented by Mongol warriors, who tenderized raw horse meat by riding on it under their saddles. There is no evidence. It is more likely named after the sauce tartare, which was served with steaks in a French dish in the early twentieth century.

Marco Polo is said to have brought pasta from China to Italy. He did not. The story began as promotional material in a newsletter, the Macaroni Journal, published by an association of American pasta makers.

Another belief is that in the Middle Ages, spices were used to hide the taste of rotten meat. Spices were costly luxuries. The people who could afford them could afford good meat, and no document of the time calls for using spices to cover up spoiled food.

Lastly, it is said that Catherine de' Medici, when she came from Italy to marry the future king of France, brought cooks who laid the foundations of French haute cuisine. This is a legend, and the sources do not support it. In each case a neat story took the place of a slower and more complex history.

# Four inventors who did not invent it
@src Histories of whipped cream, champagne, potato chips and Caesar salad

Whipped cream, called crème Chantilly, was said to have been invented in 1661 by the steward François Vatel, and named for the Château de Chantilly, where it was served. Similar recipes are recorded a century earlier in Italy and France, so he did not invent it.

The monk Dom Pérignon is often said to have invented champagne. He did not. Wine begins to bubble naturally after it is pressed, as it ferments in the bottle. At the time, bubbles were considered a flaw, and Pérignon worked, without success, to remove them.

Potato chips are said to have been created by a chef who was annoyed by a customer's complaint that his French fries were too thick and not salty enough. Recipes for potato chips were published as early as 1817. The story was made popular by a 1973 advertising campaign for a paper company.

Caesar salad was not invented by Julius Caesar. It was made by Caesar Cardini, an Italian-American restaurateur, in Tijuana, Mexico, in 1924. Having a famous person in the name gives a dish a good story, and the real stories are often just as good.

# Foods with the wrong homeland
@series history
@src Patents of John Harvey Kellogg, 1895; histories of fortune cookies, Oreos and Mongolian beef

George Washington Carver did not invent peanut butter. The first patent related to it was filed by John Harvey Kellogg in 1895, and the Incas used a paste made of peanuts centuries earlier. Carver, a scientist at the Tuskegee Institute, did compile hundreds of uses for peanuts, and also for pecans and sweet potatoes. An opinion piece by William F. Buckley Jr. may be the origin of the mistake.

Fortune cookies are not an authentic part of Chinese cooking. They originated in Japan and were introduced to the United States by Japanese immigrants. In China they are thought of as American, and are rare.

Hydrox is not a copy of the Oreo. It was invented in 1908, four years before the Oreo, and was at first more popular. The name, which some thought sounded like a laundry soap, is said to have hurt it.

Mongolian beef and Mongolian barbecue are Chinese dishes with no link to Mongolian food. They were thought up in Taiwan in the 1950s. They were to be named for Beijing, the home town of their creator, but that was avoided because of its link with communism. Mongolia was chosen because it sounded exotic.

# The banana candy and the colonial lobster
@series history
@src Isoamyl acetate chemistry; histories of lobster and tomatoes in New England

Banana-flavored candy does not taste like a real banana. A well-known explanation is that it copies the Gros Michel banana, the variety that was the main kind sold in America until a disease wiped it out and the Cavendish took over. It is not the reason. Every kind of banana gets its flavor from a mix of many compounds, but the candy flavor is a single one, isoamyl acetate. That compound is found in bananas and in many other fruits and fermented drinks. It is richer in the Gros Michel, but it was chosen for candy because it is easy to make.

A history story says that in colonial New England, laws or contracts barred prisoners and servants from being fed lobster more than twice a week. There were never any. Lobster was cheap and common, and it was food for the poor, but no such rules existed.

Another is that people in the early nineteenth century thought that tomatoes were poisonous, until a man ate one in public to show that they were safe. The belief was not widespread, and the story of the public tasting dates from the twentieth century. Tales about old foods are usually told with more confidence than the evidence allows.
