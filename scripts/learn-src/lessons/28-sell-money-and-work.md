# The brand in the brain
@src McClure et al., Neuron, 2004

In 2004 Samuel McClure and his colleagues ran a version of the Pepsi Challenge inside a brain scanner. When people sipped Coke and Pepsi without knowing which was which, their preferences split about evenly. Then they were told what they were drinking, and the result changed. When a cup was labeled Coke, about three quarters of them said they preferred it, though the contents were the same.

The scans showed more than a change in what people said. Knowing the brand changed activity in the hippocampus and the prefrontal cortex, areas tied to memory and to using emotion to shape a choice. The label seemed to change the experience, and not just the report of it. Oddly, a Pepsi label didn't have the same pull. A brand is a bundle of memories and feelings that you taste along with the product.

# The music that sold the wine
@src North, Hargreaves & McKendrick, Nature, 1997

In 1997 Adrian North and his colleagues ran a neat experiment in the wine aisle of a supermarket in England. On some days, they played French accordion music, and on others, German beer-hall music. French and German wines of similar price and style stood side by side. When French music played, shoppers bought mostly French wine. When German music played, German wine outsold French.

Afterward, when shoppers were asked whether the music had influenced them, most said no. It's a striking demonstration of how background cues steer us without our awareness. The music didn't make anyone like wine more. It brought one country to mind, and that tilted the choice. It's worth asking what you're hearing, and what it's making you think of, when you decide to buy.

# Why we love what we built
@src Norton, Mochon & Ariely, Journal of Consumer Psychology, 2012

In 2012 Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon and Dan Ariely asked people to assemble IKEA boxes, to fold origami, and to build Lego sets. Afterward they asked how much the people would pay for what they had made. The builders valued their own plain, amateur work about as highly as they valued the same thing made by an expert. People who hadn't built it valued the amateur version much less.

The effect depended on finishing. When people built something and failed, or when their creations were taken apart in front of them, the extra value disappeared. The researchers called it the IKEA effect. It helps to explain why meal kits and do-it-yourself products can be popular, and why you may overvalue your own work. When a project is yours, you aren't the best judge of its worth.

# Little touches, bigger tips
@src Rind & Bordia, 1996; Rind & Strohmetz, 1999

Restaurant tipping has been studied for years, and the results make a good lesson in persuasion. In 1996 Bruce Rind and Prashant Bordia had servers draw a smiley face on the back of some checks. The female server's tips went up with the smiley face. The male server's tips went down. The same gesture could land very differently depending on who made it.

In 1999 Rind and David Strohmetz found that when a server wrote a note on the check about an upcoming dinner special, the tips were higher than on the checks with no note. Other researchers found gains from a server giving her name, crouching down to the table, and touching a diner's shoulder lightly. What these share is a sense of personal attention. The customer feels looked after, and gives a little more.

# A number from your wallet
@src Ariely, Loewenstein & Prelec, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2003
@conf mixed

In 2003 Dan Ariely, George Loewenstein and Drazen Prelec asked MBA students to write the last two digits of their Social Security number, and then to bid on things like wine, chocolate and computer equipment. Students whose numbers were high bid far more than the others. Those in the top fifth bid, on average, more than twice as much as those in the bottom fifth.

The irrelevant number had worked as an anchor, shaping what people were willing to pay though they knew it was arbitrary. This became one of the best known demonstrations of anchoring. Replications of this exact design have been mixed, and some found weaker effects. But anchoring itself has held up well in many other studies. The first number you see, even a random one, tends to color the numbers that follow it.

# The interview that adds noise
@src Dana, Dawes & Peterson, Judgment and Decision Making, 2013

Most of us trust our own judgment in an interview. In 2013 Jason Dana, Robyn Dawes and Nathanial Peterson tested this. Students were asked to predict other students' grades for the coming semester. They had background information, such as prior grades, and for one of the students they also held a short interview. In one version, the person being interviewed was answering at random, and the interviewer didn't know it.

The interviewers felt just as sure of their impressions after random answers as after real ones. Worse, the interviews made the predictions less accurate than the grades alone. The authors concluded that a conversation gives us a coherent story that feels convincing, even when it is noise. Their advice was to avoid unstructured interviews when screening people, or at least to keep their weight small.

# The résumé with the other name
@src Bertrand & Mullainathan, American Economic Review, 2004

In 2004 Marianne Bertrand and Sendhil Mullainathan sent nearly 5,000 fictitious résumés in response to job ads in Boston and Chicago. The résumés were similar in quality, but some had names more common among white Americans, such as Emily and Greg, and others had names more common among Black Americans, such as Lakisha and Jamal.

The résumés with white-sounding names got about 50 percent more callbacks. A better résumé helped the white-named applicants a great deal, but made much less difference for the others. The study was widely discussed, and similar tests in other places and with other groups have found related results. It shows that discrimination can operate even at the first step of hiring, and that it can do so without anyone saying a word.

# Blind auditions
@src Goldin & Rouse, American Economic Review, 2000
@conf mixed

In the 1970s and 80s, major American orchestras began to hold auditions behind a screen, so that the judges couldn't see the musician. In 2000 the economists Claudia Goldin and Cecilia Rouse studied records of orchestra auditions and concluded that the screen helped women advance, and that it helped to explain the rise in the number of women in orchestras.

Later researchers have questioned how strong the evidence is, pointing to the small numbers and the way the data was analyzed, and the true effect may have been smaller. But the idea has spread well beyond music. Taking away information that shouldn't matter, such as a name or a photo, lets the decision rest on what does. It's a sensible practice whatever the size of the original effect.

# When big rewards backfire
@src Ariely, Gneezy, Loewenstein & Mazar, Review of Economic Studies, 2009
@conf mixed

Do bigger rewards make people try harder? In 2009 Dan Ariely and his colleagues tested this with games that needed attention, memory or creativity. They ran them in the United States and in rural India, where the largest reward was many times a typical day's earnings. In general, very large rewards led to worse performance than moderate ones, though there were exceptions.

The authors suggested that when the stakes are very high, the pressure gets in the way of tasks that need thinking. For simple tasks that depend only on effort, bigger rewards do help. The experiments were small, and the result has been debated. But it fits with other research on choking under pressure. A bonus may help when the work is mostly effort, and it can backfire when the work is skilled or creative.

# Asking is not negotiating
@src Small, Gelfand, Babcock & Gettman, JPSP, 2007
@conf mixed

Research by Linda Babcock and others has found that women are less likely than men to start a negotiation over pay, which can add up over a career. In 2007 Deborah Small, Michele Gelfand, Babcock and Hilary Gettman tested whether the words used to describe the opportunity make a difference. The same situation was described to some people as a chance to negotiate and to others as a chance to ask.

When it was framed as negotiating, men were more likely than women to start. When it was framed as asking, the gap disappeared. The authors suggested that negotiation sounds like a contest, which clashes with norms of politeness for people with less power, while asking doesn't. The study can't settle the whole question of pay gaps, but it shows how much a single word can change who speaks up.

# Giving when the overhead is covered
@src Gneezy, Keenan & Gneezy, Science, 2014

Many donors say they don't want their money going to administrative costs. In 2014 Uri Gneezy, Elizabeth Keenan and Ayelet Gneezy tested how much that matters. In a lab study, people were less likely to give to a charity as its overhead went up. But when they were told that someone else had already covered the overhead, many gave even when overhead was high.

They then ran a field experiment with an education foundation. An appeal that said every dollar would go to the cause, because the costs were already paid, brought a much higher rate of giving than the standard appeal. Donors, it seems, don't mind that charities have costs. They mind the thought of their own money paying for them. A charity can use this by finding a few donors to cover overhead.

# Matching gifts
@src Karlan & List, American Economic Review, 2007

In 2007 Dean Karlan and John List mailed solicitations to more than 50,000 past donors of a nonprofit, and varied the offer. Some letters made a plain request. Others said that a generous donor would match the gift, one for one, two for one or three for one. A match offer raised both the money collected per letter and the share of people who gave.

But the size of the match made no difference. A three-for-one match did no better than one-for-one. The idea of a match mattered more than its real value. People like to feel that their gift is being multiplied, but the multiple isn't what moves them. It's useful for fundraisers, and for any of us who has wondered whether a bigger promise would have worked better.

# Structured interviews win
@src Schmidt & Hunter, Psychological Bulletin, 1998

In 1998 Frank Schmidt and John Hunter reviewed 85 years of research on how well different hiring methods predict job performance. Among the best predictors were work samples, which test people on the real tasks, tests of general mental ability, and structured interviews, in which each candidate gets the same questions and is scored against a set standard. Unstructured interviews were less useful, and years of experience did poorly.

Combining methods did better still. A test of mental ability together with a structured interview predicted performance better than either alone. The review has been updated since, and some of its estimates have been revised, but the broad lesson has held up. If you're hiring, ask everyone the same questions, score them the same way, and have them do a piece of real work.
