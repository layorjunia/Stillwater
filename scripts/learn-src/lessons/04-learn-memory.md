# The method of loci
@src Dresler et al., Neuron, 2017

Greek and Roman speakers who had to deliver long speeches with no notes used a trick that memory champions still rely on today. You pick a place you know well, like your own house, and in your mind you set each thing you want to remember at a spot along a walk through it. The stranger and more vivid the image, the better it holds. A loaf of bread on the doormat, a giant telephone in the hallway, a fish swimming in the sink.

To recall the list, you simply walk the route again and look at each spot. In a 2017 study, people who trained with the method for six weeks went from remembering about 26 of 72 words to about 62. Nothing about their brains had changed. They had learned to hang new information on a place they already knew by heart, and that is something almost anyone can do.

# Chunking
@src Miller, 1956; Chase & Simon, 1973

Working memory can only hold a few items at once, so the trick is to make each item bigger. A phone number grouped as 555-0142 is easier to hold than seven loose digits, because the grouping turns many pieces into a few.

A classic study with chess players shows how far this can go. Chess masters could recall the positions of pieces on a board from a real game far better than beginners could, but they were no better at all with pieces placed at random. They weren't remembering more pieces. They were seeing familiar patterns, and each pattern counted as a single item. That's what expertise often is, a large stock of chunks. When you're learning something new, look for the groupings, because they are what let the details fit in your head.

# Dual coding
@src Paivio, 1971

In 1971 the psychologist Allan Paivio proposed that we store words and pictures in two linked systems in the mind. If you learn something both ways, you end up with two routes back to it, and when one fails, the other may still work.

That's why it helps to sketch a diagram of what you've read, or to picture an idea, instead of only rereading the words. The picture doesn't need to be good, and it doesn't need to be neat. Making it forces you to decide what the thing actually means, how the parts connect and what goes where, and that thinking is half the benefit. A shaky drawing of how a heart pumps blood will often stay with you longer than a clean paragraph about it.

# Levels of processing
@src Craik & Tulving, 1975

In 1975 Fergus Craik and Endel Tulving showed people a series of words and asked about each one of three kinds of question. Is it written in capital letters? Does it rhyme with something? Does it fit in this sentence? Afterward, the people who had thought about meaning remembered far more of the words than those who had only looked at the letters or the sound.

The lesson is that how deeply you think about something matters more than how long you look at it. Rereading is shallow, because your eyes move over words you already recognize. Explaining is deep, because you have to understand a thing to put it in your own words. If you want a fact to stick, try asking what it means, why it's true or how it connects to something else you know, and let the answer carry the memory.

# The self-reference effect
@src Rogers, Kuiper & Kirker, 1977

A 1977 study asked people to judge words in different ways. For some words, the question was "Does this word describe you?" For others, it was about the word's meaning, or about whether it described someone else. Later, the people who had asked themselves the question about their own lives remembered more of the words than any other group.

Linking new information to yourself turns out to be a strong aid to memory, perhaps because you already have so much stored about yourself that there is plenty to attach to. When you study, ask yourself how an idea connects to something you've lived or done. If you're learning about habits, think of one of your own. If it's about the history of a city you've visited, picture the street. The more personal the hook, the harder it is to lose.

# Encoding specificity
@src Tulving & Thomson, 1973

Endel Tulving and Donald Thomson found in 1973 that memory works best when the situation at recall resembles the situation at learning. In their experiments, a cue word that was present when someone learned a word helped them recall it better than a strong cue they hadn't seen before. A cue that was less obviously linked in the abstract could beat a cue that seemed better, as long as it had been there at the time.

It suggests that you should practice under conditions that look like the test. If the exam is in a quiet room, studying at a desk in silence may serve you better than studying lying on a couch with music playing. It also explains why something you can't recall can pop back when you return to the place where you learned it. The memory was stored along with its surroundings.

# Context-dependent memory
@src Godden & Baddeley, 1975; Murre, 2021
@conf mixed

In 1975 Duncan Godden and Alan Baddeley had scuba divers learn lists of words either on a beach or underwater. Then they asked the divers to recall them either in the same place or in the other one. The divers did better when they were in the same setting where they had learned.

It sounds appealing, and it fits with what many of us feel when we walk into an old school and remember things. But later attempts to repeat the experiment have found that the effect is small and doesn't always show up. In 2021 one team reported that it didn't replicate at all in their hands. The effect seems more reliable when the place actually shapes what you were thinking about at the time. So don't count on a particular room to help you, but don't be surprised if it sometimes does.

# Retrieval-induced forgetting
@src Anderson, Bjork & Bjork, 1994

Practicing some of what you know can make you worse at related things you didn't practice. In a 1994 experiment, Michael Anderson and his colleagues had people practice recalling some of the items in a category, like fruits. Later, they were worse at remembering the other fruits than they were at items from categories they hadn't practiced at all.

The idea is that when your mind picks one memory out of a group, it holds back the competitors, so that they don't intrude. That's helpful while you are searching, and it has a side effect afterward. The competitors become a bit harder to reach. It's a reason to review all of the material when you study, not just the parts you find easy or enjoyable, and to be a little wary of going over the same favorite examples again and again.

# The misinformation effect
@src Loftus & Palmer, 1974

In 1974 Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed people films of car accidents. Afterward they asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed into" each other, or when they "hit" each other. The people who heard "smashed" gave higher speeds, an average of 40.8 miles per hour against 34.0 for those who heard "hit."

A week later, the "smashed" group was also more likely to say they had seen broken glass, though there hadn't been any. A single word in a question had changed what they remembered. It shows that memory isn't a recording that we play back. It's more like a reconstruction, and it can take in suggestions from the way we're asked. It's a good reason to be careful with leading questions, especially when someone is trying to remember something important.

# Implanted false memories
@src Loftus & Pickrell, 1995

In 1995 Elizabeth Loftus and Jacqueline Pickrell asked adults to read four short stories about their own childhoods, written with the help of their relatives. Three of the stories were true, and one was invented: that they had been lost in a shopping mall as a child and rescued by an older woman.

About a quarter of the participants came to remember the made-up event, and some added details of their own. Vivid, detailed memories feel reliable, and we trust them. But a detailed memory can still be wrong, and a confident memory isn't always a true one. This doesn't mean that memories can't be trusted at all. Most of our ordinary memories are accurate enough. It means that how sure and how vivid a memory feels is not a good test of whether it happened.

# Flashbulb memories
@src Neisser & Harsch, 1992

When something shocking happens, we feel as though we'll never forget exactly where we were. In 1986, the day after the Challenger space shuttle exploded, Ulric Neisser and Nicole Harsch asked students to write down how they had heard the news. Two and a half years later, they asked them again.

About a quarter of the later accounts were wrong in nearly every detail, and the students were still very confident in them. When shown their own original notes, some of them insisted that the new version was correct. The memory felt vivid, but the details had been rebuilt over time. Strong emotion seems to make us more confident in a memory, but it doesn't make the memory more accurate.

# Source amnesia
@src Schacter, Harbluk & McLachlan, 1984

You can remember a fact and forget where you learned it. Daniel Schacter and his colleagues documented this in the 1980s, in patients with memory problems and also in healthy people. The fact survives, but the record of where it came from fades faster.

That's why a rumor you heard once, from someone you wouldn't have trusted, can seem like common knowledge months later. The content survives, but the tag that says "unreliable source" doesn't. If something seems like it's just something "everyone knows," it's worth asking where you first heard it. Sometimes you'll find that you can't say, and that's a good sign that you should check before repeating it.

# Cryptomnesia
@src Bright Tunes Music v. Harrisongs Music, 1976

Sometimes you can come up with an idea or a tune that you think is new, and it's something you heard before. In 1976 a US court decided that George Harrison had "subconsciously" copied the melody of the Chiffons' song "He's So Fine" when he wrote "My Sweet Lord."

The judge didn't believe that he had done it on purpose. A stored tune had simply come back to him without its label, and he took it for his own creation. Writers, musicians and scientists sometimes worry about it, and with good reason. It's a reason to keep track of where your ideas come from, to keep notes as you read, and to be a little modest about an idea that arrives suspiciously fully formed.

# The tip of the tongue
@src Brown & McNeill, 1966

You know the word, you can almost taste it, and it won't come. In 1966 Roger Brown and David McNeill studied this state. People in it can often tell you the first letter and how many syllables the word has, which shows that memory stores words in pieces and not as a single lump.

Pushing harder often makes it worse, because you keep rehearsing the wrong candidates. The usual advice is to move on and let it come back by itself, which it often does a minute later while you're in the shower or washing dishes. It's a small reminder that remembering isn't always something we can order up. Sometimes the search keeps working in the background after we've stopped paying attention to it.

# Prospective memory
@src Einstein & McDaniel, 1990

Remembering to do something in the future, like taking medicine or sending a message, is a different task from remembering the past. It usually fails because there was no cue at the moment you needed it. You meant to do it, you simply didn't think of it at the right time.

The solution is to attach the intention to something you'll definitely encounter. For instance, "when I pour my morning coffee, I'll take the pill." Psychologists call these implementation intentions, and they work much better than just resolving to remember. The idea is to decide in advance exactly when and where you'll act, so that the situation itself reminds you. A note on the door, an alarm, or a pill box on the kettle all do the same job.

# Memory consolidation
@src Müller & Pilzecker, 1900

A new memory is fragile at first and takes hours and days to settle. In 1900 Georg Müller and Alfons Pilzecker showed this with a simple experiment. People who learned a list of nonsense syllables and then immediately learned a second list remembered the first one worse than people who got a rest in between.

The first memory needed some quiet time to firm up, and the second list got in the way. Sleep is one of the periods when memories seem to firm up, which is a good reason not to cram all night. It's also a reason to leave small gaps in a study session, and to avoid starting something new and demanding right after you've learned something you care about. Your brain is still working on what you just gave it.

# Reconsolidation
@src Nader, Schafe & LeDoux, 2000
@conf mixed

The old picture was that once a memory is stored, it stays fixed. In 2000 Karim Nader, Glenn Schafe and Joseph LeDoux gave rats a fear memory, then reminded them of it and blocked protein production in the brain. The memory was weakened, as if it had to be rebuilt each time it was recalled.

It suggested that recalling a memory opens a window during which it can be changed. That raised the hope that painful memories could one day be softened by treatment. Scientists are still working out how far this applies to people, and the results so far have been mixed. For now it's a promising idea and not a proven therapy. It does fit with the everyday observation that each time you remember something, you may be remembering your last remembering, and not the event itself.

# Patient H.M.
@src Scoville & Milner, 1957

In 1953 a surgeon removed parts of Henry Molaison's brain, including most of the hippocampus on both sides, to treat his severe epilepsy. The seizures eased, but he could no longer form new memories of facts or events. He met his doctors again and again as if for the first time.

Yet he could learn a new skill, like tracing a star while looking at it in a mirror, and he improved day by day without remembering that he had ever practiced. His case showed that memory isn't a single thing, and that the system for facts and events is separate from the one for skills. For decades he was one of the most studied people in science, and the knowledge gained from him shaped how we understand the brain.

# Declarative and procedural memory
@src Squire, 1987

You can know that something is the case, like the capital of France, and you can know how to do something, like ride a bicycle. Psychologists call the first declarative memory and the second procedural, and they seem to depend on different brain systems.

You might be able to ride perfectly well without being able to explain how you balance. Or you might know the steps of a skill and be unable to do it. People with amnesia often keep their skills while losing their memory of learning them. It's also why you can't learn to ride a bike by reading about it. The knowing-how part has to be built through practice, and reading only gives you the knowing-that.

# Episodic and semantic memory
@src Tulving, 1972

In 1972 Endel Tulving suggested that we have two kinds of long-term memory for information. Episodic memory holds events from your own life, such as your last birthday party, and it comes with the feeling of being there. You can mentally travel back to it.

Semantic memory holds facts about the world, such as that Paris is the capital of France, without remembering when or where you learned them. The two usually work together. A memory of a trip draws on both what happened and what you know about the place. In some forms of amnesia one is damaged more than the other, and a person can know facts about their life while being unable to relive it. The distinction helps explain why a fact can feel like part of you, or not.

# The working memory model
@src Baddeley & Hitch, 1974

In 1974 Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch proposed that thinking in the moment uses several parts. There's a phonological loop for sounds and words, a visuospatial sketchpad for images and locations, and a central executive that directs attention among them.

The model explains why you can listen to music while reading a map, but find it hard to read an email while someone is talking to you. Tasks that use the same part compete, and tasks that use different parts mostly don't. It also suggests a practical rule. When you have a lot to hold in mind, don't pile more words on top of words. Draw a picture, make a list or use another channel to give your overworked loop a rest.

# Cognitive load theory
@src Sweller, 1988

John Sweller suggested in the 1980s that, because working memory is so small, teaching should avoid wasting it. Making someone hold too much at once leads to confusion and poor learning, however good the material is.

One common problem is split attention. A diagram appears on one page and its explanation three pages later, so the learner has to keep flipping back and holding pieces in mind. Putting the explanation right beside the picture lets the learner spend their limited memory on understanding. It's a reminder that making something harder to read isn't the same as making it deeper. Clear presentation leaves more room for the thinking that actually builds knowledge.

# The worked-example effect
@src Sweller & Cooper, 1985

If you're new to a subject, studying solved problems can teach you more than trying to solve them yourself. John Sweller and Graham Cooper showed this with algebra in 1985. Students who studied worked examples learned faster than those who spent the same time on practice problems.

Seeing every step frees your mind from searching for a method, and lets you notice the pattern in how the answer was built. As you get better, you should switch to solving problems on your own. The best sequence usually starts with an example and moves gradually toward independence, with the steps filled in less and less. It may feel like cheating to read the answer first, but for a beginner it's often the fastest way to understand.

# The expertise reversal effect
@src Kalyuga et al., 2003

Something that helps a beginner can get in the way of an expert. Step-by-step guidance with detailed explanations supports a novice, but for someone who already knows the material, it's redundant and can slow them down. They have to process the explanation and also check it against what they know.

Slava Kalyuga and his colleagues described the effect in 2003. It's a reminder that good teaching adapts to the learner. The same worksheet that rescues one student may bore another, and the same advice can help a beginner and annoy an expert. So when you're learning, it's worth checking where you stand. If the material feels like it's spelling out things you already know, try a harder version of it.

# The zone of proximal development
@src Vygotsky, 1930s

The Russian psychologist Lev Vygotsky, writing in the 1930s, described the distance between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with help. He thought the best teaching works in that gap.

If a task is too easy, nothing is learned. If it's too hard, the learner gives up. In between, with a little guidance that is gradually withdrawn, is where growth happens. It's a helpful way to think about your own learning, as well as about teaching someone else. If you're bored, move up a step. If you're lost, look for a small piece of help, like an example or a hint, and then try again by yourself.

# Scaffolding
@src Wood, Bruner & Ross, 1976

A scaffold on a building holds things up until the structure can stand without it. In 1976 David Wood, Jerome Bruner and Gail Ross borrowed the word for the support a tutor gives a child. The tutor does the parts the child can't yet manage, such as holding the pieces in place, and hands over more and more as the child improves.

Good scaffolding is meant to be temporary. If it never comes down, the learner never learns to stand. This is useful to remember when helping someone with homework or a new job. Giving the answer teaches little, and walking away teaches little too. What works is to do just enough, then do a little less each time, until the person doesn't need you.

# Mastery learning
@src Bloom, 1968, 1984
@conf mixed

Benjamin Bloom suggested in 1968 that students should master each step before moving to the next, with extra help and time if they need it, instead of moving on with gaps. In a regular class, the lesson moves on whether or not everyone has understood, and the gaps pile up.

In 1984 Bloom reported a "two sigma" finding: students who were tutored one-on-one with mastery methods did about two standard deviations better than those in a regular class. That's a very large difference. Later studies have found smaller gains, so it's probably not as dramatic as it first appeared. But the effect is real, and it's why practicing the basics until they're solid pays off. A shaky foundation makes every later step harder than it needs to be.

# The Leitner box
@src Leitner, 1972

In 1972 the German science writer Sebastian Leitner described a simple way to schedule flashcards using a few boxes. All new cards start in box one. When you get one right, it moves up to the next box, which you review less often. When you get one wrong, it goes back to box one.

The result is that the cards you know well get less of your time, and the ones you struggle with come back more often. It's a way of spending your effort where it's needed. You can do it with shoeboxes and index cards, and many flashcard apps use the same idea in software. The principle is the same as spaced repetition. Each success earns a longer wait, and each failure earns a short one.

# Writing by hand or typing?
@src Mueller & Oppenheimer, 2014; Morehead et al., 2019
@conf mixed

In 2014 Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer found that students who took notes by hand did better on conceptual questions than those who typed. Their explanation was that typists copy more word for word, while writers, who can't keep up, have to summarize in their own words, and summarizing is a form of thinking.

Later attempts to repeat the study found smaller differences, or none at all, so the result is less firm than it was once reported. It may depend on how you take notes. Typing and thinking about the meaning probably beats handwriting and copying. The useful rule is not about the tool. It's that notes made by putting an idea in your own words will serve you better than a transcript.

# Speed reading
@src Rayner et al., 2016

Courses promise to teach you to read at 1,000 words a minute or more with full understanding. In 2016 a team of reading researchers led by Keith Rayner reviewed the evidence and concluded that it doesn't hold up. The claims fall apart when you test what readers actually understood.

There's a trade-off between speed and comprehension. You can skim, and skimming is useful for finding the main points or deciding what's worth reading. But it's a different task from reading, and you miss details. A normal speed for adults is about 200 to 300 words a minute. If you need to read faster, the better ways are to read more, build your vocabulary, and decide what you don't need to read at all.

# The production effect
@src MacLeod et al., 2010

In 2010 Colin MacLeod and his colleagues found that people remembered words better when they read them aloud than when they read them silently. The words they had spoken were more likely to be recalled later.

Hearing your own voice adds another feature to the memory, something distinctive about that item that sets it apart from the others. It works best for the things you most want to remember, because if you read everything aloud, nothing stands out. So reading key points aloud, even quietly to yourself, can help. It's an easy trick to try when you're studying alone, and it asks nothing of you but a little courage to talk to the room.

# The Pomodoro technique
@src Cirillo, late 1980s
@conf mixed

In the late 1980s Francesco Cirillo, then a university student, used a tomato-shaped kitchen timer to break his work into 25-minute blocks with short breaks between them. The technique is named for the Italian word for tomato, pomodoro.

It gives a clear start and a clear end to each session, and that can make it easier to begin, since twenty-five minutes feels doable. It may help some people focus. But there's little controlled research to show that twenty-five minutes is the right length, so it's best seen as a handy habit and not a scientific rule. Adjust it to what works for you, and if you're in a good stretch of work, there's no law that says you have to stop.

# Transfer of learning
@src Thorndike & Woodworth, 1901

Does learning one skill help with another? In 1901 Edward Thorndike and Robert Woodworth tested it and found that practicing one kind of judgment, such as estimating the area of rectangles, did little to improve performance on others. The training stayed attached to the task it was trained on.

They suggested that transfer happens mostly when two tasks share the same elements. It's a sober point for anyone hoping that a hobby, such as chess or Latin, will make them smarter in general. Skills usually carry over only to similar tasks. That doesn't make such hobbies a waste, since they're worth doing for their own sake. It just means that if you want to get better at something, the best practice is the thing itself.

# Overlearning
@src Rohrer et al., 2005

It seems sensible to keep practicing after you've got something right, to be sure. In a 2005 study by Doug Rohrer and his colleagues, students learned geography facts and then either stopped or kept drilling the same facts in the same session.

A week later, the extra drilling made no difference to how much they remembered. The time wasn't harmful, but it wasn't useful either. The better use of time is to come back to the material later, after some forgetting has set in, and practice it again. That's the thinking behind spaced practice. Instead of stacking more repetitions on the same day, spread them out, and each return will do more work.

# The keyword method
@src Atkinson & Raugh, 1975

To learn a foreign word, find an English word that sounds like it, then make a picture that connects the two. For the Spanish word "pato," meaning duck, you might imagine a duck wearing a pot on its head. The sound gets you from "pato" to "pot," and the picture gets you from "pot" to duck.

Richard Atkinson and his colleagues tested the method in the 1970s and found that students using it learned vocabulary faster at first. It works best for concrete words, which are easy to picture, and it's less helpful for abstract ones. Some research suggests the advantage fades over longer periods unless the words are practiced. It's a good way to get a word into your head quickly, and then regular use has to keep it there.

# Memory champions have ordinary brains
@src Maguire et al., 2003

In 2003 Eleanor Maguire and her colleagues scanned the brains of people who rank among the best memorizers in the world. They found no unusual structure. These were ordinary brains, and they performed no better than other people's on general memory tests.

What distinguished them was how they remembered. Almost all used the method of loci, imagining a familiar journey and placing items along it. In other words, their results came from technique and practice and not from some special gift. That's encouraging, since it means a trained memory is available to anyone willing to put in the time. It also shows that "I have a bad memory" often means "I haven't learned how to use it."

# Interference
@src Jenkins & Dallenbach, 1924

Forgetting isn't always fading. Often one memory gets in the way of another. In 1924 John Jenkins and Karl Dallenbach had two students learn lists of nonsense syllables and then either go to sleep or stay awake. After the same number of hours, they remembered more after sleep.

When you're asleep, less new material comes in to interfere. Old things can disturb new ones, and new things can disturb old ones, and both happen all the time. It helps explain why you remember your own phone number but mix up the ones you used years ago, and why details from similar events blur together. Giving a memory some quiet time, or making it distinctive, can protect it from being overwritten.

# Savings in relearning
@src Ebbinghaus, 1885

Hermann Ebbinghaus, who started the scientific study of memory in the 1880s, noticed something. A list of syllables that he could no longer recall at all still took him fewer repetitions to learn a second time. He called the difference "savings."

The memory had faded, but it had not vanished. Something remained that made relearning quicker. It's an encouraging idea. If you've studied something before, even years ago, you'll relearn it faster than someone starting from scratch, even if you feel you've forgotten everything. So the time you spent isn't lost. A language from school or a math course you thought was long gone may come back more quickly than you expect.

# Attention is a spotlight
@src Cherry, 1953

In 1953 Colin Cherry studied how people follow one conversation in a noisy room, which became known as the cocktail-party effect. You can concentrate on one speaker and tune out the others, even when several people are talking at once.

Yet if someone across the room says your name, you often notice. So your attention filters, but not completely. Some of what you're ignoring is still being processed in the background, and something important or familiar can break through. It's one reason it's hard to concentrate in a busy place, and a good reason to find a quiet one when you're trying to learn something hard.

# Inattentional blindness
@src Simons & Chabris, 1999; Drew et al., 2013

In the famous invisible gorilla study, people counting basketball passes often failed to see a person in a gorilla suit walk through the scene, pound its chest and walk off. They were looking straight at it. Their attention was simply somewhere else.

A similar thing has been found in medicine. In 2013 researchers asked radiologists to look for lung nodules in a set of scans. In the last scan, they had placed a picture of a gorilla about 48 times the size of a typical nodule. Most of the radiologists, 83 percent, didn't see it. Experts are not immune. We see what we're looking for, and we can miss a great deal of what's right in front of us.

# Change blindness
@src Simons & Levin, 1998

We tend to think we'd notice a big change in our surroundings. In a 1998 field study by Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin, an experimenter stopped a pedestrian and asked for directions. While they talked, two men carrying a door walked between them, and as they passed, the experimenter was swapped with a different person.

About half of the pedestrians didn't notice that the person they were talking to had changed. They carried on giving directions to someone who was a different height, with different clothes and a different voice. We don't hold a complete picture of what's in front of us. We hold a rough sketch, and we fill in the rest, which is why a change can slip by unseen.

# The attentional blink
@src Raymond, Shapiro & Arnell, 1992

If you're watching a fast stream of images and looking for two particular targets, you'll often notice the first but miss the second when it comes within about half a second. In 1992 Jane Raymond, Kimron Shapiro and Karen Arnell described the effect and called it the attentional blink.

Processing the first target seems to use up your attention for a moment, so the next one slips past. It lasts only a fraction of a second, between about 200 and 500 milliseconds. But it's a fine illustration of how limited our attention is, even when we're alert and trying our best. Your mind isn't a camera that records everything. It's a spotlight, and while it's busy with one thing, the next may go unseen.

# Task switching has a cost
@src Monsell, 2003

People often believe they can do two things at once. Mostly, they're switching rapidly between them, and every switch costs something. In lab experiments, people were slower and made more mistakes on the first trial after a change of task, even when they knew the switch was coming.

Part of the cost is that your mind has to drop one set of rules and load another, and part is that bits of the old task keep interfering. Doing one task at a time and finishing it generally takes less total time than bouncing between several. That includes checking messages in the middle of work. If you can, put your phone out of reach for a stretch and let yourself stay with one thing.

# Mind wandering
@src Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010

In 2010 Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert built an app that pinged thousands of people at random moments and asked what they were doing and what they were thinking about. People's minds were wandering in 46.9% of the samples, and this was true whatever the activity.

They also reported being less happy when their minds wandered than when they were focused on what they were doing. Even when the daydream was pleasant, it didn't make people happier. That doesn't mean that all daydreaming is bad, since wandering can help with planning and creativity. But it suggests that a lot of our unhappiness has less to do with what's happening and more to do with where our attention is.

# Flow
@src Csikszentmihalyi, 1990

The psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi studied people who got completely absorbed in activities, such as rock climbers, chess players and surgeons. He called the state flow. They described losing track of themselves and of time, and of being wholly caught up in the task.

It tends to appear when a task is challenging enough to need all your attention but not so hard that it overwhelms you, and when the goals are clear and you get quick feedback on how you're doing. Time seems to disappear. It's one reason that matching difficulty to your skill makes work more enjoyable. If you're bored, raise the challenge a little. If you're anxious, make the task smaller until you find your footing.

# The Yerkes-Dodson law
@src Yerkes & Dodson, 1908
@conf mixed

In 1908 Robert Yerkes and John Dodson trained mice to avoid shocks and found that moderate shocks led to the best learning. Shocks that were too weak or too strong did worse. Psychologists later extended this idea into a rule that performance rises with arousal up to a point and then falls, giving an upside-down U.

Later work suggests that the best level depends on the task. Harder tasks do best with lower arousal, and easy ones can handle more. It's a rough guide and not a precise law, and the original study was about mice and shocks, not about people and deadlines. Still, it fits a familiar experience. A little pressure sharpens you, and a lot of pressure can make you freeze.

# Sleep and the next day's learning
@src Yoo et al., 2007

In 2007 Seung-Schik Yoo, Matthew Walker and their colleagues kept some volunteers awake all night and let others sleep, and then showed everyone pictures to remember. The next day, the people who'd been up all night remembered about 40 percent fewer of the pictures.

Brain scans showed less activity in the hippocampus, a region important for forming memories. So sleep matters for learning before you study as well as after. It's easy to think of sleep as time taken away from studying, but a tired brain takes in new things poorly. If you have a choice between an extra hour of cramming and an extra hour of sleep the night before you need to learn something, the sleep may be the better investment.

# Naps help
@src Mednick et al., 2003

In 2003 Sara Mednick and her colleagues tested people on a visual task that gets better with practice. Those who had a nap of 60 to 90 minutes improved as much as people who had been allowed a full night's sleep. Those who didn't nap got worse over the course of the day.

A well-timed nap can restore some of your ability to learn. A short one of about 20 minutes may be better for just feeling alert, since it keeps you out of deep sleep and you wake up less groggy. If you're facing a heavy day of studying and you feel your mind getting dull, a nap can be a better answer than another coffee. It may not make up for a bad night completely, but it helps.

# The Baby Einstein question
@src Zimmerman, Christakis & Meltzoff, 2007
@conf mixed

Videos for babies were sold in the 2000s as a way to boost their learning. In 2007 Frederick Zimmerman and his colleagues surveyed parents of infants aged 8 to 16 months. For each daily hour of baby videos, the children knew a few fewer words than babies who didn't watch them.

Researchers can't be sure the videos caused the difference, since parents who use videos more may differ in other ways. But the study gave no sign that the videos helped. What does help babies learn words is talking, reading and playing with the people around them. Babies learn language from conversation, with another person who is responding to them, and a screen can't do that. The most useful thing you can do is talk.

# Retrieval cues
@src Tulving & Pearlstone, 1966

Endel Tulving and Zena Pearlstone showed in 1966 that a memory can seem lost until the right cue appears. People who couldn't recall a list of words suddenly could when they were given the names of the categories as hints. The memory was there, but they couldn't reach it.

That gives a different way of thinking about forgetting. Sometimes it isn't that the information is gone, but that you have no way in. It's a reason to leave yourself cues when you study, such as a keyword or a note in the margin. It also explains why a smell or a song can bring back a whole scene. It was part of the original memory, so it now works like a key.

# Overconfidence in learning
@src Roediger & Karpicke, 2006

In a well-known 2006 study, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students read a passage and then either read it again or take a test on it. The students who had reread felt more prepared. A week later, the ones who had been tested remembered far more.

Rereading creates a feeling of familiarity, and that is easy to mistake for knowledge. The words look known, so you assume that you know them. Testing yourself feels harder, and that's partly why it works. Bringing something out of memory strengthens it, in a way that reading it again doesn't. So when you study, close the book and try to say what you remember. The struggle is a sign that you're learning, not that you're failing.

# Learning from mistakes
@src Kornell, Hays & Bjork, 2009

You might think that guessing wrong would plant the wrong answer in your mind. In a 2009 study, Nate Kornell, Matthew Hays and Robert Bjork had people try to guess the answers to questions before being shown the correct one. Even when the guesses were wrong, they learned the right answer better than people who had just been shown it.

A wrong guess followed by quick feedback seems to prepare the mind to take in the answer, and the attempt makes you curious. So it's often better to try before you look, even when you don't expect to succeed. It also takes some of the sting out of being wrong. A mistake you notice right away isn't a failure of learning. It's part of how learning works.

# The hypercorrection effect
@src Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2001

Surprisingly, the more confident you are in a wrong answer, the more likely you are to remember the correction. In 2001 Brady Butterfield and Janet Metcalfe found that mistakes made with high confidence were corrected more often than those made with low confidence.

The surprise of learning that you were wrong when you were sure seems to grab your attention and fix the right answer in place. So there's no need to feel embarrassed about being wrong, especially when you were sure. A confident mistake is one of the best ways to learn, as long as you get the correction. What doesn't work is hiding the mistake, because then you never get the chance to replace it.
