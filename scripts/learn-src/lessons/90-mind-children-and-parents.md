# Screen time in the toddler years
@src Madigan, Browne, Racine, Mori & Tough, JAMA Pediatrics, 2019

How does the amount of time that young children spend in front of screens affect their development? In 2019 Sheri Madigan and her colleagues in Canada followed about twenty-five hundred children in Calgary. Their mothers reported how many hours a day the children watched television and used other screens at the ages of two, three and five. The children were assessed on communication, motor skills, problem solving and social skills at each age.

Children who had more screen time at two and three did worse on the tests at three and five. The link held after accounting for family income and parenting. It does not prove that screens caused the delays, since a child who is developing more slowly may get more screen time. But the pattern was consistent, and it fits the advice of pediatricians that young children need talk, play and interaction more than screens.

# The shy infants
@src Kagan & Snidman, The Long Shadow of Temperament, 2004

In the 1980s the Harvard psychologist Jerome Kagan studied four-month-old babies. He showed them new things, such as mobiles, voices and smells. About one baby in five reacted strongly, with vigorous movements, arching of the back and crying. About two in five were calm. He called the first group high-reactive and the second low-reactive.

He followed the children for years. Many of the high-reactive infants became shy and cautious toddlers, and some were anxious as teenagers. Many of the low-reactive children were bold and outgoing. The link was far from perfect, and experiences changed the course for many children. High-reactive children raised by warm and encouraging parents were often less shy. Still, it was strong evidence that people differ in temperament from the first months of life.

# Do parents matter less than peers?
@src Harris, The Nurture Assumption, 1998
@conf mixed

In 1998 the writer Judith Rich Harris published a book that made a provocative claim. Her argument was that parents have much less effect on how their children turn out than most people think. She pointed to studies of twins and adopted children, which show that genes explain a large share of personality, and that the shared home environment explains very little. She argued that the other big influence is peers.

Children, she said, learn how to behave from the groups they belong to, and not from their parents. They may behave one way at home and another way at school. The book was a bestseller, and was criticized strongly. Critics said that parents matter for many things, such as values, health habits and a child's sense of security, and that parents influence which peers a child has. Most psychologists now accept a part of her argument, but not the strong version.

# Books in the house
@src Evans, Kelley, Sikora & Treiman, Research in Social Stratification and Mobility, 2010
@conf mixed

In 2010 Mariah Evans and her colleagues analyzed surveys from twenty-seven countries, with more than seventy thousand people. Each respondent was asked about how many books there had been in their home when they were sixteen, and how much education they had attained. They compared the results, while accounting for the education of the parents, and the family's wealth.

The more books there were at home, the more years of schooling the person went on to complete. Growing up in a home with five hundred books or more was associated with about three years more education than growing up with very few. Even a small library, of twenty books, made a difference. The study can't prove that books were the cause, since homes with books differ in many ways. But it's a reminder of how reading at home may support learning.

# How much parents talk
@src Weisleder & Fernald, Psychological Science, 2013

Do toddlers learn more words if their parents talk to them more? In 2013 Adriana Weisleder and Anne Fernald studied twenty-nine Spanish-speaking families with low incomes in California. They recorded entire days in the homes of the children, at the age of nineteen months, using a small recorder in a vest, and counted the words that were spoken to the children.

The amount of speech that was directed at the child varied very widely from one family to another. At twenty-four months, the children who heard more speech from caregivers knew more words, and were quicker at recognizing them. Speech that was just overheard in the room was not related to learning. What counted was talk that was directed at the child. It supports the advice to chat with babies and toddlers.

# What parents' praise teaches
@src Gunderson et al., Child Development, 2013

In 2013 Elizabeth Gunderson, Susan Levine and their colleagues at the University of Chicago studied how parents praise their young children. They made video recordings of fifty-three parents and children, at home, on three occasions, when the children were between fourteen and thirty-eight months old. They counted the praise that focused on the child's effort or strategy, such as you worked hard, and the praise that focused on the child's traits, such as you're so smart.

When the children were seven or eight, they were asked questions about their beliefs. Those whose parents had given more praise for effort, when they were toddlers, were more likely to believe that intelligence can grow with effort. The link was stronger than for any other aspect of the parents' style that they measured. It shows that the habits of praise may start to shape a child's mindset very early.

# Does more time with mother help?
@src Milkie, Nomaguchi & Denny, Journal of Marriage and Family, 2015

Many parents worry that they don't spend enough time with their children. In 2015 Melissa Milkie, Kei Nomaguchi and Kathleen Denny analyzed data from a large American study, which followed more than a thousand families from 1997 to 2014. They looked at the hours that mothers spent with their children, and at how the children fared, in behavior, emotions and test scores.

For children aged three to eleven, the amount of time that mothers spent with them made almost no difference to the outcomes. What mattered more was the quality of the time, and the mother's stress. A stressed, tired mother seemed to do harm. For adolescents, time spent together was related to fewer delinquent behaviors, though not to other measures. The findings suggest that the quality of time, and the parents' well-being, matter more than the number of hours.

# When children begin to lie
@src Talwar & Lee, Int. J. Behavioral Development, 2002; Child Development, 2011

Almost all children lie at some time, and it starts early. In 2002 Victoria Talwar and Kang Lee tested the honesty of young children with a peeking game. A child was left alone in a room with a toy hidden behind them, and told not to peek. A researcher returned and asked whether the child had peeked. Many of the three-year-olds lied, about a third. By the age of seven, most did.

The older children also lied more skillfully. They could keep their stories consistent, and faked a surprised reaction. In 2011 the researchers found that children in a school with harsh punishments lied more than those in a school with a gentler approach. Lying is a sign of developing thinking. It requires a child to understand that others have beliefs which can be changed. Punishment makes it more likely, and not less.
