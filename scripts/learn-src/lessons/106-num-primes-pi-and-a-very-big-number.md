# The gap between primes, in the end, is bounded
@src Zhang, Annals of Mathematics, 2014; Maynard, 2013; Polymath8

Prime numbers get rarer as the numbers get larger, so you might expect the gaps between one prime and the next to grow without limit. The twin prime conjecture says that there are infinitely many pairs of primes that differ by just two, such as 11 and 13, or 17 and 19. Nobody has proved it. In April 2013 a little-known mathematician, Yitang Zhang, who was a lecturer at the University of New Hampshire, showed that there are infinitely many pairs of primes that differ by less than 70 million.

Seventy million is a large gap, but it is finite, and that was the point. Within months, a collaborative online project of many mathematicians brought it down to 4,680. Then James Maynard, a young researcher, and Terence Tao, found a simpler method, and the Polymath project reduced the bound to 246. Nobody has got it down to two. But the first step was taken, by someone who had spent years without an academic post.

# Estimating pi with a needle
@src Buffon, 1777; Badger, Mathematics Magazine, 1994

Draw parallel lines on the floor, a distance t apart, and drop a needle of length l, no longer than t, at random. The French naturalist Buffon showed that the chance that the needle crosses a line is two times l, divided by pi times t. The odds depend on pi. So you can estimate pi by dropping a needle many times and counting how often it crosses. It was published in 1777, and it is one of the first problems solved with geometric probability.

In 1901 an Italian mathematician, Mario Lazzarini, reported that he had done it, with a needle of 25 millimeters and lines 30 millimeters apart. In 3,408 tosses, he got 1,808 crossings, and that gave 355 over 113, or 3.1415929, correct to six places. It is too good. The number of tosses is 213 times 16, which suggests that he stopped when the count gave the answer that he wanted. It is a standard example of confirmation bias in science.

# The days that vanished
@series history
@src Gregory XIII, 1582; Calendar (New Style) Act, 1750

The Julian calendar, which Julius Caesar introduced, had a year that was slightly too long, by about eleven minutes. Over centuries the error built up, and by the sixteenth century the spring equinox, which the Church used to fix Easter, had slipped by ten days. In 1582 Pope Gregory XIII decreed a new calendar, with a better rule for leap years. To catch up, ten days were dropped. In Spain, Portugal and most of Italy, Thursday, October 4, was followed by Friday, October 15.

Protestant countries held out for a long time. Britain and its colonies changed in 1752, when the gap was eleven days, so that September 2 was followed by September 14. There is a popular story that people rioted and shouted, Give us our eleven days. Historians have found little evidence of it, and the main source seems to be a banner in a painting by Hogarth. Some rural people did keep celebrating Christmas on the old date, in January.

# The student who was Monsieur Le Blanc
@series people
@src Germain, letters to Gauss, 1804 to 1807

Sophie Germain was born in Paris in 1776. As a girl she taught herself mathematics from books in her father's library, reading at night, in spite of her family's objections. Women could not enroll at the École Polytechnique, but she managed to obtain the lecture notes of the courses. She sent in her solutions to the problems under the name of a former student, Monsieur Le Blanc. The professor Joseph-Louis Lagrange was struck by the quality, and asked to meet the author. When he found out, he became her mentor.

In 1804 she wrote to the great mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss, again as Le Blanc, about number theory and Fermat's Last Theorem. She said that she feared the ridicule that attached to a woman who studied science. Her main result, now called Germain's theorem, proved the first case of the Last Theorem for any odd prime p, when two times p plus one is also prime. It was the most significant progress for a long time. Legendre published it, in a footnote.

# The first woman professor of mathematics
@series people
@src Kovalevskaya, 1874 to 1891

Sofia Kovalevskaya was born in Russia in 1850. Women could not go to university in her country, so she married, in a marriage of convenience, to be able to travel abroad. In Germany she could not enroll either, and she studied privately with the great mathematician Karl Weierstrass. In 1874 the University of Göttingen gave her a doctorate, summa cum laude, without an oral examination, on the strength of three papers, one on partial differential equations.

She was the first woman to receive a doctorate in mathematics in modern times. Jobs were hard to find, but in 1883 she was invited to lecture at the University of Stockholm, and in 1889 she became a full professor. In 1888 she won the Bordin Prize of the Paris Academy, for her work on how a spinning top moves, and the judges raised the prize because her paper was so good. She died in 1891, at the age of forty-one.

# A tiling that never repeats
@src Penrose, 1974; Shechtman, 1982

If you tile a bathroom floor, the pattern repeats, and you can shift it and it matches itself. It was long believed that any pattern with the order of a crystal had to repeat in this way, and that five-fold symmetry, like that of a star with five points, was impossible. In 1974 the mathematician Roger Penrose found two shapes of tile that cover a plane with fivefold symmetry, in a pattern that is ordered but never repeats exactly.

In 1982 the chemist Dan Shechtman saw, in an electron microscope, a metal alloy whose atoms formed a pattern of this kind. His colleagues did not believe him, and the famous chemist Linus Pauling said that there were no quasicrystals, only quasi-scientists. More examples were found, and quasicrystals were accepted as a new kind of solid. In 2011 Shechtman won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. A pattern from mathematics had been found in nature.

# The salesman who visited forty-eight states
@src Dantzig, Fulkerson & Johnson, Operations Research, 1954

A traveling salesman wants to visit a set of cities, once each, and return home, by the shortest route. With only a few cities it is easy. With more, the number of possible routes grows so fast that even the fastest computers cannot check them all. For 20 cities there are more than a million billion tours. Despite its simple form, the problem has become one of the best known in computer science.

In 1954 George Dantzig, Ray Fulkerson and Selmer Johnson, at the RAND Corporation, found the shortest road route that visits one city in each of the 48 states and Washington, D.C. They did not try the routes. They used linear programming, and added extra conditions that cut away false solutions, and they solved by hand, with a drawing. They worked on 42 cities and extended it to 49. Their method of adding cuts is still at the heart of the programs that solve such problems.

# Waves inside a hot iron bar
@src Fourier, Théorie analytique de la chaleur, 1822

Joseph Fourier was a French mathematician who went with Napoleon's army to Egypt, and later served as a prefect. In his spare time he studied how heat flows through a solid. In 1807 he claimed that the temperature along a bar, however irregular, could be written as a sum of simple waves, sines and cosines of different frequencies. Some of the leading mathematicians of the day doubted it. His book on the subject appeared in 1822.

The idea turned out to be one of the most useful in science. Any signal, whether a sound, an image, or a radio wave, can be broken into its component frequencies, and put together again. The methods used to store pictures in a file, to compress music, and to clean noise from a recording are descendants of his idea. He also wrote in the 1820s that the atmosphere helps to keep the Earth warm, an early statement of the greenhouse effect.

# A number too big to write down
@src Gardner, Scientific American, 1977; Graham and Rothschild, 1971

In 1971 Ronald Graham and Bruce Rothschild proved a result in a branch of mathematics called Ramsey theory, about how large a group of points must be before a certain pattern has to appear. They could not say exactly how large, but they could give an upper limit. It was an enormous number, so large that it could not be written in the ordinary way. In 1977 the writer Martin Gardner described a simpler, slightly larger number in his column in Scientific American, and it was called Graham's number.

It is built up in 64 steps, each of which makes the number unimaginably larger than the one before. If you could write a digit on every particle in the observable universe, there would not be room to write the number. It is still a definite number, and mathematicians can work out that its last digits are 2464195387. The best known limit is now far smaller, and the true answer is thought to be small, perhaps six.

# Two balls from one
@src Banach & Tarski, 1924

In 1924 the Polish mathematicians Stefan Banach and Alfred Tarski proved something that sounds impossible. A solid ball can be cut into a finite number of pieces, which can be moved around and put back together to make two balls, each the same size as the original. It was later shown that five pieces are enough. It is a theorem, which follows from the usual rules of mathematics, and it is called the Banach-Tarski paradox.

It does not mean that you could double a ball of gold. The pieces are not solid chunks. They are so complicated, with infinitely scattered points, that they have no volume, and they depend on the axiom of choice, a rule that lets you pick from infinite collections without saying how. Mathematicians do not call it a contradiction. It shows that our idea of volume breaks down for sets that are strange enough, and that infinity has surprises.

# Three rules for chance
@src Kolmogorov, Foundations of the Theory of Probability, 1933

For centuries people used probability, in gambling, in insurance, in science, without agreeing on exactly what it was. In 1933 the Russian mathematician Andrey Kolmogorov, aged thirty, published a short book that put it on a firm footing. He started from three rules. A probability is never less than zero. The probability that something in the whole set of possible outcomes happens is one. And for outcomes that cannot occur together, the probability that one or the other happens is the sum.

Everything else, such as the laws of large numbers, can be derived from those three. His approach used the language of sets and measure, which mathematicians had developed to deal with areas and volumes. It let them treat chance in the same way as they treated any other branch of mathematics. Today it is the standard definition, and it is the foundation of statistics, of machine learning and of finance.

# The machine that draws the bell curve
@src Galton, Natural Inheritance, 1889

A Galton board is a board with rows of pegs set in a triangle. A ball is dropped at the top. At each peg, it falls to the left or right, with equal chance, and in the end it lands in a slot at the bottom. Drop one ball and its path is unpredictable. Drop a thousand, and the heaps in the slots form a smooth hump, with most balls near the middle and a few at the edges.

Francis Galton, who invented it in the 1800s, used it to show how many small chance events add up to the bell curve, the normal distribution. A ball's final position is the sum of many small steps. The same thing happens with heights, test errors, and many other measurements. The shape is so common because of what is called the central limit theorem. A pattern can come out of a crowd of random events.

# A bottle with no inside
@src Klein, 1882

A Möbius strip is a band with a half twist, so that it has only one side. In 1882 the German mathematician Felix Klein described a surface that is like it, in one more dimension. It has no edge, and it has no inside and no outside. You can think of it as a tube whose end passes back through the side of the tube, and joins its own beginning. It is called the Klein bottle.

In three dimensions it cannot be built without the surface passing through itself, since it needs a fourth dimension to close up without a crossing. Glassblowers make models that cross themselves. It is a useful object in topology, which studies the properties of shapes that do not change when they are stretched. Two Möbius strips glued together along their edges make one. It is an example of a shape that we can define, but cannot hold.
