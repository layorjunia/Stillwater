# Questions pull answers
@src Sacks, Schegloff & Jefferson, Language, 1974

Some things in conversation come in pairs. A question calls for an answer, a greeting for a greeting, an offer for an acceptance or a refusal. In the 1960s and 70s the sociologists Harvey Sacks, Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson recorded ordinary talk and studied it line by line. They called these pairs adjacency pairs, and they showed that the first part sets up a strong expectation of the second.

You notice the expectation when it isn't met. If you say hello and get silence, it feels odd. If you ask a question and the other person doesn't reply, the silence itself is heard as a message. Other people can see that something is missing, and they interpret why. The pairs are a kind of hidden grammar of everyday talk, and we follow them without knowing that we do.

# Why a "no" comes slower than a "yes"
@src Pomerantz, 1984; Kendrick & Torreira, Discourse Processes, 2015

When you invite someone to dinner, and they want to come, they usually say so right away. When they don't, the answer tends to come after a pause, and begins with a softener, such as "well" or "uh." In the 1980s Anita Pomerantz described this pattern as preference organization. Some responses are preferred, in the sense that they fit what the first speaker hoped for, and these come quickly and plainly.

In 2015 Kobin Kendrick and Francisco Torreira measured the gaps in a large set of recorded conversations in English. Responses that agreed with a request or an invitation came in sooner than those that turned it down, by a fraction of a second. We hear that small delay as a sign that a refusal is coming, and the speaker who delays is cushioning it. It's part of how people spare each other's feelings without saying so.

# Who speaks when the phone rings?
@src Schegloff, American Anthropologist, 1968

When the phone rings, who speaks first? You probably say that it is the caller. In fact, it is the person who answers. In 1968 Emanuel Schegloff analyzed hundreds of recorded telephone calls. In nearly all of them, the person who picked up said hello first, and the caller replied. He argued that the ringing of the phone is a summons, like a call across the room, and that the answer is the response.

This leaves the caller to speak second, and to identify themselves, or to ask for someone. It's a small piece of order in something we don't think about. Schegloff found that the opening of a call has a clear structure, with a summons, an answer, an identification and a greeting. The structure varies from culture to culture, and from phone to phone, but the idea of a summons and an answer holds.

# Why goodbyes take so long
@src Schegloff & Sacks, Semiotica, 1973

Ending a conversation is harder than it looks. We rarely just say goodbye and leave. We say, "Well," or "OK," and then there is a pause, and some more talk, and finally the goodbyes. In 1973 Emanuel Schegloff and Harvey Sacks studied how people close conversations. They found that the end has to be negotiated, because neither person wants to cut the other off.

A word like "well" or "so" signals a wish to end. It gives the other person a chance to bring up something else, or to agree. When both agree, they move to a closing, often with a recap of plans, such as "I'll talk to you soon." Only after this do they say goodbye. It's a collaboration, and it shows how much of talk is about managing the relationship.

# Switching languages in mid-sentence
@src Poplack, Linguistics, 1980

In bilingual communities, people often slip between two languages in the same conversation, or even in the same sentence. It was once thought to be a sign of confusion, or of poor knowledge of both languages. In 1980 Shana Poplack studied Puerto Rican Spanish speakers in New York, who moved between Spanish and English as they talked. She found that the switching followed rules.

The speakers who switched the most were often the most fluent in both languages. They switched at points where the grammar of the two languages lines up, so the sentence stayed correct in each. Code-switching, as it's called, is a skill. It can also express identity, or add emphasis, or show that you belong to a particular community. It's a good example of how everyday speech can be more systematic than it seems.

# Where are the women's shoes?
@src Labov, The Social Stratification of English in New York City, 1966

In 1962 the linguist William Labov wanted to see whether the way New Yorkers pronounce the letter r reflects social class. He went to three department stores. One was expensive, one was medium priced, and one was cheap. He asked the salespeople where to find something that he knew was on the fourth floor, and, pretending that he hadn't heard, asked them to repeat it.

In the expensive store, the clerks were most likely to pronounce the r in "fourth floor." In the cheap store, they were least likely. In every store, they pronounced it more clearly the second time, when they spoke carefully. Labov concluded that pronouncing the r was seen as prestigious, and that speakers shifted toward it when they were conscious of being listened to. The study is a landmark for the way that it studied speech in real life.

# Speaking like an islander
@src Labov, Word, 1963

On the island of Martha's Vineyard, off the coast of Massachusetts, the vowels in words such as "right" and "house" are pronounced in a distinctive way. In 1961 William Labov visited the island to study this. He found that the older, local pronunciation was becoming more common among some young islanders, and not less, though tourists and mainland influences were growing.

Those who used it most were the ones who wanted to stay on the island and who identified as islanders, often fishermen. Those who planned to leave for the mainland used it least. The way that people spoke signaled who they were and where they belonged. Labov showed that language changes are not just random drift. They can be a way of saying who you are, and of marking a group.

# How a voice changes the way you are judged
@src Lambert, Hodgson, Gardner & Fillenbaum, Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1960

In 1960 Wallace Lambert and his colleagues in Montreal invented a method to study attitudes toward languages. They recorded bilingual speakers reading the same passage once in English and once in French. Listeners heard the recordings, but didn't know that each speaker appeared twice. They were asked to rate the personality of each speaker, such as how intelligent, kind or ambitious they seemed.

English-speaking listeners rated the voices more favorably when they spoke English than when they spoke French, though they were the same people. Surprisingly, French-speaking listeners did the same, rating the English guises higher. The method, called the matched-guise technique, shows how stereotypes attach to languages and accents. People are judged by how they sound, in ways that have nothing to do with who they are.

# Hearing an accent that isn't there
@src Rubin, Research in Higher Education, 1992

In 1992 Donald Rubin played American undergraduates a short lecture on a neutral topic. All of them heard the same recording, spoken by a native speaker of American English. For half of the students, a photograph of a white woman was shown, and they were told that she was the lecturer. For the others, a photograph of an Asian woman was shown.

Afterward, the students took a test on how much they remembered. Those who thought that the lecturer was Asian understood less, and many said that they could hear an accent. They did hear the same voice. Their expectation had changed how they listened. It's a clear example of how prejudice can make a listener work less hard, and then blame the speaker.

# Accent is not the same as understanding
@src Munro & Derwing, Language Learning, 1995

When we hear a foreign accent, it can feel as if the speech is hard to understand. In 1995 Murray Munro and Tracey Derwing tested this. They recorded speakers who were learning English, and asked native listeners to rate how strong the accent was. They also had the listeners write down what the speakers said, to see how much was understood.

The accent ratings and the understanding were related, but not closely. Many speakers with a strong accent were fully understood, and being understood depended on other things, such as the rhythm and the stress of the words. Intelligibility, the amount of speech that the listener gets, isn't the same as accent. It suggests that the aim of language teaching should be to be understood, and not to sound like a native.

# Four kinds of humor
@src Martin, Puhlik-Doris, Larsen, Gray & Weir, Journal of Research in Personality, 2003

Not all humor is the same. In 2003 Rod Martin and his colleagues developed a questionnaire that separates four styles. Affiliative humor is joking and teasing that brings people together. Self-enhancing humor is seeing the funny side of life's difficulties. Aggressive humor is ridicule that puts other people down. Self-defeating humor is making fun of oneself in order to be liked.

The first two styles were associated with better well-being, less depression and healthier relationships. The last two were not. Aggressive humor went with more hostility, and self-defeating humor went with more anxiety and lower self-esteem. It suggests that having a sense of humor is good for you, but only if you use it in a way that's kind to you and others.

# Just teasing
@src Keltner, Capps, Kring, Young & Heerey, Psychological Bulletin, 2001

Teasing is a mix of friendliness and aggression, and it is very common. In 2001 Dacher Keltner and his colleagues reviewed what is known about it. Teasing can be a way of bonding, showing affection through play. It can also be used to push away, to criticize or to enforce a norm.

What separates the two is the way it is done. Playful teasing is usually exaggerated, with a smile and a tone that signals that it's not meant seriously. It tends to be about things that the person can easily laugh at. Teasing about something sensitive, from someone who is not close, with no signs of play, is more likely to hurt. The same words can bond or wound, depending on the signals around them.

# Speaking powerfully
@src Erickson, Lind, Johnson & O'Barr, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 1978

In the 1970s the linguist William O'Barr and his colleagues studied how witnesses talked in court. They noticed that some used what they called powerless speech, with hedges such as "sort of," hesitations such as "um," polite forms, and rising intonation at the end of statements. Others spoke in a more direct way.

In 1978 Bonnie Erickson and her colleagues tested the effect. Mock jurors listened to recordings of the same testimony in the two styles. The witnesses who spoke in the powerless style were seen as less convincing, less competent and less trustworthy. The pattern was linked to status and not to gender, since people of low status in court, men and women, were more likely to use it. It suggests that how we say things changes how our words are weighed.

# How an interviewer's manner changes an applicant
@src Word, Zanna & Cooper, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 1974

In 1974 Carl Word, Mark Zanna and Joel Cooper ran a two-part study. In the first part, white college students interviewed job applicants who were actually part of the study, some of whom were Black and some white. The interviewers sat farther away from the Black applicants, made more speech errors, and cut the interviews shorter.

In the second part, trained interviewers treated white applicants in either of the two styles, the closer, warmer style or the more distant, less engaged one. The applicants who were treated in the distant way performed worse, and later said that they felt less comfortable. Our behavior can shape the behavior of others, in ways that confirm our expectations. This is one way that bias can create the very differences that it assumes.

# Half of a phone call
@src Emberson, Lupyan, Goldstein & Spivey, Psychological Science, 2010

Why is it so annoying to overhear someone talking on a phone? You can only hear one side. In 2010 Lauren Emberson and her colleagues tested whether this is more distracting than hearing both sides. People worked on tasks that needed attention while they heard either one side of a phone call, or a conversation between two people, or a monologue.

The half of a call was the most distracting. People did worse on their tasks, and they remembered more of the content of the overheard call. The researchers suggested that when we can only hear half of a conversation, we can't predict what will come next, and our minds work to fill in the gaps. This takes attention. The unpredictability is what makes it annoying.

# A deeper voice wins votes
@src Klofstad, Anderson & Peters, Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 2012
@conf mixed

In 2012 Casey Klofstad and his colleagues recorded men and women saying the words "I urge you to vote for me this November." They then changed the pitch of each recording, and made one version higher and one lower. They played the pairs to voters, and asked which candidate they would choose.

Voters preferred the lower-pitched voice, for both men and women. They saw it as stronger and more competent. In a later study of real elections, candidates with deeper voices were somewhat more likely to win. The effect is small, and it may reflect stereotypes about leadership more than any real difference in ability. But it is a case where a feature of the voice that has nothing to do with the message may influence what we think of the speaker.

# Does a font change what we believe?
@src Morris, New York Times, 2012
@conf mixed

In 2012 the filmmaker Errol Morris ran a large online experiment with the New York Times. Readers took a quiz, which started with a short passage by a scientist about the chances that humans would survive for a long time. The passage was shown in one of six fonts. At the end, readers were asked whether they agreed that the writer was optimistic about the future of humanity.

The people who saw the passage in the font Baskerville were somewhat more likely to agree with it than those who saw it in the others, such as Comic Sans. The difference was small, and there are many reasons the fonts might differ. It is not a controlled study. But it fits with research showing that how easy something is to read affects whether we believe it. A clear typeface can add a little credibility.
