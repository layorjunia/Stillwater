# Writing: from clay marks to the alphabet
@src Schmandt-Besserat; Standard history of writing

Writing began in Sumer, in what is now southern Iraq, around 3400 to 3200 BC. It seems to have started out as a bookkeeping tool, with little clay tokens and marks pressed into clay to keep track of grain, sheep and beer. Over the centuries, the marks became wedge-shaped signs, called cuneiform, that could stand for sounds. Then people could write names, laws, letters and stories, and the oldest known literature was born.

Most of the world's alphabets descend from a different, later invention. Around 1800 BC, workers from Canaan in Egypt's Sinai turquoise mines adapted a few Egyptian hieroglyphs into a simple script, with a sign for each sound. The Phoenicians spread it around the Mediterranean, the Greeks added vowels, and the Romans passed their version on to us. A system of about two dozen signs was far easier to learn than hundreds, and it put reading within reach of ordinary people.

# The wheel
@src Gasser & Velušček, 2003

The first wheels weren't for carts. They turned the potter's wheel, and wheeled vehicles appear a little later, around 3500 BC. A wheel and axle found in the Ljubljana marshes in Slovenia has been dated to about 3150 BC, among the oldest known.

It seems odd that the wheel came well after boats and plows, but it's actually hard. The axle and the hole have to fit very closely, and that takes skilled work. A wheel that spins on a fixed axle, or an axle that turns with the wheel, needs careful carpentry. Many people in the ancient Americas never used wheeled carts for transport, even though they knew the idea, partly because they had no large animals to pull them.

# Göbekli Tepe and the start of farming
@src Schmidt, 2006; Standard archaeology text

In 1994 the German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt began to dig on a hilltop in southeastern Turkey, and found huge T-shaped stone pillars carved with animals, arranged in circles. It was built about 11,500 years ago, before farming, and before pottery. That overturned the idea that people first settled down in towns and only then built monuments. Perhaps the need to gather for such a place helped to start settled life.

About 11,000 years ago, in a region of the Middle East called the Fertile Crescent, people began to plant seeds on purpose. Wheat, barley, lentils and peas were among the first crops. Farming let people stay in one place, store food and support larger numbers, and that led to villages, then cities, kings and writing. It also brought new problems, such as crowded living and disease.

# The Great Pyramid
@src Lehner, 1997

The Great Pyramid of Giza was built around 2560 BC as the tomb of the Egyptian king Khufu. It stood about 146 meters high, and it was the tallest human-made structure in the world for nearly 4,000 years, until the spire of Lincoln Cathedral in England surpassed it in the 1300s.

The workers weren't slaves, as people used to think. Archaeologists found their town nearby, with bakeries and graves, which shows they were paid and fed. They were organized in gangs with humorous names, such as "Friends of Khufu." It's an impressive engineering feat, done with copper tools, ramps and teamwork. Each of the roughly two million blocks had to be cut, moved and set in place, and the pyramid is still the only one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World that survives.

# The Code of Hammurabi
@src Roth, 1995

Around 1754 BC the king of Babylon, Hammurabi, had 282 laws carved on a tall black stone pillar, so that everyone could see them. It covered trade, property, family and crime. Punishments depended on the social rank of the victim and the offender, and many were based on "an eye for an eye."

It's one of the oldest long written law codes, and it reflects the idea that the king's role is to ensure justice. At the top of the pillar, the king is shown standing before the sun god, who is handing him the symbols of authority. The code was copied by scribes for more than a thousand years. Its value for us is that it shows what people worried about in a very ancient city, from lost cattle to rental disputes and bad builders.

# The Rosetta Stone
@src Champollion, 1822

For 1,400 years, nobody could read Egyptian hieroglyphs. The knowledge of how had been lost, and the pictures on temple walls were a mystery. In 1799 French soldiers digging near the town of Rashid, or Rosetta, found a stone slab carved with the same decree in three scripts: hieroglyphs, a cursive Egyptian script and ancient Greek. It dates from 196 BC.

Using the Greek as a key, the French scholar Jean-François Champollion announced in 1822 that he could read hieroglyphs. His breakthrough was to realize that the signs could stand for sounds, as well as for things. It opened up three thousand years of Egyptian history, from temples and tombs to letters and stories. The stone is now in the British Museum, and it has become a symbol of the effort of decoding any unknown language.

# The first Olympic Games
@src Standard classical history

By tradition, the first Olympic Games were held in 776 BC at Olympia in Greece, in honor of Zeus. They took place every four years, and included foot races, wrestling, boxing and chariot races. A sacred truce was declared so that athletes could travel safely, even when cities were at war.

Only free Greek men were allowed to compete, and the Games continued for more than a thousand years. Winners received a wreath of olive leaves and great fame at home, though no medals. The early athletes competed without clothes. The Games were ended in the late fourth century AD, and they were revived in Athens in 1896. The modern Games are much larger, but they still follow the same four-year rhythm.

# Athenian democracy
@src Aristotle, Constitution of the Athenians

In 508 BC the Athenian leader Cleisthenes reformed the government, giving citizens a vote in an assembly that made the laws. Any citizen could speak and vote. Officials were often chosen by lot, so that rich and poor had an equal chance.

But citizens were only free adult men born in Athens, perhaps a fifth of the population. Women, slaves and foreigners were left out. It was a limited democracy, but it introduced the idea that ordinary people should decide. The word itself comes from the Greek for "rule by the people." Later democracies have been far wider, and they've grown from that early seed, along with a lot of arguments about who should be counted.

# Marathon
@src Herodotus, Histories
@conf mixed

In 490 BC the Athenians defeated a much larger Persian army at the plain of Marathon, one of the most famous battles in history. The victory saved Athens, and gave its citizens confidence in the young democracy.

The popular story that a runner raced 26 miles to Athens with the news, shouting "Victory!" and then dropped dead, is a much later legend. What's better documented is that a runner named Pheidippides ran about 150 miles to Sparta to ask for help. The modern marathon race comes from the legend, and its length, 26.2 miles, was fixed at the 1908 London Olympics. It's a nice case of a story that's better known than the facts behind it.

# Alexander the Great
@src Arrian, Anabasis

Alexander became king of Macedon at the age of 20, in 336 BC, after his father was murdered. In just over a decade he conquered the Persian Empire, defeating King Darius, and led his army as far as the Indus River in India by 326 BC. His soldiers refused to go further.

He died in Babylon in 323 BC, at 32, and his generals divided up the empire. It spread Greek culture across much of Asia, and cities he founded, such as Alexandria in Egypt, became centers of learning. His tutor in boyhood was Aristotle. Alexander was a brilliant soldier, and often a cruel conqueror. Within a short life, he changed the map of the ancient world, and his legend lasted for many centuries.

# The First Emperor of China
@src Sima Qian, Records of the Grand Historian

In 221 BC King Zheng of Qin finished conquering the other states of China and declared himself Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor. He standardized writing, weights and measures, and even the width of cart axles, so that wagons could run in the same ruts across the empire.

His tomb is guarded by thousands of life-size clay soldiers, the Terracotta Army, which was discovered in 1974 by farmers digging a well. Each figure has a different face. The emperor was also a harsh ruler, who burned books and punished critics. His dynasty collapsed within a few years of his death, but the idea of a single united China lasted. The name China itself may come from his state of Qin.

# The Great Wall and space
@src Standard reference

One of the most popular facts is that the Great Wall of China is visible from the Moon, and it's not true. Astronauts say it's barely visible, if at all, even from low Earth orbit, because it's narrow and is similar in color to the land around it.

The claim appeared in print in the 1930s, decades before anyone went into space. China's first astronaut, Yang Liwei, said in 2003 that he could not see the wall from orbit. Cities, airports and large highways are much easier to see, especially at night. It's a good example of a widely repeated claim that turns out to be false, and of why it's good to check an assertion even when it sounds reasonable.

# From Republic to Empire: Rome and the Ides of March
@src Livy, Polybius; Plutarch, Suetonius

According to tradition, the Romans overthrew their last king in 509 BC and set up a republic, in which leaders were elected. Two consuls shared power for a year, and the Senate advised them. The Republic lasted nearly 500 years, as Rome grew from a city into the ruler of the Mediterranean.

On March 15, 44 BC, the Ides of March in the Roman calendar, Julius Caesar was stabbed to death in the Senate by a group of about 60 senators. They hoped to save the Republic from a man who'd had himself made dictator for life. But the killing led to another civil war, and within 17 years to the rule of Caesar's adopted heir, Augustus, as the first emperor, in 27 BC. The plotters' attempt to restore the Republic ended it.

# The Pax Romana
@src Standard Roman history

The "Roman Peace" was a period of about 200 years, from Augustus in 27 BC to the death of Marcus Aurelius in AD 180, when the Roman Empire was relatively stable. Trade and travel flourished, with good roads and shipping across the Mediterranean.

The peace was kept by large armies on the borders, and the people conquered by Rome didn't always feel peaceful. But it was a time of prosperity, in which a traveler could go from Britain to Syria under the same laws and the same coins. Cities grew, aqueducts and baths were built, and Latin and Greek spread. The peace didn't mean that there were no wars. It means that, for most people inside the empire, war was far away.

# The Silk Road
@src Sima Qian

The Silk Road wasn't one road but a network of trade routes linking China, India, Persia and Rome. The Han emperor sent an envoy, Zhang Qian, west around 138 BC to find allies, and that opened up the routes.

Silk, paper, spices and ideas traveled along them, as did religions. So did diseases. Most traders only carried goods part of the way, passing them to others, so a piece of silk might change hands dozens of times before it reached Rome. The name "Silk Road" was coined in 1877 by a German geographer, Ferdinand von Richthofen. It's a useful picture of how connected the ancient world already was, and of how much we owe to the traders who crossed the deserts and mountains.

# The Library of Alexandria
@src Bagnall, 2002
@conf mixed

The popular story is that the great Library of Alexandria in Egypt was destroyed in a single great fire. In fact, it declined over centuries. Funding was cut, scholars were driven out in political purges, and a number of fires damaged parts of it, including one set by Julius Caesar's troops in 48 BC that burned some storage warehouses.

Historians reject the idea of one dramatic loss. The library was founded in the 3rd century BC, and at its peak it may have held several hundred thousand scrolls. It was part of the Museum, a kind of research institute where scholars lived and worked. Its slow fading is a less dramatic story than the legend, but a sadder one, since it shows how a great institution can be lost through neglect.

# Paper
@src Tsien, 1985

The Chinese had paper by the 2nd century AD, and it was a thousand years before Europe took it up. The court official Cai Lun is traditionally credited with inventing it around AD 105, but archaeologists have found older fragments.

Arab papermakers learned the craft after 751, at the Battle of Talas, according to tradition, and it reached Europe in the 1100s, through Spain. Paper made printing and widespread literacy affordable. Before then, people wrote on parchment, which was made from animal skins, or on papyrus, which was costly and fragile. A cheap, light and smooth material for writing changed what was possible, from letters and books to government records and, eventually, newspapers.

# Vesuvius
@src Pliny the Younger, Letters

In AD 79 Mount Vesuvius erupted and buried the Roman towns of Pompeii and Herculaneum. A young man named Pliny the Younger watched from across the bay and described the towering cloud in two letters to the historian Tacitus. His uncle, Pliny the Elder, sailed toward the eruption to rescue people and died on the shore.

The ash preserved homes, shops, loaves of bread, paintings and graffiti so well that we can read about daily life in a Roman town. One wall has a scrawled complaint about a bad innkeeper, and another has an election slogan. Excavations that began in the 1700s are still going on, and new discoveries come up every year. It's a time capsule, made by a disaster.

# The Council of Nicaea
@src Eusebius; Nicene Creed

In AD 325 the Roman emperor Constantine called bishops from across the empire to Nicaea, in what's now Turkey, to settle a dispute over the nature of Christ: whether the Son was created or eternal. The disagreement threatened the unity of the church, and so of the empire.

The council produced an early form of the Nicene Creed, which affirms that the Son is of one substance with the Father. A revised version is still recited in many churches today. It was the first time that the bishops of the whole church had met in an assembly of this kind. It also set the date for Easter to be reckoned in a common way. The council shows how closely the story of the early church was tied to the Roman state.

# The fall of Western Rome
@src Standard Roman history

In 476 the Germanic general Odoacer removed the teenage emperor Romulus Augustulus, and didn't bother to replace him. That date is traditionally given as the end of the Western Roman Empire.

Historians now see it as a slow change over about a century, not a sudden collapse, as barbarian kingdoms took over provinces. Most people probably didn't notice that anything special had happened. The Eastern Roman Empire, centered on Constantinople, continued until 1453. The Roman Empire's decline has been the subject of debate ever since. Some blame economics, others politics or invasion, and many today speak of transformation rather than fall. Whatever the cause, the Latin language, Roman law and Christianity carried on, and they shaped the kingdoms that followed.

# Hagia Sophia
@src Procopius

The great church of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople, now Istanbul, was built in only five years, from 532 to 537, for the emperor Justinian. Its enormous dome, about 55 meters high, seems to float on a ring of windows.

For almost a thousand years it was one of the largest enclosed spaces in the world. When Justinian first walked in, he is said to have exclaimed that he had outdone Solomon, who built the first temple. It was later a mosque, then a museum, and in 2020 became a mosque again. The building shows how much skill the engineers of the sixth century had, and it has survived earthquakes, fires and many changes of rule.

# The Hijra
@src Standard Islamic history

The Islamic calendar counts years from AD 622, the year of the Hijra, when the Prophet Muhammad and his followers moved from Mecca to Medina after persecution. The word means "migration."

The calendar is lunar, with twelve months of 29 or 30 days, so the year is about 11 days shorter than the solar year. This means that the Islamic months, like Ramadan, move through the seasons, falling about 11 days earlier each year. Over about 33 years, the month goes all the way around. It's a different way of keeping time, tied to the Moon and not to the Sun, and it's still used to set the dates of Islamic holidays.

# The House of Wisdom
@src Gutas, 1998

In ninth-century Baghdad, the caliphs supported a center of scholarship known as the House of Wisdom. Scholars there translated Greek, Persian and Indian works into Arabic, and added their own discoveries in mathematics, astronomy and medicine. Al-Khwarizmi, who gave us the word algebra, worked there.

Many ancient texts, including some by Aristotle and Galen, survived because they were translated. Some were later translated back from Arabic into Latin, and so they came to Europe. It was part of a wider movement of translation that lasted for around two centuries. The House of Wisdom was destroyed when the Mongols sacked Baghdad in 1258. It stands as a reminder that knowledge is passed down by people, and that it can be lost.

# Charlemagne
@src Einhard, Life of Charlemagne

On Christmas Day in the year 800, Pope Leo III crowned the Frankish king Charlemagne as Emperor in Rome. He ruled much of western Europe, and promoted education, setting up schools in monasteries.

His scribes also developed a clear and legible handwriting style, Carolingian minuscule, which later became the model for our lowercase letters. The letters you're reading now descend from the script of Charlemagne's court. He himself, according to his biographer, could read but had trouble learning to write, and kept tablets under his pillow to practice. He's often called the father of Europe, and his empire was split among his grandsons within a generation after his death.

# The Vikings in America
@src Kuitems et al., Nature, 2021

About 500 years before Columbus, Norse explorers reached North America. At L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, archaeologists found the remains of buildings, and wood that had been cut with metal tools, which the locals didn't have.

In 2021 scientists used a solar storm recorded in tree rings to date the cutting precisely, to the year 1021. It's the earliest known European presence in the Americas. The Norse sagas tell of voyages to a land called Vinland, and this site is the only confirmed evidence of them. The settlement did not last long, perhaps because of conflict with the local people or the distance from home. The find shows how a single year can be read from the rings of a tree.

# The Norman Conquest
@src Bayeux Tapestry; Anglo-Saxon Chronicle

On October 14, 1066, William, Duke of Normandy, defeated the English King Harold at the Battle of Hastings. Harold was killed, perhaps by an arrow in the eye, as the Bayeux Tapestry seems to show, though that's debated.

French became the language of England's rulers for around 300 years, and English absorbed thousands of French words, which is why we have both "cow" and "beef." The English peasants who raised the animals used the old English word, while the Norman lords who ate the meat used the French one. The Tapestry itself is a 70-meter strip of embroidered cloth. The Conquest changed English law, language and landholding more than any other event in the country's history.

# Magna Carta
@src Holt, 1992

In 1215 a group of English barons forced King John to put his seal to a charter limiting his power. It promised, among other things, that no free man could be punished except by the lawful judgment of his peers or the law of the land.

Most clauses later became obsolete. The Pope soon annulled the charter, and the barons and the king went to war. But the idea that even a king is under the law influenced many later constitutions, including the American one. It was reissued several times by later kings, and a few of its clauses are still part of English law. The document itself is a collection of very specific grievances, but its spirit has been taken as a promise of liberty.

# Genghis Khan
@src Weatherford, 2004

Temüjin united the Mongol tribes and in 1206 took the title Genghis Khan, "universal ruler." He and his successors built the largest connected land empire in history. Under his grandsons, it stretched from Korea to Hungary.

It also protected trade along the Silk Road and spread ideas and technologies, though it was founded on terrible violence, and may have helped to spread the plague. Cities that resisted were often destroyed, and millions died. At the same time, the Mongols allowed religious freedom and promoted trade, and they set up a postal system that was among the fastest of its time. He's a figure of both great achievement and great destruction.

# Mansa Musa
@src Ibn Khaldun; al-Umari

Mansa Musa ruled the West African empire of Mali in the early 1300s, and was famously wealthy. In 1324 he traveled to Mecca with thousands of attendants and camels laden with gold.

He gave away so much in Cairo that the price of gold there fell for years. Back in Mali, the city of Timbuktu became a renowned center of learning, with libraries and a university. Mali was one of the main sources of gold for the Mediterranean world, and the journey put it on European maps. A map from 1375 shows him seated on a throne, holding a gold nugget. He may well have been one of the richest people who ever lived.

# The Black Death
@src Benedictow, 2004

The plague that swept through Europe between 1347 and 1351 killed perhaps a third or more of the population. It came along trade routes from Asia, spread by fleas on rats, and by people. Whole villages were emptied.

With so many dead, workers were scarce, wages rose and the old feudal order, in which peasants were tied to the land, weakened. It took Europe about two centuries to recover its population. The disease returned in waves for centuries. It's one of the worst catastrophes in human history, and it shows how a single microbe can change the course of civilization. It also led to changes in medicine and in attitudes toward the poor.

# The printing press
@src Eisenstein, 1979

Around 1450 the German goldsmith Johannes Gutenberg developed a way to print with movable metal type. Before that, books had to be copied by hand, and a Bible might take a scribe months.

His famous Bible appeared around 1455. By 1500, more than 20 million books had been printed in Europe. Books became cheap, and ideas, from science to religion, could spread quickly. The printing press helped bring about the Reformation, the Scientific Revolution and the rise of literacy. Gutenberg himself didn't get rich from it, and he lost his workshop to a creditor. It's among the most influential inventions in history, and the first great step toward the age of mass communication.

# The fall of Constantinople
@src Runciman, 1965

On May 29, 1453, the Ottoman Sultan Mehmed II captured Constantinople, the capital of the Byzantine Empire, after a siege of 53 days. His army used huge cannons to batter the ancient walls.

The city became the Ottoman capital, Istanbul. Greek scholars who fled west carried manuscripts with them, which helped to feed the Italian Renaissance. The fall was the end of the Roman Empire, which had lasted, in one form or another, for about 1,500 years. For Europeans, the closing of old trade routes increased the push to find sea routes to Asia, which led to the voyages of exploration that followed. A single siege thus helped to set the Age of Discovery in motion.

# Columbus and the Columbian exchange
@src Crosby, 1972; Standard history

In 1492 the Italian navigator Christopher Columbus, sailing for Spain, crossed the Atlantic and landed in the Caribbean. He believed he'd reached the edge of Asia. Millions of people already lived in the Americas, and his arrival began lasting contact between the hemispheres.

After that, plants, animals and diseases crossed the Atlantic in both directions, and changed both sides of the ocean. Potatoes, tomatoes, maize and chocolate went to the Old World, and horses, wheat and smallpox went to the New. The historian Alfred Crosby named the process in 1972. Many of the foods that we think of as typical of European countries, like Italian tomatoes, came from the Americas. The exchange also brought disease, conquest and enslavement for many Indigenous peoples.

# Magellan's voyage
@src Pigafetta, journal

In 1519 five ships under Ferdinand Magellan left Spain to find a westward route to the Spice Islands. The voyage was long and harsh, with mutiny, storms and starvation. Magellan was killed in a battle in the Philippines in 1521.

Only one ship, the Victoria, under Juan Sebastián Elcano, completed the journey around the world in 1522, with 18 men of the original crew of about 270. It proved that the world could be circumnavigated. When they arrived, they found that their calendar was a day behind, since they had traveled westward against the Sun. The voyage was a triumph, but it was also very costly in lives.

# The fall of the Aztecs
@src Restall, Seven Myths of the Spanish Conquest (2003)

In 1521 Hernán Cortés and his forces captured Tenochtitlan, the Aztec capital, now Mexico City. The Spaniards didn't do it alone. Thousands of Indigenous allies, who resented Aztec rule, fought with them, and smallpox, which the Europeans had brought, killed a huge part of the city's people.

The conquest had many causes, not just Spanish weapons and horses. Cortés also had help from an Indigenous woman, known as Malintzin or La Malinche, who translated and advised him. The city was larger than any in Spain at the time, with canals, markets and a great temple. The old story that a small band of Spaniards conquered an empire is now seen as much too simple.

# Luther's 95 Theses
@src Luther, 1517

In 1517 Martin Luther, a German monk and professor, wrote a list of 95 arguments against the sale of indulgences, which were pardons that people bought to reduce punishment for sin. According to tradition, he posted them on the church door at Wittenberg.

The printing press carried his ideas across Germany within weeks. The Reformation that followed split Western Christianity, and Luther later translated the Bible into German. His translation helped to shape the German language. Whether he nailed the paper to the door or simply mailed it to his bishop is debated, but the effect was the same. It started a movement that changed religion, politics and education across Europe.

# The King James Bible
@src Daniell, The Bible in English (2003)

The King James Version of the Bible was completed in 1611 by about 47 scholars, working from Hebrew and Greek texts. It built heavily on the earlier English translation by William Tyndale, who was executed in 1536.

Its rhythmic language shaped English literature and speech, giving us phrases like "the powers that be" and "the skin of my teeth." It was meant to be read aloud in church, so its sentences are made to be heard. For almost three centuries, it was the standard Bible for English speakers. Many people who've never read it use its phrases every day, and its language is still part of what we think of as grand and solemn English.

# Machu Picchu
@src Burger & Salazar, 2004

The Inca citadel of Machu Picchu, high in the Andes of Peru, was probably built around 1450 as an estate for the emperor Pachacuti. It was abandoned about a century later, around the time of the Spanish conquest, and the Spaniards apparently never found it.

In 1911 the American explorer Hiram Bingham brought it to world attention, although local farmers had known about it all along. The stonework is so precise that blades of paper can't be slipped between some of the blocks, and the Incas built without mortar. It sits at about 2,400 meters, on a ridge between two peaks. Today it's one of the most visited sites in South America, and it has become a symbol of Inca engineering.
