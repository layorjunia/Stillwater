# Coral and the algae within
@src Hoegh-Guldberg et al., Science, 2007; Hughes et al., Nature, 2017

A coral reef looks like a rock garden, but it's alive. Each coral is a colony of tiny animals, called polyps, which build a skeleton of limestone. Inside the tissue of the polyps live microscopic algae, called zooxanthellae. The algae use sunlight to make sugars, and pass most of them to the coral, which gives them shelter and nutrients in return. The algae give corals their colors.

When the water becomes too warm, the algae are expelled, and the coral turns white. It's called bleaching. A bleached coral isn't dead, but it's starving, and if the heat lasts, it dies. Mass bleaching on the Great Barrier Reef has occurred several times, in 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017 and later. Reefs cover a small part of the ocean floor, but support a large share of marine species. How much they can adapt to warmer seas is a critical question.

# Who pollinates our food?
@src Klein et al., Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 2007

Many of the foods that we eat depend on animals that carry pollen between flowers. In 2007 Alexandra-Maria Klein and her colleagues reviewed data on the crops of the world. Of the leading food crops, about three quarters depend on animal pollinators, at least in part. They include fruits, vegetables, nuts and seeds, such as apples, almonds and coffee.

However, by volume, most of the world's calories come from crops such as wheat, rice and corn, which are wind-pollinated or self-pollinated. The pollinated crops account for about a third of production. They are crucial for nutrition, since they provide many of the vitamins and minerals in our diets. Bees, flies, butterflies and bats all play a part. Declines in wild bees and other insects are a concern for food security.

# A vault for the world's seeds
@src Svalbard Global Seed Vault; Crop Trust, 2008

In the mountainside on the Norwegian island of Spitsbergen, about six hundred miles from the North Pole, there's a tunnel that leads to a vault. Opened in 2008, the Svalbard Global Seed Vault is a safety deposit box for the world's crops. Seed banks from many countries send duplicates of their collections, and the vault stores them at a temperature of about minus eighteen degrees Celsius.

It now holds more than a million samples, representing thousands of varieties of food plants. If a collection is lost in a war, a natural disaster or a failure of equipment, seeds can be taken back. This happened in 2015, when a seed bank in Aleppo, Syria, was unable to use its collection because of the war, and it asked for its seeds to be returned. The permafrost helps to keep the vault cold, even without power.

# A hundred and two toads
@src Shine, Quarterly Review of Biology, 2010

In 1935 the Australian government brought a hundred and two cane toads from Hawaii to Queensland. The aim was to control beetles that were damaging sugar cane. The toads did not eat the beetles. They did, however, thrive, and breed. They are poisonous, and they have no natural predators in Australia, so they have spread across the north of the country.

The toad population is now in the many millions, and it has moved west into the Northern Territory and Western Australia. Native animals that eat the toads, such as quolls, goannas and snakes, have been poisoned, and some have declined. The case is a classic example of an invasive species, and of how introducing an animal to control a pest can make things worse. Scientists are now working on methods to protect native animals, such as training them to avoid the toads.

# Twenty-four rabbits
@src Fenner & Fantini, Biological Control of Vertebrate Pests, 1999

In 1859 a wealthy landowner in Victoria, Australia, named Thomas Austin, wrote to a relative in England and asked for some rabbits to be sent over, so that he could hunt them for sport. The twenty-four rabbits he received bred rapidly. There was plenty of food, and no natural enemies. Within a few decades, rabbits had spread across much of the continent, in one of the fastest spreads of a mammal ever recorded.

They ate vegetation, caused erosion, and competed with native animals and with sheep. Fences, poisons and shooting made little difference. In 1950 the authorities released a virus, myxomatosis, which killed a huge number of rabbits. But the survivors became resistant, and the population recovered. Rabbit numbers are still a problem. It's often given as a lesson in how fragile an ecosystem can be.

# Martha, the last passenger pigeon
@src Greenberg, A Feathered River Across the Sky, 2014

The passenger pigeon was once the most numerous bird in North America, and perhaps in the world. Flocks of millions darkened the sky for hours or days. Estimates of the population run to several billion. The naturalist John James Audubon described a flock in 1813 that took three days to pass overhead.

The birds were hunted on an industrial scale for food, shipped by railroad to the cities, and their forest habitat was cleared. By the 1890s, they were almost gone from the wild. The last one, a female called Martha, died in the Cincinnati Zoo on September 1, 1914. Her body was frozen in a block of ice, and sent to the Smithsonian. The loss of a species of such numbers within a few decades showed that abundance is no protection.

# To the bottom of the sea
@src Piccard & Walsh, 1960; National Geographic, 2012

The deepest known place in the ocean is the Challenger Deep, in the Mariana Trench in the western Pacific. It is about eleven kilometers down, nearly seven miles. It's deeper than Mount Everest is high, and at the bottom, the pressure is more than a thousand times that at the surface. In 1960 Jacques Piccard and Lieutenant Don Walsh of the United States Navy reached it in a submersible called the Trieste. They spent about twenty minutes there.

They saw a flat fish, which they took to be a sole, but it's now doubted that fish could live at that depth. In 2012 the film director James Cameron made the first solo descent, in a submersible that he helped to design. Since then, other crewed and unmanned vehicles have visited. Animals live even there, among them amphipods and sea cucumbers. And sadly, plastic waste has been found at the bottom.

# Life without sunlight
@src Corliss et al., Science, 1979; Van Dover, The Ecology of Deep-Sea Hydrothermal Vents, 2000

In 1977 scientists diving in the submersible Alvin, near the Galápagos Islands, found something that no one had expected. On the ocean floor, over two kilometers deep, hot water was gushing out of cracks in the rock, forming chimneys. Around them, in the dark and cold, there was a thriving community of animals, including giant tube worms, clams and crabs.

It was thought that all life depended on sunlight, which supports the plants at the base of the food chain. But here, bacteria were making food from chemicals in the hot water, such as hydrogen sulfide, in a process called chemosynthesis. The tube worms, which have no mouth or gut, housed the bacteria in their bodies. The discovery changed our ideas about where life can live, and suggested that it might exist on other worlds, such as the oceans of icy moons.

# A sea of light
@src Martini & Haddock, Scientific Reports, 2017

Most of the ocean is dark, and many creatures there make their own light. In 2017 Séverine Martini and Steven Haddock studied the animals in the water column off Monterey Bay in California. They went down in robotic submersibles, and counted the animals that they saw, looking for those that were known to glow.

They found that about three quarters of the animals that they saw, from the surface to a depth of about four kilometers, could produce light. This is called bioluminescence. The animals use it for many things, including attracting prey, evading predators, and finding mates. Jellyfish, squid, shrimp and fish all do it. It's the most common form of communication on Earth, by the volume of living space that it covers.

# The Lisbon earthquake
@src Shrady, The Last Day, 2008

On the morning of November 1, 1755, which was All Saints' Day, an earthquake struck Lisbon, the capital of Portugal. The churches were full. The quake was followed by fires, and then, about forty minutes later, by a tsunami that rushed into the harbor. Tens of thousands of people died, estimates run from thirty thousand to fifty thousand, and most of the city was destroyed.

The disaster shocked Europe, and it caused a crisis of thought. If the world was governed by a benevolent God, how could such a thing happen, and on a holy day? Voltaire wrote a poem on the disaster, and made it a theme in his novel Candide. The philosopher Immanuel Kant wrote about natural causes of earthquakes. Some historians see it as a turning point in the Enlightenment, and in the beginning of seismology.

# The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami
@src United Nations, 2005; Telford & Cosgrave, Disasters, 2007

On December 26, 2004, a huge earthquake occurred under the sea off the coast of Sumatra, Indonesia. It had a magnitude of about 9.1, one of the strongest ever recorded. It displaced a large section of the seafloor, and generated a series of giant waves that crossed the Indian Ocean, reaching coasts as far away as East Africa.

About two hundred and thirty thousand people died, in fourteen countries, and many more lost their homes. Most of the dead were in Indonesia. There was no warning system in the Indian Ocean, as there was in the Pacific, and many people did not know to run away from the sea. Afterward, an international tsunami warning system was set up for the region. A warning that gives people even minutes can save many lives.

# Lucy
@src Johanson & Edey, Lucy: The Beginnings of Humankind, 1981

On November 24, 1974, the American paleoanthropologist Donald Johanson and a student, Tom Gray, were looking for fossils in the Afar region of Ethiopia. They spotted a piece of an arm bone, and then other bones nearby. Over the next days, the team found about forty percent of a skeleton. It was a small, adult female. She was about three feet seven inches tall.

The fossil was dated to about 3.2 million years ago. It was named Lucy, after the Beatles song Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds, which was playing in the camp. She belonged to a species, Australopithecus afarensis. Her pelvis and leg bones showed that she walked upright, while her skull was small, with a brain not much bigger than a chimpanzee's. She showed that our ancestors walked upright before their brains grew large.
