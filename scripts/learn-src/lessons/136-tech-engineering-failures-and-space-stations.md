# The warship that sank before it left the harbor
@series history
@src Vasa, 1628; Vasa Museum, Stockholm

The Vasa was a Swedish warship, built between 1626 and 1628 on the orders of King Gustavus Adolphus. She was richly decorated, and meant to be a symbol of the king's ambitions for Sweden. When she was finished, she was one of the most powerfully armed ships in the world, with bronze cannons that had been cast specially for her.

On August 10, 1628, she set out on her first voyage from Stockholm. She had sailed only about 1,300 meters, a little over three quarters of a mile, when a gust of wind heeled her over, and she sank in a few minutes. The reason was that she was dangerously unstable. She had too much weight in the upper part of the hull. Accounts say that a test of her stability, in which a group of men ran from side to side, was stopped because the ship rocked so much, but she was sent to sea all the same.

She lay on the bottom for 333 years, until she was found in the late 1950s in a busy shipping lane in Stockholm's harbor, and was raised in 1961 with her hull mostly intact. She is now in a museum, and had been visited by over 45 million people by the beginning of 2025. A ship that failed in minutes has become one of the best preserved of the early modern age.

# The bridge that was not built for the wind
@series history
@src Tay Bridge disaster, December 28, 1879; Thomas Bouch

On the evening of Sunday, December 28, 1879, during a violent windstorm, the Tay Rail Bridge in Scotland collapsed as a passenger train from Edinburgh was crossing it to Dundee. All on board were killed. The number is uncertain, and is usually put at between 59 and 75.

The bridge, designed by Sir Thomas Bouch, was made of lattice girders on iron piers, with cast iron columns and wrought iron cross-bracing. The piers were narrower, and the bracing less extensive and strong, than in earlier designs of his. For a planned bridge over the Firth of Forth, Bouch had asked experts about wind pressure. On their advice, he made no explicit allowance for the force of wind on the Tay Bridge.

An inquiry found other faults, in the detailed design, in maintenance, and in the quality control of the castings, and some of these were at least partly his responsibility. Bouch died less than a year later, with his reputation ruined, and his design for the Forth Bridge was dropped. British bridge builders afterward had to allow for wind loads of up to 56 pounds per square foot. It is still the fifth deadliest railway accident in the history of the United Kingdom.

# The bridge that made people march in step
@series tech
@src London Millennium Footbridge, opened June 10, 2000; Arup

The Millennium Bridge in London is a steel suspension bridge for pedestrians, across the Thames, between Bankside and the City, in view of St. Paul's Cathedral. It was designed by the engineering firm Arup, with an architect and a sculptor. It opened on June 10, 2000, with thousands of people crossing it.

Almost at once it began to sway from side to side, in a way that alarmed those on it. Londoners nicknamed it the Wobbly Bridge. It was closed later that day, and after two days of limited access, it was closed again for almost two years, while it was altered.

The cause was a feedback effect. When the bridge moved slightly, people on it began to adjust their walking, without knowing it, to keep their balance, and so they stepped in time with the sway, which pushed it more. The engineers fixed it by adding dampers that absorb the movement. It reopened in February 2002, after tests in which 700 staff from Arup crossed it. The lesson was that a structure must be designed for the way people behave, and not only for the weight they carry.

# The fire in the capsule on the launch pad
@series history
@src Apollo 1, January 27, 1967; NASA Accident Review Board

Apollo 1 was to be the first crewed flight of the Apollo program, the American effort to land a man on the Moon. It was scheduled for February 21, 1967. On January 27, during a rehearsal on the launch pad at Cape Kennedy, a fire broke out in the cabin. It killed all three crew members, Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee, and destroyed the command module.

The review board found that the fire was started by an electrical spark. It spread very quickly because the cabin was filled with pure oxygen, at a pressure above that of the air outside, and because of materials in the cabin, such as nylon, that burned easily. The crew could not get out. The hatch was a plug door that opened inward, and it could not be opened against the pressure inside.

The test had not been regarded as dangerous, because the rocket had no fuel in it, and so emergency preparations were poor. During the congressional inquiry, Senator Walter Mondale revealed an internal NASA document that listed problems with the main contractor, North American Aviation. The name Apollo 1 was made official in honor of the crew. The program redesigned the capsule, with a hatch that opened outward and a safer atmosphere, and went on to reach the Moon in 1969.

# The dams that were built for a flood that came anyway
@series history
@src Banqiao Dam failure, August 1975; Typhoon Nina

In August 1975, after Typhoon Nina made landfall in China, the Banqiao Dam and 61 others in Henan province collapsed. The flood that followed was one of the deadliest in the history of China, and the third deadliest in the world. It affected 12,000 square kilometers and some 10 million people. Estimates of the number of dead range widely, from 26,000 to 240,000, and millions of houses were destroyed.

The Banqiao Dam had been designed for a once-in-a-thousand-years rainfall of 300 millimeters in a day. But, according to accounts, more than a normal year's rain fell in a single day near the center of the storm. Many of the dams had been built with Soviet help, during the Great Leap Forward, and the quality was poor. They had been designed mainly to store water, and not to prevent floods.

Some experts also point to the effects of policies in that period on forests and the land, and to the way that officials handled the crisis. The event took place during the Cultural Revolution, when news was controlled, and the full scale of the disaster was not made public for years. It is a lesson in how a design that assumes a certain limit can fail when nature exceeds it, and how the fault can lie in organization as well as engineering.

# The airliner that landed on a racetrack
@series history
@src Air Canada Flight 143, July 23, 1983

On July 23, 1983, an Air Canada Boeing 767, on a flight from Montreal to Edmonton, ran out of fuel over Manitoba, at 41,000 feet. Its fuel gauges were not working, and the tanks had been filled with too little fuel, because of a mistake in a calculation. Both engines stopped.

The cause was a chain of events. A fuel-quantity sensor had failed, and the only replacement available was also faulty. The maintenance crew misunderstood the problem and switched off the backup system, which meant that the fuel had to be measured by hand with a dipstick. The aircraft's computer needed the fuel in kilograms. In the conversion from volume to weight, the crew used a factor for pounds, which gives a number more than twice as large, and the plane took on less than half of the fuel that it needed.

The pilots glided the powerless airliner to an emergency landing at a former Royal Canadian Air Force base at Gimli, Manitoba, which had become a racetrack and was in use that day. No one was seriously hurt. The aircraft was repaired, and flew until 2008. It became known as the Gimli Glider. The accident is a classic case of how a mix-up of units, joined to a chain of small errors, can lead to a crisis.

# The space station that was a rocket stage
@series tech
@src Skylab, 1973 to 1979; Apollo Applications Program

Skylab was the first space station of the United States, launched by NASA on May 14, 1973. It was occupied for about 24 weeks, between May 1973 and February 1974, by three crews of three astronauts. It was built from the empty third stage of a Saturn V rocket, converted into a workshop, and it carried a solar observatory, Earth observation equipment and hundreds of experiments. The astronauts made ten spacewalks.

It was damaged at launch. A heat and meteoroid shield tore away, which also took off one of its solar panels, and the station overheated. The first crew arrived and repaired it by putting up a sunshade, and freeing a jammed panel. The living and working space inside the station was over 350 cubic meters, a volume that no other station beat until the Soviet Mir was complete, in 1996.

A plan to boost Skylab into a higher orbit with the Space Shuttle was abandoned when it was clear that the shuttle would not be ready by 1979. The station's orbit decayed, and on July 11, 1979, it broke up in the atmosphere, scattering debris across the Indian Ocean and Western Australia. It was the world's second space station, after the Soviet Salyut 1.

# The station that stayed up for fifteen years
@series tech
@src Mir, 1986 to 2001; Valeri Polyakov

Mir, whose name means both peace and world in Russian, was a space station that circled the Earth from 1986 to 2001. It was operated first by the Soviet Union and later by Russia. It was the first modular space station, put together in orbit from 1986 to 1996, from pieces that were launched one at a time. It was the largest artificial satellite in orbit until the International Space Station came along.

People lived on it for 3,644 days in a row, which was the record for continuous human presence in space until the space station that followed it surpassed that number in 2010. The cosmonaut Valeri Polyakov holds the record for the longest single spaceflight. He spent 437 days on board, from 1994 to 1995. In its fifteen years it was occupied for twelve and a half, and 105 cosmonauts and astronauts from 12 countries visited. They carried out 80 spacewalks.

The crews did experiments in biology, human health, physics, astronomy and meteorology, and tested the technology needed for living in space for a long time. Mir had its troubles, including a fire and a collision with a supply ship in 1997. It was brought down in a controlled way into the South Pacific in 2001. Much of what was learned there was used in planning the next space station.
