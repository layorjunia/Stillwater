# The woman who cannot feel fear, except in one way
@src Feinstein et al., Nature Neuroscience, 2013; Urbach and Wiethe, 1929

Urbach-Wiethe disease is a very rare inherited disorder, with about 400 reported cases. It was first reported in 1929 by Erich Urbach and Camillo Wiethe. It mainly affects the skin and the voice, which becomes hoarse. In some patients, the disease also hardens tissue deep in both temporal lobes of the brain, where it destroys the amygdala, a small structure that is important in processing fear.

A patient known as S.M. has this damage on both sides. Reports say that she shows little fear in everyday life. She will pick up a snake, or watch a frightening scene in a film like The Shining, without being affected. For years, she became a prime case for the idea that the amygdala is the brain's fear center.

In 2013 Justin Feinstein and his colleagues tested her with a different threat. She and two other patients with the same condition breathed air with 35 percent carbon dioxide, which gives a sense of suffocation. They felt significantly more fear and panic than 12 healthy people, of whom only three panicked. The authors suggested that the amygdala may normally hold panic back, and that fear from inside the body runs through other regions, such as the brainstem and the insula.

# The four colors that most of us cannot see
@series sci
@conf mixed
@src Tetrachromacy; vertebrate color vision

Most people have three kinds of cone cells in their eyes, which respond to red, green and blue light, and the brain mixes the three signals into all of the colors that we see. A tetrachromat has four kinds. That would give a color space with four dimensions, and such a person might tell apart colors that look identical to the rest of us.

Many animals are tetrachromats, among them birds, fish and reptiles. The common ancestor of all backboned animals was one. Early mammals, which are thought to have lived mostly at night, lost two of the four types and were left with two. Later, some primates, including our ancestors, gained a third type again by a change in a gene.

Researchers have looked for people who see in four dimensions, mostly women who carry gene variants for an extra kind of cone. The idea is attractive, but a fourth cone type does not by itself mean that the brain can use it, and proof is difficult. Tests that show real extra color discrimination have been reported in only a few people. Birds, then, may see a world in colors that humans can only imagine.

# Why you never see your eyes move
@series brain
@src Erdmann and Dodge, 1898; saccadic suppression research

Try this at a mirror. Look at your left eye, and then at your right. You will never see your eyes move. Yet a person watching can see them move clearly.

The reason is that the brain cuts off vision during fast eye movements. These jumps are called saccades, and you make several each second as you read or look around. A fast movement would blur the image on the retina. So the brain suppresses the signal while the eye moves, and the blur and the gap are never noticed. The effect is called saccadic masking, and it was first described by Erdmann and Dodge in 1898, when they found that an observer could never see the motion of his own eyes.

The brain also fills the gap. After a saccade you may think that the second you looked at a clock lasted longer than usual, with the second hand seeming to hang for a moment. That is chronostasis, or the stopped clock illusion, and the saccadic gap is often used to explain it. What we think is a smooth view of the world is built out of a series of snapshots, with the dark gaps edited out.

# The man who lived in a cave to find time
@series brain
@src Michel Siffre, Scarasson cave, 1962; Midnight Cave, 1972; Czeisler et al., Science, 1999

On July 16, 1962, a young French geologist, Michel Siffre, climbed down 130 meters into an icy cave, called Scarasson, in the Alps on the border of France and Italy. His plan was to live there with no clock, no sun and no calendar, to see how a person keeps time. He stayed 63 days.

When the team on the surface called him to say that his time was up, he thought it was only about the 20th of August, and that he had nearly a month to go. In fact it was mid-September, and he had lost track of about 25 days. His sleeping and waking continued in a regular cycle, of about 24 and a half hours, close to the length of a day. In 1972 he went back, and spent 205 days in Midnight Cave in Texas. The American space agency, NASA, studied his work.

His experiments helped to start the study of body clocks. Later work done by Charles Czeisler and colleagues in 1999, in controlled light, found that the average human clock runs at about 24.18 hours, a little closer to a day than the cave studies had suggested. Siffre was a single volunteer, and not a physiologist. He died in 2024 at the age of 85.

# The disease that takes away sleep
@series brain
@src Fatal familial insomnia; prion disease literature

Fatal familial insomnia is a very rare brain disease. It is caused by a mutation in a gene called PRNP, which makes a protein that can fold into a harmful shape, called a prion. Mad cow disease belongs to the same family of prion diseases. In this disease the damage begins in the thalamus, a part of the brain that helps to regulate sleep. It was first described in an Italian family in the 1980s.

The disease runs in four stages. In the first, lasting about four months, the sleep gets worse and the person has panic attacks, paranoia and phobias. In the second, for about five months, hallucinations and panic grow. In the third, over about three months, sleep is lost altogether, and the person loses weight quickly. In the last, for about six months, there is dementia, and then death.

Death usually comes within a few months to a few years from the start. There is no known treatment that changes the course of the disease. There is also a sporadic form, which occurs with no family history. The disease is a stark case of how much the brain depends on sleep, and it has been the subject of much research on prions.

# The syndrome that makes the world grow and shrink
@series brain
@src Todd, 1955; Alice in Wonderland syndrome literature

In 1955 a psychiatrist, John Todd, described a condition in which people see objects as the wrong size or distance. Things may look smaller than they are, which is called micropsia, or larger, called macropsia. They may seem too close or too far. Some people also feel that their own body has become larger or smaller. It is now called Alice in Wonderland syndrome, or Todd's syndrome, after Lewis Carroll's story, in which Alice grows and shrinks.

The cause is not known. It is often linked to migraines, head injuries, or an infection with the Epstein-Barr virus, and it may involve abnormal electrical activity in parts of the brain that handle vision. It can also be caused temporarily by certain drugs. It is more common in children, and it usually passes with time.

Some people have wondered whether Carroll himself, who had migraines, drew on such experiences, but that is a guess. Doctors do not agree on exactly which symptoms count. Some use the name only for changes in the body image, and use Alice in Wonderland-like syndrome for the other changes. The condition shows how much the sense of size is something that the brain constructs.

# The stroke patient who woke up with an accent
@series brain
@src Foreign accent syndrome case reports, 1907 to 2009

Foreign accent syndrome is an extremely rare condition in which a person begins to speak in a way that sounds like a foreign accent, without having learned one. A speaker of British English may seem to have an Italian accent, or an American may seem to be French. Most often it follows a stroke, though head injuries and migraines can cause it.

The first case was reported in 1907. Between 1941 and 2009 only 62 cases were recorded. The cause seems to be damage to the parts of the brain that plan and coordinate the movements of speech. The changes in rhythm, stress and vowels are what listeners hear as foreign. The patient does not take on any particular accent, and does not become better at any foreign language. No verified case of improved language skill after brain injury has been found.

News stories often name the accent that listeners think they hear. In truth the accents are not accurate, and the listeners are doing the matching. Patients do not say it is effortless. They feel that they have a speech disorder, and many find it upsetting. The condition shows that an accent is a pattern of timing and movement, which the brain can alter.

# The man who inspired Rain Man
@series brain
@conf mixed
@src Kim Peek, 1951 to 2009

Kim Peek was born in Salt Lake City, Utah, in 1951. He was called a megasavant, because he had an exceptional memory and a remarkable intelligence, but he also had great difficulty with many everyday tasks and with social life. He was the inspiration for the character Raymond Babbitt, played by Dustin Hoffman, in the 1988 film Rain Man.

He was born with an enlarged head, damage to the cerebellum, and no corpus callosum, the bundle of nerves that joins the two halves of the brain. Doctors thought that he should be institutionalized, but his parents refused and raised him at home. By his father's account, he began to memorize books at 16 to 20 months. He was reported to have memorized about 12,000 books. He had once been called autistic, but is now thought to have had a condition called FG syndrome.

Some scientists speculated that his unusual brain wiring, with extra connections between areas that are normally separate, explained his memory. This is a guess, and the evidence is limited, since he was only one person. Peek died in 2009 at 58. He showed that the range of what a human brain can do is wider than we assume, and that great ability and great difficulty can exist together.

# Do rats laugh when they are tickled?
@series brain
@conf mixed
@src Panksepp & Burgdorf, Physiology and Behavior, 2003

The neuroscientist Jaak Panksepp noticed that young rats make high-pitched sounds, at about 50 kilohertz, far above the range of human hearing, when they play. In 2003 he and Jeff Burgdorf reported that tickling raises the rate of these chirps. The rats also seemed to like it. They sought out the hand that had tickled them, and the ones that were most ticklish were also the most playful.

The chirping dropped when the rats were hungry, or when they were exposed to the smell of a cat, or to bright light, even when the tickling was kept the same. Panksepp called it social joy, and he argued that the sounds may be an evolutionary relative of human laughter. He hoped that mapping the brain systems behind it would help to find medicines for depression.

He was careful to say that it does not mean that rats have a sense of humor. Other scientists have questioned the idea. In 2022 a review by Burke, Pellis and Achterberg argued that the kinds of calls rats make while being tickled differ from those they make while playing with each other, so the calls may not be a simple form of laughter. The work remains a good example of how emotions in animals can be studied.

# The nasal spray that raised trust
@series mind
@conf contested
@src Kosfeld, Heinrichs, Zak, Fischbacher & Fehr, Nature, 2005

Oxytocin is a hormone that plays a role in childbirth and in bonding between mother and baby. In 2005 a team led by Michael Kosfeld asked whether it affects trust between adults. They used a game with real money. An investor could hand over some money to a trustee, the amount would be tripled, and the trustee could send back as much as he or she chose.

Of 29 investors who had a nasal spray of oxytocin, 13, or 45 percent, handed over the most money allowed. Of 29 who had a placebo, only 6, or 21 percent, did so. The average amount given was about 17 percent higher in the oxytocin group. The study was double blind, so that neither the volunteers nor the staff knew who had the real hormone.

A control experiment used a game that was like the trust game, but had no human partner. Oxytocin did not make people more willing to take risks in it, so the effect seemed to be about trust and not about taking chances. The paper was widely noted, and it gave oxytocin the nickname of the trust hormone. Later studies have given mixed results, so it is best regarded as an important finding that has not been settled.

# The man who recited twenty-two thousand digits of pi
@series mind
@conf mixed
@src Daniel Tammet, Oxford, March 14, 2004; accounts in The Scotsman and others

On March 14, 2004, Pi Day, Daniel Tammet sat in front of an audience at the Museum of the History of Science in Oxford and recited the digits of pi. He went on for five hours and nine minutes, and reached 22,514 digits, setting a European record. He did it to raise money for an epilepsy charity.

Tammet has said that he sees numbers as shapes, with colors and textures. Every number up to 10,000, he says, has its own look. He recalled the digits by moving through a landscape of these shapes, in a sense walking along them. His abilities are said to have appeared after epileptic seizures in his childhood. He is a savant who is also a synesthete, a person whose senses are linked.

The record has been disputed. Some record-keeping groups count a lower figure, because of a mistake that was not corrected without help, and the count has also been given as 22,511. A later reciter is said to have surpassed his total. It is still a striking feat of memory, and it gives a rare view into how a different mind can handle numbers.

# The silence you can hear
@series brain
@src Heller & Bergman, Annals of Otology, Rhinology and Laryngology, 1953

In 1953 two researchers, Morris Heller and Moe Bergman, asked a simple question. Do people with normal hearing hear anything when it is completely quiet? They put volunteers in a special room built to remove all sound, called an anechoic chamber, and asked them what they heard. Their paper was titled Tinnitus Aurium in Normally Hearing Persons.

Most of the volunteers reported hearing something, such as a buzzing, ringing, humming or pulsing. It was the first time that tinnitus-like sounds had been seen to occur in healthy people in absolute silence. One later account says that 95 percent of the participants reported such sounds. Later studies, which repeated the experiment, found lower figures of around 64 to 68 percent.

The sounds are produced inside the hearing system and the brain, and not by the air. In ordinary life they are covered by background noise. One idea is that in silence the brain turns up its sensitivity, and may even invent a signal. For most people the sound is faint and goes away. For those who have tinnitus all the time, it can be hard to live with. The finding is a reminder that complete silence is rare, and that the brain fills it.
