# Blaming the person, not the situation
@src Jones & Harris, 1967

In 1967 Edward Jones and Victor Harris asked students to read an essay either praising or criticizing Fidel Castro's Cuba. Some were told that the writer had chosen his position freely. Others were told that the writer had been assigned it, as in a debate class, and had no choice. Logic says that an assigned essay tells you little about what the writer believes. But the readers still concluded that the writers of pro-Castro essays must be somewhat pro-Castro, even when they knew that the position had been assigned.

The result became a classic example of what Lee Ross later named the fundamental attribution error: we tend to explain other people's behavior by their character and underrate the pull of their situation. If a coworker snaps at you, it's easy to decide that they're rude, and to forget that they may have just had terrible news. The correction is simple to say and hard to do. Before judging someone's character, ask what they were up against.

# The quiz-show experiment
@src Ross, Amabile & Steinmetz, 1977

In 1977 Lee Ross and his colleagues set up a quiz game with two players picked at random, one as questioner and the other as contestant. The questioner was told to write hard questions from his own areas of knowledge, and the contestant had to answer them. Naturally the questioners seemed brilliant and the contestants seemed ignorant, because the questioners chose the topics.

Afterward, both the contestants and a group of observers rated the questioners as more knowledgeable than the contestants. They had overlooked the obvious advantage of the role, and read the performance as a trait. It's a neat illustration of how roles shape what we see. The person at the front of the room, or at the head of the table, gets credit for knowing more, partly because they were handed the questions.

# Same event, different explanations
@src Jones & Nisbett, 1971

When you trip on the sidewalk, you blame the cracked pavement. When you see someone else trip, you may think that they're clumsy. Edward Jones and Richard Nisbett described this pattern in 1971 as the actor-observer difference. We explain our own behavior by the situation, and other people's behavior by their traits.

One reason is information. We know how our day has gone and what pressures we're under, while for other people, all we see is the behavior. It's also a matter of what's in view: when you're acting, the situation fills your attention, and when you're watching, the person does. A simple way to correct it is to give others the same generous explanation that you give yourself. They probably had a reason too, and you just can't see it.

# How little it takes to form a team
@src Tajfel et al., 1971

In 1971 Henri Tajfel and his colleagues ran an experiment in Bristol to find the smallest grounds on which people would favor their own group. Teenage boys were shown paintings by Klee and Kandinsky and told that they were being sorted by their preferences. In fact, the groups were random. The boys were then asked to divide small sums of money between two others, identified only by their group.

They gave more to members of their own group, even though they knew nothing about them, would never meet them and had nothing to gain. The boys sometimes chose to give the other group less, in order to keep a bigger difference, even when it meant less for their own side. Tajfel called these the minimal groups. The finding shows how easily we sort the world into us and them, and why even trivial labels, like a team color, can set off loyalty.

# Contact between groups
@src Allport, 1954; Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006

In 1954 the psychologist Gordon Allport proposed that contact between members of different groups could reduce prejudice, but only under the right conditions: equal status, common goals, cooperation and the support of authorities. Without these, contact might even make things worse.

In 2006 Thomas Pettigrew and Linda Tropp tested the idea by combining 515 studies from around the world. They found that, on the whole, contact does reduce prejudice, and the effect showed up even when Allport's conditions weren't all met, though it was stronger when they were. Friendship across group lines seemed especially powerful. It's an encouraging result. People who spend time together, working on something that matters, tend to find that the other side is more like them than they'd assumed.

# The test that measures speed
@src Greenwald, McGhee & Schwartz, 1998
@conf mixed

In 1998 Anthony Greenwald and his colleagues introduced the Implicit Association Test. On a computer, you sort words and pictures into categories as fast as you can, such as pairing pictures of faces with words that are good or bad. If you sort faster when one group goes with "good" than when it goes with "bad," the test reads that as an implicit preference. Millions of people have taken it online.

It's been enormously influential, and also controversial. Studies find that scores on the test have only a weak link to how people actually behave, and the same person's results can change from one day to the next. Many researchers see it as a useful tool for studying how associations form, and not as a way to diagnose an individual. A better use is as a reminder that our minds hold associations that we didn't choose, and that behavior is what counts.

# Why people do not help when there are others
@src Darley & Latané, 1968

In 1968 John Darley and Bibb Latané asked students to take part in a discussion by intercom, each in a separate booth. During the talk, one of the voices, actually a recording, began to have what sounded like a seizure and called for help. When a student thought that he was the only listener, most went for help quickly. When he thought that four others were also listening, far fewer did, and it took them longer.

The researchers called it diffusion of responsibility. When many people could help, each feels that someone else will, so no one does. A later review of many studies, in 2011, found that the effect is real but weaker in clear and dangerous emergencies, where people are more likely to act. The lesson for real life is to be the one who acts. If you need help, point to a single person and say, "You in the blue jacket, call 911."

# The story that was not quite true
@src Manning, Levine & Collins, American Psychologist, 2007
@conf mixed

In 1964 a young woman named Kitty Genovese was murdered in Queens, New York. A newspaper reported that 38 neighbors watched and did nothing, and the story became a textbook example of urban apathy and of the bystander effect. It inspired a great deal of research.

In 2007 Rachel Manning, Mark Levine and Alan Collins went back to the records and found that the account was exaggerated. There weren't 38 witnesses who saw the attack, and some people did call the police, and at least one went to help. The story had been shaped by the media. The bystander effect is real, but the case that made it famous isn't a good example. It's a reminder to be careful about stories that fit a point too well.

# The crowd that makes us better or worse
@src Triplett, 1898; Zajonc, 1965

In 1898 Norman Triplett noticed that cyclists rode faster when they raced against others than when they rode alone against the clock. He is credited with doing one of the first experiments in social psychology, in which children winding fishing reels also went faster in pairs. Having others around seemed to boost performance.

In 1965 Robert Zajonc found the explanation that fits the full picture. The presence of others increases arousal, which helps us with tasks that we know well, and hurts with tasks that are new or hard. A skilled pianist plays better in front of an audience, and a beginner makes more mistakes. The practical advice is to practice a new skill in private until it's well learned, and then perform it in front of others.

# Making a norm from nothing
@src Sherif, 1935

In the 1930s Muzafer Sherif took advantage of an optical illusion. In a dark room, a single point of light seems to move, though it doesn't. People asked to say how far it moved gave wildly different answers when alone. When they were put in groups and heard each other's guesses, their answers gradually converged on a common figure.

The group had created a norm, and the norm lasted. When individuals were tested alone a week or more later, they stuck to the group's estimate. Sherif's point was that when a situation is uncertain, we use other people as a guide, and we're often not aware that we're doing it. It's a quiet form of influence that's much more common than the dramatic kind, and a reason to ask yourself where your own judgments come from.

# Why groups go to extremes
@src Moscovici & Zavalloni, 1969

You might expect that a group discussion would pull people toward the middle. In the 1960s researchers found that it often does the opposite. In 1969 Serge Moscovici and Marisa Zavalloni asked French students about their attitudes to topics like de Gaulle and Americans. After discussing it in groups, their views became more extreme in the direction in which they'd already leaned.

It's now called group polarization. People who agree hear more arguments for their side, and want to be seen as good members of the group, so they go a step further. It's one reason why online communities of like-minded people can become more radical. A way to fight it is to include someone who disagrees, and to listen to them seriously.

# What groups fail to share
@src Stasser & Titus, JPSP, 1985

When a group makes a decision, you'd hope that each member would bring what they know to the table. In 1985 Garold Stasser and William Titus gave small groups the job of choosing a candidate for student president. Each member got a different part of the facts, and the best candidate would be clear only if the pieces were all put together. Most groups failed.

They spent their time discussing what everyone already knew, and left out the unique facts held by one person. It's called the hidden profile problem, or the common knowledge effect. Shared information feels safe and gets repeated, while the new fact is overlooked. A good chair asks everyone to write down what they know before the discussion begins, and asks, "Who has information that we haven't heard yet?"

# The brainstorming myth
@src Mullen, Johnson & Salas, 1991; Diehl & Stroebe, 1987

Brainstorming in a group has been a popular method since the 1950s, when the advertising executive Alex Osborn promoted it. The idea is that people spark off each other. But in 1987 Michael Diehl and Wolfgang Stroebe compared groups with the same number of people working alone, and found that the individuals, pooled together, came up with more ideas, and often better ones.

A review in 1991 by Brian Mullen and his colleagues found the same across many studies. Reasons include that people wait their turn, copy each other, worry about being judged, and let others do the work. The better method is to let everyone think alone first, write their ideas down, and then share and build on them. The group is good for combining and refining ideas, though not for generating them.

# Beautiful is good
@src Dion, Berscheid & Walster, 1972

In 1972 Karen Dion and her colleagues showed students photographs of people who varied in attractiveness and asked them to predict the person's traits and future. The students assumed that the attractive people had better personalities, happier marriages and better jobs. They didn't expect them to be better parents, but otherwise it was a halo around good looks.

The idea is "what is beautiful is good," and it has a good deal of support. Attractive people are treated better in many settings, from classrooms to courtrooms. It's partly unconscious. It's useful to know it, both so that you can notice it in your own judgments, and so that you don't read too much into a person's appearance. Good looks are no guide to character.

# Why we befriend our neighbors
@src Festinger, Schachter & Back, 1950

In the late 1940s Leon Festinger and his colleagues studied friendships in Westgate, a housing complex for married students at MIT. They found that who became friends depended largely on how close people lived. Neighbors in the next apartment were most likely to be close, then those two doors away, and so on. People who lived by the stairs, where they met others, had more friends.

Physical distance, in other words, shaped friendship more than shared interests did. Proximity brings repeated, unplanned meetings, which lead to liking, as the mere exposure effect suggests. It's a practical lesson for anyone who wants to make friends. Go to the same place regularly, such as a class, a gym or a coffee shop, and let familiarity do its work.

# Birds of a feather
@src Newcomb, 1961; Montoya, Horton & Kirchner, 2008

In 1961 Theodore Newcomb gave a group of male students at the University of Michigan free housing in return for taking part in a study, and assigned them to rooms. Over the semester, he followed who became friends. The best predictor was similarity of attitudes and values, and not just who lived nearby.

The idea that opposites attract has little backing. In 2008 Matthew Montoya and his colleagues combined many studies and found that perceived similarity, and actual similarity, were both strongly linked to liking in the early stages of knowing someone. Later in a relationship, actual similarity mattered less. That doesn't mean that you need to be a copy of your friends, but it helps to share what you care about.

# Comparing ourselves with others
@src Festinger, Human Relations, 1954

In 1954 Leon Festinger proposed that people have a basic drive to evaluate themselves, and when there's no objective measure, they do it by comparing themselves with others. Am I good at this? How do I compare to people like me? We tend to choose comparisons that are close to us, because they're the most informative.

Comparisons going up, to people who do better, can inspire or discourage. Comparisons going down can reassure or make us smug. The theory helps to explain why we feel happier when we're doing well compared with those around us, and why a friend's success can sting. It's worth noticing which comparisons you make, and choosing ones that motivate and don't torment.

# The illusion that we are in control
@src Langer, JPSP, 1975

In 1975 Ellen Langer ran a simple lottery in an office. Some employees were given a ticket, and others were allowed to choose their own. Before the draw, she asked each person how much they'd sell the ticket for. Those who'd picked their own number wanted far more than those who'd been handed one, though the chance of winning was identical.

She called it the illusion of control, the belief that we can influence outcomes that are really down to chance. We see it in people who throw dice harder when they want a high number, or who believe that they can feel when the stock market will turn. Some sense of control is good for well-being, but when the odds are random, it's misplaced. A good question to ask is what, exactly, you control here.

# Punishing cheats keeps cooperation going
@src Fehr & Gächter, Nature, 2002

In 2002 Ernst Fehr and Simon Gächter ran a game in which strangers could put money into a common pot, which was then multiplied and shared equally. Everyone does best if all contribute, but each person can do better by giving nothing and taking a share. With no way to punish, contributions fell quickly toward zero.

Then the players were given the option to pay a small amount to fine someone who gave too little. Many did, even though it cost them and brought no personal gain. And with punishment available, cooperation stayed high. People care about fairness enough to pay to enforce it. It helps explain why communities with ways to sanction free riders are able to maintain shared resources, and why a sense of being cheated can be so strong.

# The joy of giving it away
@src Dunn, Aknin & Norton, Science, 2008

In 2008 Elizabeth Dunn, Lara Aknin and Michael Norton gave people an envelope with five or twenty dollars, and told them to spend it by the end of the day. Some were told to spend it on themselves, and some on someone else, such as a gift or a donation. That evening, people who spent the money on others reported being happier than those who spent it on themselves, and the amount of money made no difference.

The same group had earlier found that people who spent more on others, in a national survey, were happier. They also found that people mispredict this, and expect to be happier spending on themselves. It's one of the more reliable findings in the psychology of happiness. A small gift to someone else tends to bring more pleasure than a treat for yourself, and it needs only a few dollars.

# The pull of a littered street
@src Keizer, Lindenberg & Steg, Science, 2008

In 2008 Kees Keizer and his colleagues in the Netherlands put flyers on the handlebars of bicycles parked in an alley. When the alley walls were clean, most people took the flyer with them. When the walls were covered in graffiti, far more people dropped the flyer on the ground. In some of their tests, the rate of littering about doubled.

The study supports a theory called broken windows, which suggests that visible signs of disorder encourage more disorder, because they signal that the rules aren't being followed here. It doesn't show that policing is the answer. But it shows how much the environment tells us about what's acceptable. A clean and cared-for place tends to stay that way, partly because people take their cue from it.

# Power makes us less aware
@src Galinsky et al., Psychological Science, 2006

In 2006 Adam Galinsky and his colleagues first asked people to remember a time when they'd had power over others, or a time when somebody had power over them. Then they asked them to write the letter E on their forehead. Those who'd recalled having power were about three times as likely to write the E so that it read correctly to themselves, and backward to anyone looking at them.

The researchers took this as a sign that power reduces perspective taking, the tendency to see things as another person does. Others found that powerful people are also worse at reading emotions. It doesn't mean that all leaders are self-absorbed, and it can be countered by making an effort. But a person who has power has to work harder to remember how things look from below.

# The pain of being left out
@src Eisenberger, Lieberman & Williams, Science, 2003

In 2003 Naomi Eisenberger and her colleagues put people in a brain scanner and had them play a ball-tossing video game with two other players, who they thought were real. After a while, the other two stopped throwing the ball to them. The excluded players reported distress, and their brains showed activity in a region, the anterior cingulate, that is also active when we feel physical pain.

The researchers suggested that social pain borrows from the physical pain system, because for our ancestors, being cast out of the group was a danger to survival. It helps to explain why rejection hurts so much, and why the language we use for it, such as "hurt feelings," is physical. If someone seems unreasonably upset about being left out, they may be feeling something that is, in some ways, like a bruise.

# Cheating a little
@src Mazar, Amir & Ariely, 2008

Most people think of themselves as honest. In 2008 Nina Mazar, On Amir and Dan Ariely asked students to solve puzzles and gave them a chance to cheat by misreporting their scores for money. A few cheated a lot. Many cheated a little, and the amount did not rise much when the reward grew bigger or the risk of being caught went down.

They explained it as a balance between wanting a good self-image and wanting the money. We cheat as much as we can without having to change how we see ourselves. Reminding people of morals, such as asking them to recall the Ten Commandments, reduced cheating in the first experiments, though it hasn't always held up in later ones. Most dishonesty isn't a matter of a few bad apples. It's a bit of fudging by many ordinary people.

# The honesty pledge that did not hold up
@src Kristal et al., PNAS, 2020; Shu et al., PNAS, 2012 (retracted 2021)
@conf contested

In 2012 a paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences reported that people were more honest if they signed a statement of honesty at the top of a form, before they filled it in, instead of at the bottom. It was an appealing and cheap idea, and it attracted a great deal of attention, including from governments and insurance companies.

In 2020 a team that tried to repeat the lab experiments, led by Ariella Kristal, found no effect. In 2021 a group of researchers who study data pointed out that the data in one of the paper's field experiments, at an insurance company, showed signs of having been fabricated, and the paper was retracted. The lesson isn't that signing doesn't matter. It's that a striking result needs to be repeated before it is trusted, and that even a published one can be wrong.

# When a nudge backfires
@src Schultz et al., Psychological Science, 2007

In 2007 Wesley Schultz and his colleagues left notes on the doors of households in California telling them how their electricity use compared with that of their neighbors. Households that were using more than average cut back, as hoped. But those who were using less than average increased their use, drifting up toward the norm. It was called the boomerang effect.

The researchers then added a simple smiley face to the notes of the low users, to show approval. It prevented the increase. The result shows that information about what others do can pull people in both directions, and that a signal of approval or disapproval helps to hold them. When you talk about how common a good or bad behavior is, it's wise to think about who is listening.

# The moral argument with no reasons
@src Haidt, Psychological Review, 2001

In 2001 Jonathan Haidt published a paper called "The emotional dog and its rational tail." He asked people about a story: a brother and sister, adults, decide to have sex once, in secret, using protection, and they don't regret it. Most people said that it was wrong. When asked why, they listed reasons, such as risk of birth defects or harm to their relationship, which the story had excluded. Then they said, "I don't know, I just know that it's wrong."

Haidt called this moral dumbfounding. He proposed that moral judgments are mostly quick, emotional reactions, and that our reasons come later, like a lawyer arguing for a client. The idea is debated, and people can and do change their moral views through reasoning. But it's a reminder that when we're sure that something is wrong, the certainty may arrive before the argument does.

# The danger of being just a bit better
@src Monin & Miller, JPSP, 2001
@conf mixed

In 2001 Benoît Monin and Dale Miller reported a puzzling effect. People who had first been given a chance to show that they weren't prejudiced, for example by disagreeing with a sexist statement, were afterward more willing to make a prejudiced choice in a hiring decision. It was as if they had earned credit that they could spend.

It's known as moral licensing: a good deed seems to give us permission to do something less good. It could help explain why someone who exercises goes on to eat dessert, or why a person who donates to a charity feels free to cut corners elsewhere. Later studies have found the effect to be smaller than first thought, but it's real in some settings. A good practice is to be wary of the feeling that you've earned a lapse.
