# Water, earth and sea: aqua, hydr, mar, terr, geo

Many English words about the natural world come from Latin and Greek roots, and the elements are a good place to begin. Aqua is the Latin word for "water." An aquarium is a place for water animals, something aquatic lives in or near water, and an aqueduct is a channel for carrying water, like the great stone ones the Romans built. An aquifer is a layer of rock that holds underground water. The Greek root hydr also means "water." To hydrate is to add water to the body, a hydrant is an outlet where water comes out, and the element hydrogen is named for the water that it makes when it burns.

Mar is Latin for "sea." Something marine belongs to the sea, a submarine travels under it, and a mariner is a sailor. Terr is Latin for "earth" or "land." Terrain is the shape of the ground, a territory is a piece of land, and something terrestrial belongs to the earth. A terrier is a dog that goes into the earth after burrowing animals. Geo is the Greek word for "earth," and geography is earth writing, geology is the study of the earth, and geometry was originally earth measure, from the surveying of land.

# Stars, light and heat: astro, luc, photo, therm

Four roots are enough to read much of the vocabulary of light and heat. Astro is Greek for "star." Astronomy is the study of the stars, an asterisk is a little star, the symbol *, and an astronaut is a star sailor. Luc, or lum, is Latin for "light." Something lucid is clear and bright, to illuminate is to throw light on something, and something luminous gives off light. Translucent means letting light through, but not clearly. The word "Lucifer" means light-bringer, and "lunar," relating to the moon, comes from the same family.

Photo is Greek for "light." A photograph is a drawing with light, photosynthesis is how plants put together sugar using light, and a photon is a particle of light. Therm is Greek for "heat." A thermometer measures heat, a thermostat keeps it steady, and hypothermia is a dangerous drop in body heat. A thermos bottle keeps drinks hot or cold. Whenever a word concerns light, or temperature, one of these roots is likely hiding in it.

# Seeing: vid, spect, opt, scope

English has a great many roots for "see," because Latin and Greek each supplied several. Vid, or vis, comes from the Latin videre, and video means "I see." Vision is the power of sight, something evident is clearly seen, and to supervise is to see over, or to oversee. Spect comes from specere, "to look." To inspect is to look into, a spectator looks on, and perspective is a way of looking through. To respect is to look back at someone, to regard them with honor, and to suspect is to look at from below, with doubt.

The Greek roots appear in the sciences. Opt, from the Greek for "eye" or "sight," gives us optic, optician and myopia, which means short sight. The ending -scope, from "to look at," gives us microscope, telescope and periscope. Even the word "television" is a hybrid, built from the Greek tele, "far," and the Latin visio, "sight." It's a pleasing mix, and an example of how English borrows from both languages at once.

# Hearing and distance: aud, phon, tele

Three roots can help you with words about sound. Aud comes from the Latin verb audire, "to hear." An audience is a group who listen, audio is sound, and an auditorium is a hall for hearing. Audible means able to be heard, and an audition is a hearing of a performer. Even "audit" comes from it, since early auditors listened as people read out the accounts.

Phon is from the Greek for "sound" or "voice." A telephone carries sound far, a symphony is sounds together, and a microphone makes small sounds bigger. Phonics teaches reading by the sounds of the letters, and the saxophone is named for its inventor, Adolphe Sax, and its sound. Tele, the Greek word for "far," completes the family. The telescope looks far, television sees far, and the telephone sounds far. The prefix keeps coming back in modern words, such as "teleconference" and "telecommute."

# Saying and writing: dict, voc, log, graph, scrib

English is full of words about speaking and writing, and they come from five roots. Dict is from the Latin dicere, "to say." To dictate is to say aloud for someone else to write, to predict is to say before, and a verdict is a true saying, the decision of a jury. A dictionary is a book of sayings. Voc comes from vocare, "to call," and vox, "voice." Vocal means of the voice, to invoke is to call upon, and a vocation is a calling. A vocabulary is the collection of words that you can call on.

Log, from the Greek logos, means "word," "reason" or "study." A dialogue is words exchanged between two people, and a prologue is words that come before a story. Graph is Greek for "to write" or "to draw." An autograph is your own writing of your name, a telegram is writing that goes far, and a photograph is light writing. Scrib, or script, is the Latin version. To describe is to write down what something is like, to prescribe is to write before, and a manuscript is handwritten. Between them, these roots cover most of what we do with words.

# Knowing and feeling: cogn, psych, sens, path, mem, phil

A good number of roots deal with the inner life. Cogn comes from the Latin cognoscere, "to know." To recognize is to know again, cognitive describes thinking, and incognito means unknown, hiding who you are. It's even related to the English word "know," through an ancestor older than Latin. Psych is Greek for "mind," "soul" or "breath." Psychology is the study of the mind, and a psychiatrist is a healer of the mind. In Greek myth, Psyche was a mortal woman who became the goddess of the soul.

Sens, from the Latin "to feel," gives us sense, sentiment and consent, which is feeling together, or agreeing. Path, from the Greek for "feeling" or "suffering," gives us sympathy, empathy and apathy, a lack of feeling. Mem, from the Latin for "mindful," gives us memory, memorial and memento, a keepsake. Phil is Greek for "to love," and philosophy is the love of wisdom. A bibliophile is a lover of books. These roots remind us that thinking and feeling have always shared the same vocabulary.

# Going and coming: ced, gress, ven, migr, mov

A family of roots expresses movement from place to place. Ced, ceed and cess come from the Latin for "to go" or "to yield." To proceed is to go forward, to recede is to go back, and access is a way of going to a place. Only three common English words end in "-ceed": exceed, proceed and succeed. Gress, or grad, means "step." To progress is to step forward, to regress is to step back, and to graduate is to step up a grade. Something gradual happens step by step.

Ven, from "to come," gives us event, which is a coming out, prevent, which is to come before, and convention, a coming together. Migr is "to move from place to place," which gives us migrate, immigrant, who moves into a country, and emigrant, who moves out. Mov, or mot, is "to move." To remove is to move away, emotion is a moving out, since feelings stir us from calm, and a movie is a moving picture. Each root adds its own shade to the idea of travel.

# Carrying and leading: port, fer, mit, duc, tract

Five Latin roots describe how things get from one place to another. Port means "to carry." To transport is to carry across, to import is to carry in, to export is to carry out, and something portable can be carried easily. Fer means the same thing. To transfer is to carry across, to refer is to carry back, and a ferry carries people over water. A conifer bears cones, and fertile means able to bear fruit.

Mit, or miss, means "to send." To transmit is to send across, a mission is a task someone is sent to do, and to dismiss is to send away. Duc, or duct, means "to lead." To produce is to lead forth, to reduce is to lead back, and to conduct is to lead with. Education comes from "leading out," drawing out what's in a person. Tract means "to pull." To attract is to pull toward, a tractor pulls, and to distract is to pull your attention apart.

# Turning and bending: vert, volv, cycl, flect, plic

Many words about shape and movement come from roots that mean "turn," "roll," "bend" or "fold." Vert, or vers, is Latin for "to turn." To convert is to turn around, to reverse is to turn back, and an introvert is someone turned inward. Volv means "to roll." To revolve is to roll around and back, to evolve is to roll out, to unfold gradually, and a volume was originally a scroll, rolled up. Cycl is the Greek root for "circle" or "wheel." A bicycle has two wheels, a cyclone is a circling storm, and an encyclopedia was once a "circle of learning."

Flect, or flex, means "to bend." To reflect is to bend back, as a mirror does with light, something flexible can bend without breaking, and to deflect is to bend something away. Plic, or ply, is "to fold." To apply is to fold something onto something else, something complicated is folded together, and to reply is to fold back an answer. "Simple" comes from "one fold," and "duplicate" from "two folds." Think of these roots as the moves of a dance.

# Taking and touching: cap, tend, pend, tact

Four roots are about the way hands and bodies deal with things. Cap, capt and cept come from the Latin capere, "to take" or "seize." To capture is to take hold of, to accept is to take something to yourself, and a concept is a thing taken together in the mind, an idea. Tend, or tens, means "to stretch." To extend is to stretch out, tension is a pulling tight, and to pretend is to stretch something forward, like a cover. A tent is stretched cloth.

Pend, or pens, means "to hang" or "to weigh." To suspend is to hang something under, a pendulum is a hanging weight, and to depend is to hang from something else. Expensive means weighed out, because money used to be weighed. Tact, or tang, is from the Latin for "to touch." Contact is touching together, something tangible can be touched, and something intact has not been touched, and so is whole. Tact, the skill of handling delicate situations, means a delicate touch.

# Breaking and cutting: frag, rupt, cide, sect

Four roots deal with what happens when something is split. Frag, or fract, from the Latin frangere, means "to break." Something fragile is easily broken, a fracture is a break, especially in a bone, and a fraction is a broken piece of a whole number. To refract is to break back, which is what happens to light when it bends as it passes from air into water. Rupt, from rumpere, is also "to break." To erupt is to break out, to interrupt is to break between, and to be bankrupt is to be broken financially.

Cide, or cis, from the Latin for "to kill" or "to cut," gives us homicide, suicide and pesticide, and also incision, a cut, and concise, cut down to the essentials. Even "decide" belongs here, since to decide is to cut off other options. Sect comes from secare, "to cut." A section is a cut part, to dissect is to cut apart, and an insect is a cut-up creature, named for its body, which looks as if it's divided into segments.

# Making and building: fac, struct, form, lab, techn

Five roots are about the work of making things. Fac, fact and fect come from the Latin facere, "to make" or "to do." A factory is a place of making, to manufacture was originally to make by hand, and an effect is something made by a cause. A fact was originally a thing done. Struct, from struere, means "to build." A structure is a built thing, to construct is to build together, and to destroy is to unbuild. An instrument is a tool for building.

Form is from the Latin forma, "shape." To transform is to change shape, a uniform has one shape, and a formula was originally a little form, a small pattern or rule. Lab comes from labor, "work." A laboratory is a place of work, to collaborate is to work together, and to elaborate is to work something out in detail. Techn is the Greek root for "art" or "skill." Technique is a way of doing a skill, and technology is the study of crafts and now of machines. To the Greeks, the same word meant both art and craft.

# Strength, greatness and balance: fort, magn, equi

Some roots describe qualities. Fort comes from the Latin fortis, "strong." To fortify is to make strong, as with a castle wall, effort is strength put out, and fortitude is inner strength, the courage to endure. To comfort someone is to strengthen them with your presence. Magn comes from magnus, "great." To magnify is to make something appear greater, something magnificent is great in deed or appearance, and magnitude is size, as in the magnitude of an earthquake. Someone magnanimous has a great soul, and is generous.

Equi comes from aequus, "equal" or "even." An equation says that two things are equal, and equilibrium means equal balance, when forces cancel out. An equinox, in spring and fall, is the time when day and night are equal in length, from "equal night." Equity, meaning fairness, is also related. It's pleasing that three roots so basic should give us words for strength, size and fairness, which are things we care about in life as well as in language.

# Law, people and rule: jud, dem, arch, liber, cracy

A set of roots from Greek and Latin describes how people govern themselves. Jud, or jus, from the Latin for "law" or "right," gives us judge, a person who speaks the law, and justice. Prejudice is a judgment made before knowing the facts, and a jury is a group sworn to give a lawful verdict. Dem comes from the Greek demos, "the people." Democracy is rule by the people, demographics are statistics about a population, and an epidemic is a disease that comes upon the people. A demagogue is a leader who appeals to the crowd's emotions.

Arch is Greek for "ruler" or "first." A monarchy is rule by one, anarchy is without rule, and archaic means from the first, very old. An architect is a chief builder. Liber is the Latin root for "free." Liberty is the state of being free, to liberate is to set free, and "liberal" first meant generous, free-giving, before the word took on political meanings. The ending -cracy, from the Greek for "power," completes the set, as in democracy and aristocracy.

# Family and birth: pater, mater, nat, gen, nov

A few roots describe family and origins. Pater is the Latin word for "father." Paternal means fatherly, and a patriot loves the fatherland, from the Latin for the "country of one's fathers." A patron was a protector, like a father, who looked after his dependents. Mater is "mother." Maternal means motherly, matrimony is the state of marriage, from a word that meant the condition of being a mother, and a matriarch is a woman who rules a family. The words "matter" and "material" are closely related, as if everything were made by a mother.

Nat comes from the Latin "to be born." Something native is born in a place, a nation is people born together in the same land, and prenatal means before birth. Gen means "birth," "kind" or "origin," as in generate, genesis and genre. A gene is a unit of heredity passed from parent to child. Nov is Latin for "new." A novel was once simply something new, which is why the book form got that name. To renovate is to make it new again, and a novice is a beginner.

# Belief, trust and thanks: cred, fid, grat

Three roots describe the way that we depend on one another. Cred comes from the Latin credere, "to believe" or "to trust." Credit is trust that you'll pay later, something credible is believable, and a creed is a statement of belief, from the Latin for "I believe." A credential is something that makes people trust you. Fid comes from fides, "faith" or "trust." Fidelity is faithfulness, which is why the motto of the US Marine Corps is "Semper Fidelis." To confide is to trust someone with a secret, and to be confident is to have trust, especially in yourself. The common dog name "Fido" is Latin for "I trust."

Grat comes from gratus, "pleasing" or "thankful." To be grateful is to be full of thanks, gratis means free of charge, given as a favor, and to congratulate is to rejoice together with someone. A gratuity is a tip, a pleasing extra. The Spanish "gracias" and the French "grâce" come from the same root. When you thank someone, you are using a word that began as "pleasing."

# Life and death: bio, vit, mort, zoo, anthrop

The biggest subjects have the oldest roots. Bio comes from the Greek word for "life." Biology is the study of life, a biography is the writing of a life, and a biome is a large community of living things, such as a desert or a rainforest. An antibiotic is a medicine "against life," though it is against only the harmful kind. Vit, or viv, is the Latin counterpart. Something vital is of life, something vivid is full of life, and to survive is to live beyond danger. A vitamin was named "vitamine" in 1912, a life amine.

Mort is Latin for "death." Something mortal is subject to death, and immortal is not. A mortgage is a "dead pledge," and a mortuary is a place for the dead. Zoo comes from the Greek for "animal," so zoology is the study of animals, and a zoo is short for "zoological garden." Anthrop is Greek for "human," so anthropology is the study of humans, and a misanthrope is a hater of humankind. Between them, they cover being alive, being human and dying.

# Hands, feet and teeth: man, ped, dent, carn, vor

Some roots simply name parts of the body. Man comes from the Latin manus, "hand." A manual is done by hand, a manuscript is handwritten, and to manufacture was to make by hand. To emancipate is to release from the hand, to set free. Ped is from the Latin pes, "foot," and the Greek cousin is pod. A pedal is pushed with the foot, a pedestrian goes on foot, a centipede seems to have a hundred feet, and a podium is a place to stand. A podiatrist is a foot doctor.

Dent comes from the Latin dens, "tooth." A dentist treats the teeth, and a trident is a three-toothed spear. The dandelion is named from the French for "lion's tooth," because of its jagged leaves. Carn is Latin for "flesh." A carnivore is a flesh eater, and carnage is the slaughter of many people. Vor means "to eat," so a herbivore eats plants and voracious means eating greedily. They are everyday roots for everyday words.

# Inside the body: cardi, derm, gastr, hem, neuro

Doctors have borrowed most of their words from Greek, and a short list of roots can take you a long way. Cardi means "heart." Cardiac means of the heart, and cardiology is its study. Derm means "skin." Dermatology is the study of the skin, and hypodermic means under the skin. A pachyderm is a thick-skinned animal, like an elephant. Gastr means "stomach," as in gastric juice, and a gastropod is a stomach-foot, such as a snail. Hem, from the Greek for "blood," gives us hemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen, and anemia, a shortage of red blood cells.

Neuro means "nerve." A neuron is a nerve cell, and a neurotransmitter carries messages between them. Oste means "bone," so osteoporosis is a condition of weakened bones. Somn is Latin for "sleep." Insomnia is no sleep, and a somnambulist is a sleepwalker. The Roman god of sleep was Somnus, and his Greek counterpart was Hypnos, who gave us "hypnosis." If you meet a word you don't know at the doctor's office, look for one of these.

# Breath, god and city: spir, theo, urb

Three roots take us to some larger ideas. Spir, from the Latin for "to breathe," links breath with spirit. To inspire is to breathe into, to fill with an idea. Respiration is breathing again and again, to expire is to breathe out or to breathe one's last, and to conspire is to breathe together, to whisper a plot. The word "spirit" itself is "the breath of life." Theo is the Greek root for "god." Theology is the study of the divine, monotheism is belief in one god, and a theocracy is a state ruled in the name of god.

The word "enthusiasm" began here too, from the Greek for "having the god within." Urb is the Latin root for "city." Something urban is of the city, a suburb is under, or near, the city, and urbane means polished and refined, as city people were once thought to be. A conurbation is a group of cities that have grown together. Urban and rural are opposites, and one of them has been growing for centuries.

# Asking, emptiness and truth: quer, vac, ver

Three roots that look unrelated each describe something we all do. Quer, or quis, comes from the Latin for "to ask" or "to seek." A query is a question, to inquire is to ask into something, and a quest is a search. An inquisition is an investigation, and "exquisite" comes from it too, meaning sought out, and so choice. Vac comes from vacare, "empty." Something vacant is empty, a vacuum is an empty space with no air, and to evacuate is to empty out a place. A vacation is a time emptied of work.

Ver is Latin for "true." To verify is to make true, by checking, and veracity is truthfulness. To aver is to declare something to be true. Even the word "very," as in "the very truth," comes from it, by way of an Old French word for true. The three roots make a small story. We ask a question, we find an empty answer, and we look for what's true.

# Time and the body: chron, corp

Two roots are about the most basic things we have, time and a body. Chron is from the Greek word for "time." Chronology is the order of events in time, and a chronic illness is one that lasts a long time. To synchronize is to make things happen at the same time. An anachronism is something that doesn't belong in its time, like a clock in a play set in ancient Rome. Chronos was also the Greek personification of time.

Corp comes from the Latin corpus, "body," and it shows up in more words than you might expect. A corporation is a legal body made of many people, a corpse is a dead body, and a corps, pronounced "core," is a body of people, like the Peace Corps. To incorporate is to put something into a body. Even "corpulent" comes from it, meaning having a large body. The roots show how language treats a company as a body, with time passing through it.

# Closing, joining and measuring: clud, junct, meter

Three more roots are worth knowing for the way that they explain everyday words. Clud, or clus, comes from the Latin word for "to close" or "to shut." To include is to shut something in, to exclude is to shut it out, and a conclusion is a closing, the end of an argument. Claustrophobia, the fear of closed spaces, comes from the same root. A cloister is a closed courtyard, and a clause was originally a closing part of a sentence.

Junct is Latin for "join," from jungere. A junction is a place where things join, like a road meeting another. A conjunction is a word that joins others, such as "and" or "but," and an adjunct is something joined to a main thing as an extra. Meter, or metr, comes from the Greek metron, "measure." A thermometer measures heat, a diameter measures across a circle, and symmetry means measured together, with matching parts. The unit called the meter was set up in France in the 1790s, and its name was taken from the same Greek word.
