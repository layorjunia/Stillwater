# The architect who read the oldest Greek
@series history
@src Ventris and Chadwick, 1953; Kober's research; Linear B tablets

In 1900 the English archaeologist Arthur Evans dug up thousands of clay tablets at Knossos, on Crete, covered in a script nobody could read. It was called Linear B. The signs were of two kinds, about 87 that stood for syllables and over 100 that stood for things such as grain and sheep. The earliest tablets date from about 1450 BC.

The script was cracked in 1952 by Michael Ventris, an English architect who had been interested in the puzzle since he was a boy. He worked from the research of an American classicist, Alice Kober, who had found patterns in the endings of words, and had died in 1950. Ventris had expected the language to be something other than Greek. When the sounds fell into place, the tablets turned out to be Greek, the oldest form we know, now called Mycenaean.

Most of the tablets are dull lists kept by palace officials, counting goods and workers. The handwriting of only a few dozen scribes has been identified, 45 at Pylos and 66 at Knossos. Linear B is the only Bronze Age script of the Aegean that has been deciphered. Its sister script Linear A is still unread, and the language it wrote is unknown.

# The book nobody can read
@series history
@src Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, Yale; Voynich manuscript studies

In 1912 a Polish book dealer named Wilfrid Voynich bought an old manuscript of about 240 pages, filled with writing in a script that no one could read. It had drawings of strange plants, charts of stars, and small human figures bathing in green pools. The manuscript is named for him. Since 1969 it has been held by Yale University's Beinecke Library, and in 2020 Yale put the whole book online.

Carbon dating suggests that the vellum was made between 1404 and 1438, somewhere in central Europe. The first owner who can be traced was Georg Baresch, an alchemist in Prague in the 1600s, who wrote that he could make no sense of it. Professional codebreakers from both world wars, and many amateurs, have tried to read it.

No one has succeeded. The suggestions include an unknown language, a cipher, an invented tongue, a hoax and a work of fiction. The text has patterns, such as repeated words, that make it look like language, which is part of what keeps people trying. The honest answer after more than a century is that nobody knows what it says, or whether it says anything.

# The dog and the cave of bulls
@series history
@src Lascaux Cave, Dordogne, France; UNESCO World Heritage List, 1979

On September 12, 1940, an 18-year-old named Marcel Ravidat was out in the woods near the village of Montignac, in southwestern France. His dog, Robot, was said to have investigated a hole left where a tree had been pulled up. Ravidat came back with three friends and they climbed down a shaft of about fifteen meters.

They found a cave whose walls were covered with paintings of animals. There are more than 600 painted figures, mostly large animals that lived in the region in the Upper Paleolithic, such as horses, deer, aurochs and ibex. Parts of the cave have names, such as the Hall of the Bulls. The paintings are thought to be 17,000 to 22,000 years old, the work of many generations.

After the war the cave was opened to visitors, and the breath and warmth of crowds began to harm the paintings. It was closed to the public in 1963, and there are now replicas for people to visit. In 1979 UNESCO placed it on the World Heritage List. The story of the dog has been changed in the telling. Ravidat later said that the dog had fallen into the hole, and that is probably not what happened.

# The day the forest fell down in Siberia
@series sci
@src Tunguska event, June 30, 1908; Kulik expeditions, 1921 to 1927

At about seven in the morning on June 30, 1908, a huge explosion took place over a remote region of Siberia, near the Podkamennaya Tunguska River. Witnesses were knocked to the ground, and at least three people died. Windows shattered in places 200 kilometers away. Seismic stations in Russia and Germany picked up the blast, and for several nights afterward the skies over Europe were bright enough to read by.

No scientific team went there until 1921, when Leonid Kulik led an expedition, and he found the center of the blast only in 1927. About 2,150 square kilometers of forest had been flattened, and the trees lay in a radial pattern, pointing away from a center. Many trees still stood at the center with their branches stripped. That pointed to an explosion in the air.

The consensus now is that a stony object, perhaps 50 to 60 meters across, entered the atmosphere at high speed and exploded between five and ten kilometers up, with the energy of 10 to 15 megatons of TNT. No crater was found, and no fragment has been formally identified. The event is the largest known impact of its kind in recorded history.

# The pollution that made the world breathable
@series sci
@src Great Oxidation Event, about 2.4 billion years ago

For the first half of the Earth's history, the air had almost no free oxygen. Then, about 2.46 billion years ago, oxygen began to build up in the atmosphere and the shallow seas. It is called the Great Oxidation Event. The cause was life itself. Microbes that carried out photosynthesis, using sunlight to make food, gave off oxygen as a waste product.

Oxygen is a reactive gas, and to the organisms that lived then, mostly microbes that did not use it, it was poisonous. The event may have wiped out many of them, though it is not counted among the great mass extinctions, partly because the evidence from microscopic life so long ago is hard to read. It also changed the chemistry of the planet, as iron in the oceans rusted and settled out.

The event is usually dated from about 2.46 billion to about 2.06 billion years ago. Oxygen then stayed at a low level, from under one percent to perhaps ten percent of today's, for a very long time. It rose to modern levels much later. The oxygen that animals now breathe began as the waste of bacteria, and was once a disaster for the life that existed.

# The fish with wrists
@series sci
@src Daeschler, Shubin & Jenkins, Nature, 2006

For a long time, there was a gap in the fossil record between fish and the four-legged animals that walk on land. In 2004 a team looked for it on Ellesmere Island, in the Canadian Arctic. The team was Edward Daeschler, Neil Shubin and Farish Jenkins. They chose the place because its rocks were of the right age, about 375 million years, and had been laid down by streams, in the late Devonian period.

They found the remains of an animal that was part fish and part land animal. It had scales and gills, but also a flat head, a neck, and sturdy bones inside its fins. The bones would have let it prop itself up in shallow water. They named it Tiktaalik, a word from the Inuit language of the region, and published the find in the journal Nature on April 6, 2006.

It was between 1.25 and 2.75 meters long. Tiktaalik is now one of the best known transitional fossils. It does not mean that fish came ashore in a single step. Related animals such as Acanthostega and Ichthyostega, from Greenland, give other pieces of the story. But it showed that the places and rocks where scientists expected to find such a creature really contained it.

# The relatives we found in a finger bone
@series history
@src Denisova Cave, Altai Mountains; Max Planck studies, 2010 onward

In 2008 archaeologists digging in Denisova Cave, in the Altai Mountains of Siberia, found a small piece of a finger bone from a young girl. In 2010 researchers who extracted DNA from it announced something unexpected. It was neither modern human nor Neanderthal. They had found a new kind of ancient human, and they called it Denisovan, after the cave.

For years little more was known, because there were few remains. Studies of nuclear DNA showed that the Denisovans were closer to Neanderthals than to us. Other fossils have since been identified as Denisovan, from a cave on the Tibetan Plateau, from Laos, from the sea floor off Taiwan, and from a skull found in Harbin in China, which was shown to be Denisovan from its proteins.

They lived across Asia from about 200,000 to 32,000 years ago. DNA suggests that they had dark skin, eyes and hair. No formal species name has been given, since the first remains were so small. The discovery is a reminder that a species can be found first in DNA, and that a bone the size of a fingertip can change the story of human origins.

# The small people of Flores
@series history
@conf mixed
@src Brown et al., Nature, 2004; Liang Bua excavations

On September 2, 2003, a team of Australian and Indonesian archaeologists digging in a cave called Liang Bua, on the island of Flores in Indonesia, found the remains of a very small adult. Stooped over a skeleton, they found a person who would have stood about 1.1 meters, or 3 feet 7 inches, tall, with a skull about the size of a grapefruit. They called her LB1.

Partial skeletons of 15 individuals have since been recovered. The species was named Homo floresiensis, and nicknamed the hobbit. At first it was thought that they may have lived as recently as 12,000 years ago. More careful dating has put the skeletons at 60,000 to 100,000 years old, and the latest evidence for the species at about 50,000 years ago.

Some scientists argued that LB1 was a modern human with a disease. Most now accept that it was a distinct species, whose small size is an example of island dwarfism, which is seen in many animals isolated on islands. What its ancestors were is still debated. The finding showed that other kinds of humans lived on Earth until fairly recently, and not all of them looked as we expected.

# The eruption that may have nearly ended us
@series sci
@conf contested
@src Toba eruption, about 74,000 years ago; Rose and Chesner; later studies

About 74,000 years ago a volcano on the island of Sumatra, in what is now Lake Toba, erupted on a scale far beyond any in recorded history. It is rated the largest explosive eruption of the last 2.5 million years, at the top of the volcanic explosivity scale. It probably lasted from nine days to two weeks, and happened in the northern summer.

In the 1990s an idea took hold that its ash and sulfur cooled the Earth for years, and that humanity dropped to a few thousand survivors. This is the Toba catastrophe theory, which was supported by the low genetic variation found in people today. It was an appealing story, and it was widely reported.

Later work has made it far less certain. Studies of ash layers and of tools at excavation sites suggest that the eruption did not cause a global disaster, and that people lived through it. Genetic evidence of a bottleneck has other explanations. The case is a warning about neat stories. A major event and a pattern in the evidence can be linked in a way that later turns out to be incomplete.

# The jar of lightning and early Earth
@series sci
@src Miller, Science, 1953; Johnson et al., Science, 2008

In 1952 a graduate student at the University of Chicago, Stanley Miller, set up an experiment to test a question about the origin of life. He was supervised by the Nobel laureate Harold Urey. Miller built a closed set of glass flasks, with water that was boiled, and a mixture of gases that were thought to resemble the early Earth's air, namely methane, ammonia and hydrogen.

He passed electric sparks through the gases, like lightning, and let them cool and run through the water. After a few days the water had turned brown, and it contained amino acids, the building blocks of proteins. He published it in 1953. It showed that molecules of life could form from simple chemicals under plausible conditions, and it supported an idea of Alexander Oparin and J. B. S. Haldane.

After Miller died in 2007, scientists found sealed vials from his old experiments and tested them with modern methods. They found that more amino acids had formed than he had been able to detect. Many researchers now think that the early atmosphere was different from his mixture. But similar experiments under other conditions also produce amino acids, and the work opened the field of experimental origin-of-life research.

# The most beautiful experiment in biology
@series sci
@src Meselson & Stahl, 1958

When Watson and Crick described DNA in 1953, they suggested a way it might copy itself. The two strands would come apart, and each would act as a template for a new partner. That would mean that each new molecule has one old strand and one new strand. It is called semiconservative replication. But other ideas were possible. In one, the whole molecule would stay intact as a template, and produce an all-new copy.

In 1958 Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl found a way to test it. They grew bacteria for many generations in a food that contained a heavy form of nitrogen, so that the bacteria's DNA was heavy. Then they switched them to ordinary light nitrogen, and spun samples of the DNA in a tube to separate heavy from light.

After one generation, all the DNA was of one intermediate weight, which was neither all heavy nor all light. After two generations, there were two bands, one intermediate and one light. That ruled out the other ideas, and fit the Watson and Crick model. The experiment has been called the most beautiful experiment in biology, because the result was so clean.

# The mice that carried a message
@series sci
@src Griffith, 1928; Avery, MacLeod & McCarty, Journal of Experimental Medicine, 1944

In 1928 a British doctor, Frederick Griffith, was trying to make a vaccine against pneumonia. He used two strains of the pneumococcus bacterium. One had a smooth coat and killed mice. The other, rough, was harmless. When he killed the smooth ones with heat and injected the remains, the mice lived. When he injected the dead smooth bacteria with living rough ones, the mice died.

He found live smooth bacteria in the dead mice. Something in the dead bacteria had changed the harmless ones into killers. He called it transformation. Until then, people thought that bacterial types were fixed. The experiment was the first sign that bacteria can pass on genetic information.

What the substance was remained a puzzle. In 1944 Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod and Maclyn McCarty, at the Rockefeller Institute, reported that it was DNA. Most scientists had believed that proteins carried heredity, since they seemed more complex, and many doubted the result. It took further work, including a famous 1952 experiment with viruses by Hershey and Chase, to settle it. The mice carried the first clue to what genes are made of.

# The man who won two Nobel Prizes for reading
@series people
@src Sanger; Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 1958 and 1980

Frederick Sanger was born in 1918 in a village in Gloucestershire, England, the son of a doctor, and was brought up as a Quaker. He became a biochemist. In 1958 he won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for working out the order of the amino acids in insulin, the first protein to be completely sequenced. It showed that a protein has a definite and exact structure.

He did not stop. At the new Laboratory of Molecular Biology in Cambridge, he went on to invent a way to read the order of the letters in DNA, which was published in the 1970s. It was the first DNA sequencing method, and a version of it remained in wide use for decades. It made the Human Genome Project possible. In 1980 he shared a second Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Walter Gilbert and Paul Berg.

Only five people have won two Nobel Prizes, and Sanger is one of only three to win twice in the same field. He turned down a knighthood, and preferred to be called plain Mr. Sanger. He died in 2013 at the age of 95. His career shows how much a science can change when someone invents a way to read what is written in it.

# The sheep that was not the first clone
@series sci
@src Roslin Institute; Wilmut, Campbell et al., Nature, 1997

Dolly was born on July 5, 1996, at the Roslin Institute near Edinburgh, Scotland. She was a Finn-Dorset sheep, and the first mammal cloned from an adult cell. Ian Wilmut, Keith Campbell and their colleagues took a cell from the udder of a six-year-old sheep, put its nucleus into an egg cell from which the nucleus had been removed, and gave a pulse of electricity to start development.

It was said that she was named after the singer Dolly Parton, since the cell came from a mammary gland. The importance was that it proved that a cell from an adult body, once specialized, can be made to start again and produce a whole animal. Contrary to what many believe, she was not the first animal to be cloned. John Gurdon had cloned frogs in 1958 from the cells of tadpoles.

Dolly lived at Roslin, and gave birth to lambs. She was put down in 2003 at the age of six because of a progressive lung disease, and no link to her cloning was found. Her body is displayed at the National Museum of Scotland. Her work led to research on stem cells, including the discovery that adult cells can be turned back into a flexible state.

# The study that taught medicine to ask permission
@series history
@src U.S. Public Health Service, 1932 to 1972; Belmont Report, 1979

In 1932 the United States Public Health Service began a study in Macon County, Alabama, of how syphilis develops when it is not treated. It enrolled 600 poor Black sharecroppers, 399 with syphilis and 201 without. The men were told that they were being treated for bad blood. They were offered free medical care, meals and burial insurance.

They were not told that they had syphilis, and they were not given effective treatment. By 1945 penicillin was widely used to cure the disease, but the study went on without giving it to the men. More than 100 of them died as a result of the disease, and wives and children were also infected. The study went on for forty years.

In 1972 a news report exposed it, and it was ended. The government set up new rules. In 1979 the Belmont Report laid out the principles of respect for persons, doing good and avoiding harm, and fairness, which became the basis of rules for informed consent in research on people in the United States. In 1997 President Bill Clinton formally apologized to the survivors. The study is often named as one reason that many Black Americans have distrusted medical research.

# The doctor who made polio a disease of the past
@series people
@src Albert Sabin, 1906 to 1993; oral polio vaccine

Albert Sabin was born Abram Saperstejn, in 1906, in Białystok, in the Russian Empire, now in Poland. His family emigrated to the United States in 1921 and settled in Paterson, New Jersey. He began a dentistry course at New York University, but wanted to do medical research instead, and he received a medical degree in 1931.

In the 1950s the world had a vaccine against polio, the injected one of Jonas Salk. Sabin believed that a vaccine made of a weakened live virus, taken by mouth, would give longer protection and be easier to give. He worked on it for years at a research center in Cincinnati. Large trials were carried out in the Soviet Union on millions of children, and the oral vaccine was licensed in the United States in 1961.

It was cheap and could be given on a sugar cube, and it played a key role in almost eradicating polio. Sabin never took out a patent, and gave up any profit. He died in 1993. Cases of polio around the world have fallen by more than 99 percent since 1988, and the disease remains in only a few places.

# The cook who gave typhoid to dozens
@series history
@conf mixed
@src Mary Mallon, 1869 to 1938; George Soper, 1907

Mary Mallon was an Irish-born cook who worked in the homes of wealthy families in New York in the early 1900s. Between 1897 and 1907, members of four of the families she worked for fell ill with typhoid fever. In 1907 an investigator, George Soper, traced the cases to her. She was a healthy carrier, who gave off the bacteria without being sick herself.

She was the first person in the United States found to be an asymptomatic carrier of typhoid. She is believed to have infected up to 57 people, and three deaths were confirmed. She did not believe that she could be spreading the disease, and she resisted. In 1907 she was held by force at a hospital on North Brother Island, in New York's East River, and released in 1910 after promising not to work as a cook.

She took a job under a false name, and in 1915 there was an outbreak at a maternity hospital with 25 cases. She was returned to the island and lived there until her death in 1938. About 400 other carriers were found while she was confined, and most were not treated as she was. Her nickname has become a phrase for anyone who spreads disease. The case raises the question of when public health can limit a person's freedom.

# The merchant who brought printing to England
@series words
@src William Caxton, about 1422 to 1491; preface to Eneydos, 1490

William Caxton was an English merchant who lived much of his life in Bruges, in Flanders. On his business trips he saw the new printing trade in Cologne, and with a partner, Colard Mansion, he set up a press in Bruges. In 1471 he finished his own translation of a book of tales about Troy, which became one of the first books printed in English.

In 1476 he came back to England and set up a press at Westminster, near London. The first book that he is known to have printed there was Chaucer's Canterbury Tales. He went on to publish romances, histories, and the first English translation of Aesop's Fables, in 1484. Caxton was not a skilled translator, and he worked in a hurry.

His business also forced him to make choices about English. The language varied from one region to another, and he wrote that a book that pleased one county might not please another. In a preface he told of merchants on a ship waiting for good weather on the Thames, and one asked a woman for eggs, and she said she spoke no French, until another said "eyren." Printers began to fix spellings, and a standard form of English started to take shape.

# The printer who invented the paperback
@series words
@src Aldus Manutius, Venice, about 1449 to 1515

Aldus Manutius was an Italian scholar who became a printer in Venice. His press, the Aldine Press, was known for its carefully made editions. He wanted to give readers the works of ancient Greece and Rome in their original languages, and he was the first to print many of the Greek classics, such as Aristotle and Aristophanes.

Until then large books were the rule, meant to sit on a desk. Aldus brought out small books, in a size that could fit in a pocket or saddlebag, called enchiridia. They were the ancestors of the paperback. He also had a new typeface cut, based on the slanting handwriting of scholars of his day. It was the first known ancestor of italic type, and he used it to fit more words on a small page.

He also helped to set the use of punctuation marks, such as the comma and the semicolon. His reputation for accuracy was such that Erasmus came to Venice to have his work printed by him. Other printers copied his innovations, and pirated his editions, in spite of his efforts to stop them. Much of what a modern book looks like was set in his workshop.
