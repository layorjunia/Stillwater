# The cliff that was staged for the camera
@series history
@src Disney, White Wilderness, 1958; accounts of its making

Many people believe that lemmings, small rodents of the Arctic, throw themselves off cliffs in a mass suicide. The idea was spread by a 1958 Disney nature film, White Wilderness. It showed a crowd of lemmings plunging into the sea. In fact the animals in the scene were pushed off the cliff by the filmmakers, to create the illusion.

The belief is older than the film. It goes back at least to the late nineteenth century, though where it started is uncertain. Lemmings do move around in large numbers when their population is high, and some drown when they try to cross water. But they are looking for food and space, and they are not trying to die.

The phrase has entered everyday speech, and people say that a crowd rushing to a bad decision is like lemmings going over a cliff. It is a good example of a myth that a single powerful film can fix in the public mind. The scene that taught millions of people about animal behavior was made up.

# The pen that did not cost millions
@series history
@src Fisher Pen Company; NASA purchases, 1960s

A well-known story says that NASA spent millions of dollars to develop a pen that would write in space, while the Soviets simply used pencils. It is a good joke about waste, but it is not true. The pen was developed on his own by Paul C. Fisher, who ran the Fisher Pen Company, and he spent about a million dollars of his own money on it.

Pencils were not a good choice in a spacecraft. Their shavings and bits of graphite can float about, and are a hazard around instruments and in the air that the crew breathes. NASA tested the Fisher pen, which uses pressurized ink and writes upside down and in extreme heat and cold, and approved it. It bought 400 pens at about six dollars each.

The Soviet Union later bought the same pens for its Soyuz flights. A related myth says that NASA invented Tang, Velcro and Teflon. It did not, though many other products, such as memory foam and space blankets, did come from space research. The story survives because it is about a simple answer to an expensive problem, but the reality was that the engineers were right.

# Why the far side of the Moon is not dark
@series sci
@src Luna 3, 1959; tidal locking

The Moon always shows the same face to the Earth, because it turns once on its axis in exactly the time that it takes to go around us once. It is called tidal locking. Because of it, people long thought of the other side as hidden and mysterious, and called it the dark side of the Moon.

But it is not dark. As the Moon travels around the Earth, the sun shines on every part of it. The far side gets about as much sunlight as the near side, and has a day and a night just as the near side does. At new moon, when we see nothing, the far side is fully lit.

Nobody on Earth had seen it until 1959, when the Soviet probe Luna 3 took the first photographs. The word dark refers to the fact that it was unknown, in the sense of the old phrase dark continent, and not to a lack of light. That is why the better name is far side. Astronomers also found it looks different from the near side, with far fewer of the dark plains.

# The frog that jumps out, and the one that says ribbit
@series history
@src Studies of frog thermal tolerance; Pacific tree frog

A famous story says that if you put a frog in a pot of cold water and heat it slowly, the frog will not notice, and will be boiled to death. It is used as a warning about change that creeps up on people. It is not true of frogs. If a frog is dropped into boiling water, it dies at once. If it is in water that is slowly heated, it tries to escape long before it reaches the temperature at which it would be harmed.

The saying is a metaphor, and it can still be used as one. But the frog is not as foolish as it suggests. Frogs, like other animals, react to discomfort, and they get out.

Another mistaken idea about frogs comes from the movies. The classic ribbit sound is made by only a few species, mainly the Pacific tree frog and the Baja California chorus frog. It spread because of its heavy use in films shot in Hollywood, where those frogs live, and soon many viewers thought that all frogs sound like that. Frogs around the world call in very different ways.

# Four fish with unfair reputations
@series sci
@src Goldfish memory studies; piranha and pacu reports; candiru case report, 1997

The memory of a goldfish is said to last three seconds. It does not. A goldfish can remember for up to a few months. That is why people can train them to expect food at certain times and to respond to signals.

Piranhas are famous as killers. In fact they eat many things, plants as well as meat. They swim in schools mainly to protect themselves from predators and not to attack. Attacks on humans are very rare, and happen when they are under stress and feel threatened, and then the bites are usually on hands and feet.

A relative, the pacu, was falsely said to bite off men's testicles. The story began in 2013, when a report about a pacu found in the strait between Sweden and Denmark was changed by a joke into a claim that it ate nuts. The candiru, a parasitic catfish of the Amazon, is said to swim up a person's urethra. The only documented case, written up in 1997, was heavily criticized by other scientists, and the story is now largely regarded as a myth.

# The pink sweat of the hippopotamus
@series sci
@src Hippopotamus skin secretions; hipposudoric acid

Hippos are said to sweat blood and to give pink milk. They do neither. The skin of a hippo gives off a reddish fluid, and it can look alarming. It is not sweat and it is not blood. The color comes from a red pigment called hipposudoric acid.

The pigment seems to act as a natural sunscreen, protecting the hippo's skin in the strong sun. Hippos spend much of the day in water, but their skin is thin and can crack and burn when it dries. The secretion forms a film over the skin, which helps to protect it.

The milk is not pink, either. It is white or a pale beige. The idea that a hippo's milk could be pink seems to come from the mistaken view of the red secretion as blood. The animal is a good example of the kind of misunderstanding that takes hold, when a very odd-looking natural thing is given a dramatic explanation.

# Porcupines and skunks do not do what the stories say
@series sci
@src Porcupine anatomy; skunk odor treatments

A porcupine cannot shoot its quills. The quills are loose, and they come out easily when they touch an attacker. A porcupine defends itself by turning its back and reversing into the enemy, so that the quills stick in. They do not fly through the air, though the stories say so.

Another idea concerns skunks. Many people think that a bath in tomato juice cures a dog that has been sprayed. It does not. After a time, a person who has been sprayed stops noticing the smell, because of olfactory fatigue, the nose's tendency to tune out a smell that continues. Tomato juice seems to work because the smell has faded, and not because the juice has changed it.

Treatments that really work are chemical, and break down the compounds in the spray. People should not rely on a household remedy because it seems to work. Both myths are about the same mistake, which is believing that an animal weapon does something more dramatic than it does, or that a remedy works because its use was followed by relief.

# Three more animal tales that are not true
@series sci
@src Rabbit diet; catnip response; elephant behavior

Rabbits do not live on carrots. In the wild they eat grasses, clovers and dark green plants. Carrots are rich in sugar, and a rabbit that eats a great many of them is likely to be unhealthy. They are a treat, and not a main meal.

Catnip does not affect every cat. Only about two thirds of cats respond to it. Whether a cat does so is inherited. Other plants, such as valerian root, can have a similar effect on cats that are not interested in catnip.

Old elephants do not leave the herd and go to a special graveyard to die. The tale has been told for a long time, and has appeared in adventure stories, but there is no evidence for it. What is true is that elephants show strong interest in the bones of dead elephants, touching and turning them, and this behavior may have helped to feed the legend. They are social animals, and they mourn in their own way.

# The eagle that sounds like a hawk
@series sci
@src Bald eagle vocalizations; red-tailed hawk

When a bald eagle appears in a film or a television advertisement, it is often accompanied by a loud and fierce scream. The sound is real, but it does not belong to the eagle. It is the cry of a red-tailed hawk, which is used in the sound track because it fits our idea of what an eagle should sound like.

The real call of the bald eagle is much softer and higher. It is a series of weak, chirping notes, and it resembles the calls of gulls more than anything else. To many people it is a surprise, and even a disappointment, for a bird that is the national symbol of the United States.

The mix-up shows how much of what we think we know about animals comes from how they are shown in the media, and how little from the animals. A sound that is used often enough becomes the right one in people's minds. Even those who have seen real eagles are often startled by the sound that they make.

# The sixty starlings and the Shakespeare myth
@series history
@src Eugene Schieffelin, 1890; Edwin Way Teale, 1948

In 1890 a New York drug manufacturer named Eugene Schieffelin released about sixty European starlings in Central Park. The birds thrived and spread. Starlings are now one of the most numerous birds in North America, and they are considered a pest by many farmers.

A popular story says that Schieffelin wanted to bring to America every kind of bird that is mentioned in the works of William Shakespeare, because he was a fan of the playwright, and that the starling, mentioned once in the play Henry IV, was one. It is a good story, but there is no evidence that it is true.

The claim has been traced to an essay in 1948 by the naturalist Edwin Way Teale. His notes appear to show that it was a speculation of his own, and not a fact. Acclimatization societies of the time did bring many species from Europe, for reasons of sentiment and also of use. The Shakespeare tale is an example of an attractive explanation that outlived its source. Starlings did come from the Old World, and they did come in 1890.

# Why a meteor glows, and why an egg stands up
@series sci
@src Atmospheric entry physics; egg balancing

When a meteor or a spacecraft hits the upper air, it glows and gets very hot. Many people say that this is caused by friction with the air. It is not the main cause. The air in front of an object moving at several kilometers a second cannot get out of the way, and is squeezed, so that it is compressed very fast. When air is compressed in this way, it gets very hot, and it is that heat that glows and warms the object.

This is called adiabatic compression, and it is the same thing that heats the air in a bicycle pump. At high speed it is so intense that spacecraft returning from orbit need heat shields to survive.

A similar idea is that you can only balance an egg on its end at the time of the spring equinox, when day and night are equal. It is false. With patience, an egg can be balanced on any day of the year, because the tiny bumps on the shell give it several points of contact. The belief is a reminder that when a feat is said to depend on the stars, it is a good idea to try it on a different date.

# Two things most people believe about sharks
@series sci
@src Lane and Comac, Sharks Don't Get Cancer, 1992; great white shark research

In 1992 a book called Sharks Don't Get Cancer was published. It claimed that sharks are free from the disease, and that eating the cartilage that makes their skeletons could prevent cancer in people. It sold widely, and extracts of shark cartilage were sold as a supplement. The first claim is wrong. Cancers have been found in sharks, and the data do not support any conclusions about how often they get tumors.

Another belief is that great white sharks attack surfers and divers because they mistake them for seals. The pattern of attacks suggests otherwise. When a great white attacks a seal, it rises fast and strikes with great violence. When it attacks a human, it usually approaches at a normal speed, bites, and swims away. Great whites have good eyesight and color vision.

The researchers who study this think that the bite on a human is a test, a way of finding out what an unfamiliar object is. A human is not food that a shark wants, and a bite is often enough to injure badly, though many people survive. The sharks of the movies are not what the evidence shows.

# Snakes that do not unhinge, and do not dance
@series sci
@src Snake skull anatomy; snake hearing research

Snakes can swallow things much larger than their heads. Many people say that this is because they can unhinge their jaws. They cannot. The jaw bones are connected to the skull by a movable bone, called the quadrate, which lets the mouth open very wide. At the front, the two halves of the lower jaw are joined by a flexible ligament, so that they can spread apart.

Snakes are not deaf, as is often said. They can hear sounds carried in the air, though they do not hear high pitches as well as we do. They can also feel vibrations through the ground.

Snake charmers play a flute, and a cobra seems to sway to the music. It is not responding to the tune. Snakes cannot hear music in the way that humans do. The snake follows the movement of the charmer's swaying instrument, which it sees as a threat that it must watch. The music is for the audience. The snake is following the motion, and it is ready to strike.

# The bumblebee that was said to be unable to fly
@series sci
@src Bumblebee flight myth, 1934; pollination data

There is a story that scientists have proved that a bumblebee cannot fly. The physics, it says, shows that its wings are too small for its body. It is told as a lesson about believing in yourself. The truth is that no such proof exists. The physics of insect flight is quite well understood. The story seems to come from a calculation that treated the bee as a fixed-wing aircraft, mentioned in a 1934 book. It was repeated in a film, Bee Movie, in 2007.

Another claim is that if bees died out, humanity would starve within four years. Honey bees are important for the pollination of many plants. But the main staple crops of the world, wheat, maize, rice, soybeans and sorghum, are pollinated by the wind or by themselves. Only a little over ten percent of the human plant diet depends on insect pollination.

A third idea is that a bee dies after stinging. That happens in only a small minority of species, including the honey bee when it stings a mammal. Most bees survive a sting, and a honey bee that stings another insect does too. It would be a mistake to ignore the decline of bees, but not for these reasons.

# Cockroaches, earwigs and houseflies
@series sci
@src Radiation resistance research; insect life spans

It is said that after a nuclear war, only cockroaches would survive. Cockroaches are more resistant to radiation than humans, but they are not immune to radiation sickness. They are also not unusually tough compared with other insects. Parasitic wasps, tardigrades, and some bacteria, such as Deinococcus radiodurans, can survive much more.

Earwigs are said to creep into the ears of sleeping people and lay eggs. There are a few stories of earwigs found in ears, but the insects are not known to do it on purpose. Their name may come from the shape of their hind wings, which look like a human ear when unfolded.

Houseflies live for about 20 to 30 days, and not 24 hours as people say. The insect with a very short adult life is a different one. Females of an American mayfly, Dolania americana, live as adults for as little as five minutes. These are not true flies, and their adult stage exists only for mating and laying eggs. The myths show how an interesting figure can spread beyond what the facts support.

# What the spiders and other crawlers are not
@series sci
@src Arachnid and insect myths; Pholcidae venom

Some people say that you swallow eight spiders a year in your sleep. It is not true. A sleeping person makes breathing and heartbeat sounds that warn off spiders, and most people wake up if a spider crawls on their face.

The daddy longlegs spider is said to be the most venomous spider in the world, but with fangs too short to bite. In truth its fangs can pierce human skin, and the venom is so slight that it causes only a mild burning for a few seconds. The name is also given to harvestmen and crane flies, which are not spiders at all.

Female praying mantises do not always eat the male while mating. It happens sometimes, and it is more often seen in captivity. Termites, often called white ants, are not ants. They are highly specialized cockroaches. Ticks do not jump or fall from trees onto people. They wait on grass and bushes, holding on, until a host passes, and they find it by smell, heat or the carbon dioxide in a breath. Not all earthworms become two worms when cut in half. Only a few species can regrow a head.

# What to do about a jellyfish sting
@series sci
@src Jellyfish sting treatment studies; giant clam biology

A common piece of advice is to urinate on a jellyfish sting. It is an old tale, and it does not help. Urine may make the pain worse. It can cause the stinging cells that have not yet fired to release more of their venom. The best immediate treatment is to rinse the area with salt water. For some kinds of jellyfish, vinegar helps too.

Another myth about the sea is that the giant clam is a man-eater. According to stories, a diver who puts a foot between its shells will be caught and drowned. The giant clam is not aggressive toward people, and cannot snap a limb. Its shells close very slowly, and some specimens cannot close all the way.

Both stories are about a creature that is thought to be a trap for the unwary. The facts are less dramatic. The sting of a jellyfish is a real hazard, and some, like the box jellyfish, can be deadly, so correct first aid matters. The giant clam is harmless to people. What these cases show is that a good story can last longer than any evidence for it.

# Mushrooms are not plants, and other things about plants
@series sci
@src Fungi classification; poinsettia toxicity; sunflower heliotropism

Mushrooms, molds and other fungi are not plants. For a long time they were classed with plants, since they stand in one place and have some features in common. But modern genetic evidence shows that fungi are more closely related to animals than they are to plants. They are still often covered in botany classes.

Poinsettias, the red Christmas plants, are said to be deadly poison to children and cats. They are not. They can irritate the skin or the stomach, and if eaten they may cause diarrhea and vomiting, but they rarely cause serious illness.

Sunflowers are said to follow the Sun across the sky. Young plants do this, a movement called heliotropism. Once the plant has flowered, the head stays fixed in one direction, often east, and does not track the sun. A grown sunflower greets the morning sun, which warms it faster, and that may help attract bees. It does not turn through the day, as so many people believe.

# What the word theory means in science
@series think
@src National Academy of Sciences usage; evolution education research

When people say, it's only a theory, they mean that it is a guess. In science the word has a different meaning. A scientific theory is a set of principles that explains something in the natural world, and has been tested many times. Evolution is a theory in the same sense as the germ theory of disease or the theory of gravity. A hypothesis is closer to what we mean by a guess.

A second confusion is about what the theory covers. The theory of evolution deals with how living things change over generations after life has begun. It does not try to explain how life itself began. That is the subject of a different field, called abiogenesis. It also does not explain the origin of the universe, which is the business of cosmology and the Big Bang model.

The confusion arises because a word has two meanings, and because the theories are often discussed together. A fact and a theory are not opposites. Facts are observations, and a theory is the explanation that fits them. The theory of evolution would be a better-known idea if the words were used with care.

# Survival of the fittest does not mean the strongest
@series think
@src Evolutionary biology; Herbert Spencer's phrase

The phrase survival of the fittest suggests a contest of strength, with the biggest and most powerful animal winning. But in biology, fitness has a different meaning. It means reproductive success, how many offspring an individual leaves that themselves survive and reproduce. A small, quiet animal that raises many young can be fitter than a strong one that has few.

Evolution also has no goal. It does not plan to improve an animal. Biologists often speak as if it does, and say that dinosaurs evolved feathers for courtship. That is shorthand. The fuller version is that feathers spread when they gave an advantage to the animals that had them. Students who read the shorthand literally often think that evolution has a purpose.

Nor is evolution a ladder from lower to higher forms, and it does not always lead to more complexity. A population may become simpler. A parasite may lose genes that it no longer needs. Natural selection only makes later generations better suited to their environment than earlier ones. Fitness is always measured against the place and the time where an animal lives.

# Who is related to whom
@series sci
@src Phylogeny of synapsids and diapsids; human and chimpanzee ancestry

People often say that humans evolved from chimpanzees. We did not. Humans and chimpanzees share a common ancestor, which lived about five to eight million years ago. The two lines then went their separate ways, and both have changed since. Chimpanzees are our cousins, and not our ancestors.

A similar mistake is the idea that mammals evolved from the reptiles of today. They did not. The first animals that were fully adapted to land split into two lines, about 320 million years ago, in the Carboniferous period. One line, the synapsids, led to mammals, and the other, the diapsids, led to the lizards, snakes, crocodiles, dinosaurs and birds. The mammals are the only survivors of the synapsid line.

It is also true that humans are animals, though in daily speech we use the word animal as the opposite of human. Biologically we are a kind of ape. The family tree is a branching one, and every living species is at the tip of its own branch.

# Are mutations the same everywhere in a genome?
@series sci
@conf mixed
@src Monroe et al., Nature, 2022; DNA repair research

A standard teaching is that mutations are random. It is true that they are random with respect to what the organism needs, since they do not arise because they would help. But they are not equally likely everywhere in the genome. Some regions are more likely to change than others, depending on how well the cell's repair machinery works there, and on other biases in the process.

In a 2022 study of a small plant, Arabidopsis thaliana, researchers found that genes in important regions of the genome were protected from mutation better than other parts. They reported that mutations were biased in a way that benefits the plant, and that beneficial changes were more likely than expected in some places.

The result was a surprise, and it has been debated. Other scientists argued that the pattern might have other explanations, and the claim that mutation is directed to benefit is not accepted by most biologists. What is certain is that mutation rates vary across the genome. The original idea of random change has been refined, and it is still a matter of active research.

# The dinosaurs that never went away
@series sci
@src Cretaceous-Paleogene extinction; origin of birds

The dinosaurs are often given as an example of a species that was too big and too slow to survive. It is not what the evidence shows. The non-bird dinosaurs were widespread and successful for well over a hundred million years, and they were wiped out by a sudden catastrophe, the asteroid impact and its aftermath, about 66 million years ago, and not by being poorly adapted.

Moreover, not all the dinosaurs died. Birds are classified within a group of dinosaurs, the theropods. The question of where birds came from was open for over a century, but there is now a clear consensus that they evolved from small feathered theropods in the Jurassic period. Many lines of dinosaurs ended at the close of the Cretaceous, but one did not.

More than 11,000 species of birds are alive today. When we say that dinosaurs are extinct, we usually mean the non-avian ones. A sparrow at a bird feeder is a dinosaur, in the sense that biologists use. The old textbook picture of a failed group is a good example of how a story can persist after the science has moved on.

# Five famous prehistoric animals that were not dinosaurs
@series sci
@src Paleontological classification; Mesozoic and Permian reptiles

Many of the best-known prehistoric animals are not dinosaurs. The mosasaurs, the great sea reptiles, were actually giant lizards. The ichthyosaurs, which looked like dolphins, and the plesiosaurs, with their long necks and flippers, were reptiles that were even more distantly related to the dinosaurs. None of the animals that are known to have been fully marine was a dinosaur, though a few dinosaurs, such as Spinosaurus and the penguins, spent much of their time in water.

The pterosaurs, called pterodactyls in popular speech, were flying reptiles. They were close relatives of the dinosaurs, but a dinosaur is defined as a descendant of the last common ancestor of two groups, and pterosaurs fall outside it.

And Dimetrodon, the animal with the great sail on its back, is often shown with dinosaurs. It died out some 40 million years before the first dinosaurs appeared. It was a synapsid, a member of the line that led to mammals, and it was more closely related to us than to any dinosaur. When you see a toy dinosaur in a set, it is worth asking which of the animals really are.

# Dinosaurs, people and oil
@series sci
@src Cretaceous-Paleogene extinction, 66 million years ago; origin of fossil fuels

Humans and the non-bird dinosaurs never lived at the same time. The last of the large dinosaurs died out 66 million years ago in the mass extinction at the end of the Cretaceous period. The earliest members of our genus, Homo, appeared only about 2.3 to 2.4 million years ago, and our own species much later. Cartoons showing cave people with dinosaurs are fiction.

Many people also believe that oil and gas are made from the bodies of dinosaurs. They are not. Petroleum comes mostly from tiny algae and animals called zooplankton that died and sank to the sea floor, where there was no oxygen to break them down, and were buried. Over millions of years, heat and pressure turned them into oil. Coal comes from plants that were buried and fossilized, mostly in great swamps.

Only a very small part of the world's coal contains fossils of dinosaurs. The idea is so widespread that one oil company once had a dinosaur as its logo. The reality is both less dramatic and more interesting. Most of the fuel that powers the modern world is the remains of microscopic sea life and ancient plants.

# Were the dinosaurs cold-blooded?
@series sci
@conf mixed
@src Dinosaur thermoregulation research; pterosaur and ichthyosaur studies

The old picture of the dinosaurs was of slow, sluggish reptiles that warmed up in the sun like a modern lizard. That view has changed. How dinosaurs controlled their body temperature is still an active topic of study, but the evidence makes a simple cold-blooded picture hard to accept.

Dinosaurs grew very fast, as shown by the structure of their bones, and growth at that rate needs a lot of energy. Their fossils have been found in polar regions, where winters were cold and dark, and cold-blooded animals would struggle. The pterosaurs had a hair-like covering on their bodies, and it is inferred that they were warm-blooded, since no modern cold-blooded animal has one. Estimates of the body temperature of ichthyosaurs are similar to those of marine mammals in all climates.

The truth is probably more complex than either warm or cold. Different dinosaurs may have had different ways of regulating their temperature, and some may have been in between. Birds, the living dinosaurs, are fully warm-blooded. The question reminds us that a fossil can tell us a lot about how an animal lived, but never the whole story.
