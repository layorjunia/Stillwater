# How a great scholar's name became an insult
@src Richard Stanyhurst; Oxford English Dictionary; history of the dunce cap

The word dunce means a person who is slow to learn. It began as the name of a great thinker. John Duns Scotus was a Scottish theologian and philosopher of the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries, and for a long time his works were studied with respect. Writers in the sixteenth century, such as Richard Stanyhurst, explained dunce as coming from his name.

In the 1500s the followers of Scotus, called Scotists, argued against the new learning of the Renaissance humanists. The humanists and reformers mocked them. The terms Dunsmen, duns and dunce became insults, and meant a person who was incapable of scholarship. The Oxford English Dictionary records the word in the mid-sixteenth century, for a follower of Duns Scotus, a pedant, and a fool.

The dunce cap, the tall pointed hat that was placed on slow pupils, has a similar story. Scotus is said to have believed that a cone-shaped cap would funnel knowledge into the brain, and for centuries such a hat was a sign of an intelligent man. The first picture of the punishment hat is in a 1727 edition of a book. A scholar's name, and his hat, became the signs of a fool.

# The heat that Pasteur did not invent
@series history
@src Louis Pasteur, 1860s; Lazzaro Spallanzani, 1768; Tamonin-nikki

Pasteurization is named for Louis Pasteur, the French scientist who showed in the 1860s that gentle heating could kill the unwanted microbes in wine and keep it from spoiling. The process was later applied to milk and to fruit juices. It heats a food to a temperature below boiling, usually below 100 degrees Celsius, to destroy the germs that cause disease and decay, though most bacterial spores survive.

But the idea of heating drinks to preserve them was older than Pasteur. People in China are known to have heated wine for preservation by 1117, and the diary of a series of Japanese monks, the Tamonin-nikki, written between 1478 and 1618, describes the practice. In 1768 the Italian priest and scientist Lazzaro Spallanzani showed that a broth could be made sterile by heating.

What Pasteur added was an explanation. He showed that microbes were the cause of spoilage, and that a mild heat could remove them without ruining the wine. Today the process is used in the dairy industry and many others, and newer methods, such as high pressure and pulses of electricity, are used to pasteurize without heat. A word that bears one man's name records a practice that many others had used.

# From pirates to long speeches
@series words
@src Oxford English Dictionary; history of the Senate filibuster

In a legislature, a filibuster is the use of long speeches and other delaying tactics to prevent a vote on a bill. It is called talking a bill to death. The word has an odd history, because it began as a name for pirates. It comes ultimately from the Dutch word vrijbuiter, which means a freebooter, a pillaging adventurer. The Oxford English Dictionary finds its only known early use in a 1587 book that described flibutors who robbed supply convoys.

In the late eighteenth century the word came back into English through the French flibustier. In the early 1850s it was borrowed again, from the Spanish filibustero, meaning a lawless plunderer. It was applied to adventurers such as William Walker, an American who led private military expeditions in Latin America.

Over the middle and late nineteenth century it came to be used in American English for obstructing progress in a legislative body. The first known practitioner of the tactic was in ancient Rome. He was the senator Cato the Younger, who spoke at length to block a vote. A word for raiding ships came to mean raiding the time of a debate.

# The tomb that gave us a word
@series history
@src Mausoleum at Halicarnassus, 353 to 351 BC; Antipater of Sidon

The Mausoleum at Halicarnassus was a tomb built between 353 and 351 BC in the city of Halicarnassus, now Bodrum in Turkey. It was built for Mausolus, a local ruler in the Persian Empire, and his sister and wife, Artemisia II. The architects were two Greeks, Satyros of Paros and Pythius of Priene.

It was about 45 meters, or 148 feet, high, and its four sides were decorated with carved reliefs, each by a different famous Greek sculptor. In all it held some 400 statues. It was thought so beautiful that Antipater of Sidon included it among his list of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World.

The building stood for about 1,600 years, and was brought down by earthquakes between the twelfth and fifteenth centuries. It was the last of the six lost wonders to fall. The name of the tomb is still with us, since any grand tomb above ground is now called a mausoleum. The man who ruled for a quarter of a century is remembered mainly for a grave.

# The youth who loved his reflection
@series words
@src Ovid, Metamorphoses; Greek myth of Narcissus and Nemesis

In Greek mythology, Narcissus was a handsome young man who rejected everyone who loved him, including the nymph Echo. In the best known version, told by the Roman poet Ovid, the goddess Nemesis made him fall in love with his own reflection in a pool. He stayed by the water, unable to look away, until he died. In the place where he died, a flower grew, which is called the narcissus.

The story was popular. It appears in nearly fifty wall paintings at Pompeii, and in mosaics and on carved tombs. The name of the youth gave us the word narcissism, which now means an excessive love of oneself.

The goddess who punished him has also given us a word. Nemesis in Greek means distribution, from a verb meaning to give what is due. She was the goddess of retribution for hubris, which means arrogance before the gods. She was at first a distributor of fortune, good or bad, in due proportion to what each person deserved. In English, a nemesis is now an enemy who seems fated to bring us down. The two words are linked in a single story about the price of vanity.

# The acrobat who gave us a garment
@series history
@src Jules Léotard, 1838 to 1870

Jules Léotard was born in Toulouse, France, in 1838. His father ran a swimming pool and taught gymnastics, and the young man practiced over the water. He studied law and passed his exams, but at 18 he began to try out trapeze bars, ropes and rings suspended over the pool, and then joined a circus in Paris, the Cirque Napoléon.

On November 12, 1859, he performed the first flying trapeze routine, swinging from one bar to another, on three bars. In 1861 he appeared in London, at the Alhambra music hall. His act did not stress the danger. It was admired for its ease and grace, and it inspired wonder. A song of 1867, The Daring Young Man on the Flying Trapeze, was based on him.

He also designed a costume, a one-piece knitted garment that was tight, so that it would not catch on the equipment, and would let him move freely. It showed off his body, and it took his name. The leotard is worn by gymnasts and dancers. Léotard died of an illness in 1870, at the age of 32, but his name lives on in the clothing.

# The road builder who gave us two words
@series words
@src John Loudon McAdam, 1756 to 1836

John Loudon McAdam was a Scottish engineer who was born in Ayr in 1756. At fourteen he moved to New York, where he made his fortune as a merchant, and he returned to Scotland in 1783. He later became interested in roads, which in those days were rough tracks that turned to mud.

He invented a way of building a road with a smooth, hard surface. It used carefully chosen stones of mixed sizes, packed in a set structure, so that the road was firm, durable and not muddy. His method was called macadamisation, and a road built in this way was a macadam road. It spread across Britain and the rest of the world.

Later a major improvement was made. A road builder added tar, first coal tar, to bind the stones of the surface together. The product was called tar macadam, shortened to tarmac. A name that is used at airports for a paved surface is thus a short form of the name of a Scottish road builder. His family name, which had been McGregor, had been changed to McAdam in the reign of James VI for political reasons.

# Who did and did not invent the flush toilet
@series history
@src Sir John Harington; Alexander Cumming, 1775; Thomas Crapper

Thomas Crapper is often said to have invented the flush toilet. He did not. A forerunner of the modern flush toilet was built by an Elizabethan courtier, Sir John Harington, in the sixteenth century. In 1775 a Scottish mechanic, Alexander Cumming, designed and patented a toilet with an S-trap, the bent pipe that keeps sewer gas from coming back, and with a flushing mechanism.

Crapper was a London plumber of the late nineteenth century. He did much to make the flush toilet popular, and held nine patents, including one for the floating ballcock, which shuts off the water as the tank fills. He ran a successful business and put his name on his products.

The word crap is not related to his name, though the coincidence has helped the myth. Many inventions are named for a person who improved or promoted the device, and not the first to think of it. Harington, Cumming and Crapper each added something, over three centuries. The history of the toilet is a good case of how an invention is the work of many hands.

# What Al Gore said about the internet
@series tech
@src Al Gore, interview, March 1999; High Performance Computing Act, 1991

Al Gore is often quoted as having claimed that he invented the internet. He never said that. In an interview in 1999 he said that during his service in the United States Congress, he took the initiative in creating the Internet. It was a clumsy phrase, and it was widely mocked, but it referred to his work in politics.

As a senator, he was the original drafter of the High Performance Computing and Communication Act of 1991. It gave large funding to supercomputing centers. That led to upgrades of a major part of the network, which was already in existence in the early 1990s, called NSFNet. It also helped the development of a program called NCSA Mosaic, a browser that made the World Wide Web popular.

The people who invented the underlying technology were others. Vint Cerf and Robert Kahn designed the protocols that allow networks to talk to each other, and both of them have said that Gore was a leader in the policy that helped it to grow. The truth is that he did not invent it, and did not claim to. He was one of the politicians who understood early what it could become.

# The mass that does not increase
@series sci
@src Special relativity; invariant mass; Einstein's equation

It is often said that as an object approaches the speed of light, its mass increases without limit, and that this is why nothing can go faster. The idea is a common way of describing relativity. But modern physicists mostly avoid it. In special relativity, mass has two meanings. The invariant mass, also called rest mass, is the same for all observers in all frames of reference.

What grows with speed is the energy and the momentum of a body. The relativistic mass, which depends on the observer's speed, is just another way of talking about its total energy. Writers on the subject now prefer to speak of the energy. The equation that is famous, E equals mc squared, is in full written with a factor, called gamma, that depends on speed. For an object at rest, gamma is one, and the familiar form results.

The old way of speaking has some truth in it. The inertia of an object, its resistance to being pushed, does grow as it speeds up, and a particle of light has no rest mass but adds to the inertia of a system that contains it. But saying that mass increases can give a wrong picture, in which the object itself changes. The better statement is that its energy and momentum grow.

# Can you sink in quicksand?
@series sci
@src Physics of quicksand; soil liquefaction

In adventure films, a person who steps into quicksand is dragged under, and disappears. It is a staple of fiction. In real life a person cannot be completely submerged in quicksand. It is a mixture of fine sand, silt or clay and water. It forms in loose sand that is saturated with water, when the sand is suddenly disturbed, and the water cannot escape.

The sand then acts like a liquid, and cannot support weight. But a liquid has buoyancy. By Archimedes' principle, an object sinks only to the level at which its weight equals that of the mixture it pushes aside. Because the mixture is denser than water, and a human body is less dense than the mixture, a person floats, and sinks no further than the waist or chest.

Quicksand is also strange in another way. It is a fluid that thins when disturbed. A small change in the stress on it can cause a sudden drop in its thickness, and then, when a person struggles, the water and the sand separate, so that the sand packs tightly around the legs. That makes it hard to pull out. The real danger is of being trapped by the legs, especially near the shore, where a rising tide can drown a person who is stuck.
