# Houston, we've had a problem
@src NASA, Apollo 13 Review Board Report, 1970

On April 13, 1970, two days into the Apollo 13 mission to the Moon, an oxygen tank exploded in the service module. The astronauts, Jim Lovell, Jack Swigert and Fred Haise, were about two hundred thousand miles from Earth. The explosion crippled the electrical system, and the crew had to use the lunar module, designed for two men for two days, as a lifeboat for three people for four days.

The air in the lunar module started to fill with carbon dioxide, since the filters were the wrong shape for the command module's equipment. Engineers on the ground, working against the clock, designed an adapter using only the items that were on board, such as plastic bags, cardboard and duct tape. The crew built it by following instructions read out over the radio. They splashed down safely on April 17. The mission is often called a successful failure.

# The ice water and the O-rings
@src Rogers Commission Report, 1986; Feynman, What Do You Care What Other People Think?, 1988

On January 28, 1986, the space shuttle Challenger broke apart seventy-three seconds after launch, killing all seven crew. The weather had been unusually cold. A presidential commission was set up to find the cause. One of its members was the physicist Richard Feynman.

Feynman learned from engineers that the rubber O-rings that seal the joints in the solid rocket boosters might lose their flexibility in the cold. At a televised hearing on February 11, he clamped a piece of the rubber in a small clamp, dropped it in a glass of ice water, and then pulled it out. It didn't spring back. The commission concluded that the O-ring failure was the immediate cause, and that managers had ignored warnings. Feynman wrote that nature cannot be fooled.

# The telescope that needed glasses
@src Allen Report, NASA, 1990; Chaisson, The Hubble Wars, 1994

The Hubble Space Telescope was launched in April 1990 after years of delays, and at a cost of about a billion and a half dollars. When the first images came back, scientists saw that something was wrong. The pictures were blurry. The main mirror, which is eight feet across, had been ground to the wrong shape. It was too flat at the edge by about one fiftieth the width of a human hair.

The error had been caused by a flaw in a test instrument that had been assembled incorrectly. In December 1993 astronauts on the space shuttle Endeavour, over five days, installed a set of corrective mirrors, in effect, glasses for the telescope. After that, Hubble produced sharp pictures, and went on to make some of the greatest discoveries in astronomy. It has been serviced several times, and was still in use more than thirty years after launch.

# Lindbergh crosses the Atlantic
@src Lindbergh, The Spirit of St. Louis, 1953

In 1919 a New York hotel owner, Raymond Orteig, offered a prize of twenty-five thousand dollars to the first pilot to fly nonstop between New York and Paris. Several teams tried and failed, and some died in the attempt. In 1927 a twenty-five-year-old airmail pilot named Charles Lindbergh decided to try it alone, in a single-engine plane built for the purpose, the Spirit of St. Louis.

He took off from Long Island on the morning of May 20, 1927. He had no radio, and no parachute, and he stayed awake for the whole flight, using a periscope to see forward, as the tank blocked the view from the cockpit. After thirty-three and a half hours, he landed at Le Bourget airfield outside Paris, where about a hundred and fifty thousand people were waiting. He became an international hero overnight.

# The Dust Bowl
@src Worster, Dust Bowl: The Southern Plains in the 1930s, 1979; Egan, The Worst Hard Time, 2005

In the early twentieth century, farmers plowed up millions of acres of the grasslands of the American Great Plains to grow wheat. The deep-rooted native grass had held the soil in place. In the 1930s a severe drought set in, and the crops failed. With nothing to hold it, the dry topsoil blew away.

On April 14, 1935, which came to be known as Black Sunday, a huge dust storm swept across Oklahoma, Texas and Kansas, turning the afternoon to night. Dust drifted like snow, and it caused a lung disease called dust pneumonia. Hundreds of thousands of people left the region, many of them to California. In response, the government set up the Soil Conservation Service, and encouraged farmers to plant trees and to plow in ways that protected the soil. It's one of the great examples of an environmental disaster that was caused by human practices.

# Cutting through continents
@src McCullough, The Path Between the Seas, 1977

The Suez Canal in Egypt, opened in 1869, links the Mediterranean and the Red Sea. It saved ships about four thousand miles on a voyage from Britain to India, since they no longer had to sail around Africa. It was built by a French company led by Ferdinand de Lesseps, and it took ten years, using the labor of many thousands of Egyptians.

De Lesseps then tried to build a canal across Panama, and failed. The French effort began in 1881, and was ruined by disease, mostly yellow fever and malaria, and by landslides. More than twenty thousand workers died. The Americans took over in 1904. Doctor William Gorgas led a campaign to eliminate the mosquitoes that spread the diseases, which was the key to success. The Panama Canal opened in 1914. It cut the trip from New York to San Francisco by about eight thousand miles.

# The golden spike
@src Ambrose, Nothing Like It in the World, 2000

Before 1869, crossing the United States took months by wagon, or weeks by ship around South America. In the 1860s two companies built a railroad to link the East and West. The Central Pacific started in Sacramento, California, and built eastward, over the Sierra Nevada mountains. The Union Pacific started in Omaha, Nebraska, and built westward, across the plains.

Much of the labor was done by Chinese immigrants on the Central Pacific, and Irish immigrants and Civil War veterans on the Union Pacific. The two lines met on May 10, 1869, at Promontory Summit, Utah. A golden spike was driven into the final tie. The news was telegraphed across the country. A journey that took months could now be made in about a week. The railroad changed the nation's economy and opened the West to settlement, at a terrible cost to Native Americans.

# The first transatlantic cable
@src Gordon, A Thread Across the Ocean, 2002

Until the middle of the nineteenth century, news from Europe took about ten days to reach America, carried by ship. In 1858 an American businessman, Cyrus Field, led the effort to lay a telegraph cable on the floor of the Atlantic Ocean. It took several attempts, and two ships had to meet in mid-ocean to splice two halves of the cable.

On August 16, 1858, Queen Victoria sent a message of ninety-eight words to President James Buchanan. It took sixteen hours to send, as the signal was weak. There were celebrations in New York, with fireworks, which set the city hall on fire. A few weeks later, the cable failed, probably because a technician had used too high a voltage. A durable cable was laid in 1866, and it worked. Messages now crossed the ocean in minutes.

# The Domesday survey
@src Domesday Book, 1086; Bates, William the Conqueror, 2016

In 1085, at Christmas, William the Conqueror, who had conquered England in 1066, decided to carry out a survey. He sent officials to every part of the kingdom, with the aim of finding out who owned what, how much land and how many animals and people there were, and how much tax should be due. The work was done in about a year.

The results were written in a book that was called Domesday, since there was no appeal from its judgments, as with the Last Judgment. It records about thirteen thousand places. It's a snapshot of eleventh-century England, and it's the earliest public record that survives. The Domesday Book is held at the National Archives in Kew. A survey on such a scale was very rare in medieval Europe. It suggests how thoroughly the Normans took over the country.

# The Dead Sea Scrolls
@src Vermes, The Complete Dead Sea Scrolls in English, 1997

In 1947 a young Bedouin shepherd, probably Muhammed edh-Dhib, was looking for a lost goat near the Dead Sea. He threw a stone into a cave, and heard the sound of breaking pottery. Inside were clay jars that contained ancient scrolls wrapped in linen. Over the next ten years, scrolls were found in eleven caves near the site of Qumran, in what is now the West Bank.

There were about nine hundred manuscripts, most of them in fragments. They include the oldest known copies of books of the Hebrew Bible, a thousand years older than any other. They date from about the third century BC to the first century AD. Some of the scrolls are on the rules and beliefs of a Jewish community, possibly the Essenes, who lived at Qumran. It was one of the great manuscript discoveries of the twentieth century.

# The Terracotta Army
@src Ledderose & Schlombs, Jenseits der Grossen Mauer, 1990; Portal, The First Emperor, 2007

In March 1974 some farmers digging a well near the city of Xi'an in China hit something hard. It was a clay head. Archaeologists came, and found the tomb complex of Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor of China, who died in 210 BC. He had ordered an army of clay soldiers to be made, to protect him in the afterlife.

Excavations have revealed thousands of life-size warriors, in battle formation, with horses and chariots. It's estimated that there are about eight thousand soldiers. Each of them has a different face, and they were originally painted in bright colors, which have flaked off. The emperor's own burial chamber has not been opened. Ancient historians wrote that it contains rivers of mercury, and soil tests have found high levels of the metal.

# Tutankhamun
@src Carter & Mace, The Tomb of Tut-ankh-Amen, 1923

On November 4, 1922, the English archaeologist Howard Carter, who had spent years searching the Valley of the Kings in Egypt, uncovered a step cut into the rock. It led to a doorway sealed with plaster. When it was opened, a few weeks later, in the presence of his sponsor, Lord Carnarvon, Carter peered in with a candle. Asked if he could see anything, he said, yes, wonderful things.

It was the tomb of Tutankhamun, a young king who died in about 1323 BC, at about eighteen or nineteen. It was almost untouched, and held thousands of objects, including a gold funeral mask, a throne and chariots. A story spread that a curse killed those who opened it, after Carnarvon died of an infection in 1923. But most of the people involved lived for many years. Carter himself lived until 1939.

# Easter Island
@src Hunt & Lipo, The Statues That Walked, 2011
@conf contested

Easter Island, or Rapa Nui, is a small island in the Pacific, more than two thousand miles from South America. It's famous for its nearly nine hundred huge stone statues, the moai. A popular story, told by Jared Diamond among others, is that the islanders cut down all of the island's trees to move the statues, destroyed their own environment, and collapsed into war and famine.

Archaeologists Terry Hunt and Carl Lipo have proposed a different account. They say that rats, brought by the first Polynesian settlers, ate the seeds of the palm trees and stopped them from regrowing. They also find that the islanders were skillful, and that they could walk the statues upright, with ropes, rather than dragging them on logs. And the population collapse may have been caused largely by diseases and slave raids brought by Europeans. The debate is still going on.
