# What each side wants, and what each side says
@src Fisher & Ury, Getting to Yes, 1981

When people negotiate, they usually start by stating positions. I want this price, or I want that border. Roger Fisher and William Ury, of the Harvard Negotiation Project, argued in their 1981 book, Getting to Yes, that positions are a poor place to begin. Behind each position there are interests, which are the reasons that the person wants it, and the same interest can often be met in more than one way.

Their main example was the dispute over the Sinai Peninsula in 1978, at Camp David. Israel held the Sinai, and Egypt demanded it back, so their positions seemed to clash. But Israel's interest was security, since it did not want Egyptian tanks on its border. Egypt's interest was sovereignty, since it wanted its own land back. The answer was that Egypt took sovereignty, while large areas were kept free of troops. Asking why makes it possible to find a deal that was not visible from the positions.

# The open window in the next room
@src Follett, Constructive Conflict, 1925

In January 1925 the American writer on management Mary Parker Follett gave a talk about how to deal with conflict. She said that there are three ways. One side can dominate, or both can compromise, or they can integrate, which means finding a solution that gives both what they truly want. She gave a small example, from a library, where one man wanted the window open, and another wanted it shut.

She did not suggest opening it halfway, which would please neither. She asked why each wanted what he wanted. One wanted fresh air, and the other did not want a draft blowing on him. So the librarian opened a window in the next room, which was empty. Both got what they wanted. Follett's example later appeared in Getting to Yes. A compromise splits the difference, and the conflict tends to return, but a solution that meets both needs makes the conflict go away.

# The negotiator who copied the other
@src Maddux, Mullen & Galinsky, J. Experimental Social Psychology, 2008

Psychologists have found that people like those who mimic them, in a small, unnoticed way, by copying their posture or gestures. In 2008 William Maddux, Elizabeth Mullen and Adam Galinsky asked whether it helps in negotiation. In one study, they asked some business school students to copy, in a subtle way, the posture and movements of the person across the table. Others were not asked to mimic.

The mimickers did better. In a negotiation over a job offer, they created more value, and took a larger share of it, though not at the expense of the other side. In a second study, about a gas station for sale where the two sides seemed to be at an impasse, pairs in which one person mimicked were more likely to reach a deal at all. The authors found that trust explained the effect. People who feel mirrored feel understood.

# Five ways of handling a quarrel
@conf mixed
@src Thomas & Kilmann, 1974

In 1974 the psychologists Kenneth Thomas and Ralph Kilmann devised a questionnaire about how people behave in conflict. They described five modes, on two dimensions. One is how assertive you are, which is how far you pursue your own concerns. The other is how cooperative you are, which is how far you try to satisfy the other person's.

The competing style is high in assertiveness and low in cooperation. Accommodating is the reverse. Avoiding is low in both. Compromising is in the middle on both, and collaborating is high in both. The authors did not say that one is best. Competing is useful in an emergency, avoiding when an issue is trivial, and collaborating when the stakes are high on both sides. The value is in noticing which style you use by habit, and choosing another when the situation calls for it.

# Four steps for saying something hard
@conf mixed
@src Rosenberg, Nonviolent Communication, 1999

In the 1960s the psychologist Marshall Rosenberg, who worked with civil rights groups, developed a method of speaking that he called nonviolent communication. It has four parts. State what you observe, without judging, as in, you came home at nine. Say how you feel about it. Say the need behind the feeling, such as that you need to be able to count on a plan. Then make a specific request, such as would you tell me if you will be late.

He argued that judgments and demands cause people to defend themselves, and that facts and feelings do not. The method is widely taught in schools, workplaces and mediation. The research on it is thin, and some find the formula stiff. The core idea is sound, and matches other findings. A complaint about a specific behavior is easier to hear than an attack on the person, and a request is easier to answer than a demand.

# Two letters from Moscow
@series history
@src Cuban Missile Crisis, October 26 to 28, 1962

On the evening of October 26, 1962, in the worst days of the Cuban Missile Crisis, President Kennedy received a long, private and emotional letter from the Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev. It offered to remove the missiles from Cuba, if the United States promised not to invade. The next morning a second, harder letter arrived, which was broadcast on Moscow radio. It demanded that the United States also remove its missiles from Turkey.

Kennedy's advisers argued over what to do. In the end he answered the first letter, and accepted its terms, and ignored the demand in the second. He sent his brother, Robert Kennedy, to tell the Soviet ambassador, in private, that the Jupiter missiles in Turkey would be removed soon, but that this could not be a public part of the deal. On October 28 Khrushchev announced that the Soviet missiles would be removed. The Jupiters left Turkey in April 1963.

# Contact between groups
@conf mixed
@src Allport, The Nature of Prejudice, 1954; Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006

In 1954 the psychologist Gordon Allport suggested that contact between members of different groups can reduce prejudice, but only under certain conditions. The groups should have equal status in the situation, a common goal, and a need to cooperate, and the contact should have the support of authorities, such as a school or an employer. Without them, contact could make things worse. This became known as the contact hypothesis.

In 2006 Thomas Pettigrew and Linda Tropp pooled 713 samples from 515 studies. They found that contact generally reduced prejudice, and that Allport's conditions made it stronger, but were not essential. Later reviews found that the effects are smaller than first thought, especially for prejudice based on race or ethnicity, and that they may not last. Still, the finding that knowing people from other groups is linked to less prejudice is among the best supported in social psychology.

# Prison, but with the guards left to themselves
@conf mixed
@src Reicher & Haslam, British Journal of Social Psychology, 2006

In the 1971 Stanford prison experiment, students acted as guards and prisoners, and the guards became abusive. It has often been said that this shows that people given power turn cruel. In 2001 the psychologists Stephen Reicher and Alexander Haslam, with the BBC, tried a different version. They divided men at random into guards and prisoners, in a purpose-built setting, and watched them. The BBC broadcast it in four hours.

They were not told how to behave. The guards did not agree on how to use their authority, and did not act as a group. The study argued that tyranny does not arise simply from having power. It comes when groups fail, when people feel that they cannot make things work, and then accept a strong hand. The study had its critics, too. But it casts doubt on the idea that the roles alone make people cruel.

# The courts held under a tree
@series history
@src Gacaca courts, Rwanda, 2002 to 2012

After the genocide in Rwanda in 1994, in which an estimated half a million to a million people were killed in about a hundred days, the courts had to deal with more than a hundred thousand suspects in jail. At the pace of the regular courts it would have taken generations. In 2002 Rwanda revived a traditional system, called gacaca, which means grass, where the elders of a village used to settle disputes, seated on the lawn. The new courts were run by judges who were elected by their neighbors.

There were about 11,000 of them. They opened in 2005 and closed in June 2012, and they heard nearly two million cases, with most ending in a conviction. The idea was to establish the truth and allow reconciliation, as well as justice. Human Rights Watch and others criticized the lack of legal training and the unfair trials. The results were mixed. It was a bold attempt to deal with a crime too large for any normal system.
