# What mindfulness trials show
@src Goyal et al., JAMA Internal Medicine, 2014

Mindfulness meditation has become very popular, and many claims are made for it. In 2014 Madhav Goyal and his colleagues at Johns Hopkins reviewed the evidence from trials. They found forty-seven trials, with about thirty-five hundred participants, that compared meditation programs with a control, such as a waiting list or education. They looked at what was reported in terms of anxiety, depression, pain and stress.

There was moderate evidence that the programs improved anxiety, depression and pain. The size of the effects was modest, about the same as that seen with antidepressants in some trials. The evidence for stress and quality of life was weaker, and there was little evidence of effects on mood, attention or sleep. Mindfulness was no better than other active treatments, such as exercise or medicine. It's a useful tool, and not a cure for everything.

# A walk in nature and the ruminating mind
@src Bratman, Hamilton, Hahn, Daily & Gross, PNAS, 2015

Rumination is the habit of going over and over negative thoughts, and it's linked to depression. In 2015 Gregory Bratman and his colleagues at Stanford asked people to take a ninety-minute walk, either in a grassy area with oaks near the university, or along a busy road in Palo Alto. Before and after, they were asked about rumination, and their brains were scanned.

After the nature walk, people reported less rumination. Their brain scans showed less activity in the subgenual prefrontal cortex, a region that is overactive when people brood. The walk on the busy road produced no such change. The study was small, but it fits other research on the benefits of green spaces. A short walk in a park may not cure depression, but it's an easy way to get some distance from a thought loop.

# How many sessions does therapy need?
@src Howard, Kopta, Krause & Orlinsky, American Psychologist, 1986

How long does it take for psychotherapy to help? In 1986 Kenneth Howard and his colleagues combined the data from fifteen studies, with over two thousand patients. They plotted how the percentage of patients who had improved rose with the number of sessions.

About half of the patients had improved after eight weekly sessions, and about three quarters after twenty-six. Most of the improvement came early, and then the gains became smaller, which is called a diminishing returns curve. Later studies have found a similar pattern, though people with more severe or long-standing problems often need more. The research is a reason to expect some benefit within a couple of months, and to review progress if there is none.

# Self-help books can help
@src Cuijpers, 1997; Cuijpers et al., Psychological Medicine, 2010

Many people turn to self-help books for depression and anxiety. Do they work? In 1997 Pim Cuijpers combined the results of studies of bibliotherapy, in which people with depression read a book based on cognitive behavioral therapy. The people who read the books improved more than those who were put on a waiting list. The effect was large.

A later review in 2010, by Pim Cuijpers and his colleagues, looked at studies of guided self-help, in which a person gets some support from a therapist, and found that it worked about as well as face-to-face therapy for depression and anxiety. The books that work are based on techniques that have been tested, such as behavioral activation and thought records. It's a reasonable first step, for mild to moderate problems, and also a supplement to therapy.

# Writing about your best possible self
@src King, PSPB, 2001; Sheldon & Lyubomirsky, J. Positive Psychology, 2006
@conf mixed

In 2001 Laura King asked students to write for twenty minutes a day, for four days. One group wrote about their best possible future selves, imagining that everything had gone as well as it could. A second group wrote about a traumatic event. A third wrote about their plans for the day. Months later, those who wrote about their best selves were happier, and had fewer visits to the health center.

In 2006 Kennon Sheldon and Sonja Lyubomirsky used it again, and found that it gave a short-term boost in mood, particularly when people kept doing it, and found it meaningful. Later studies show a small but consistent effect on optimism and well-being. It may work because it helps people to set goals, and think about what matters to them. It's a simple exercise, and it costs nothing to try.

# Cutting social media for three weeks
@src Hunt, Marx, Lipson & Young, Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 2018
@conf mixed

In 2018 Melissa Hunt and her colleagues at the University of Pennsylvania asked a hundred and forty-three undergraduates to use their phones in one of two ways for three weeks. One group continued as usual. The other group limited its use of Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat to ten minutes a day on each. The students sent in screenshots to show how much they were using.

At the end of the three weeks, the group with limits reported less loneliness and less depression than the control group. Both groups improved on other measures, such as anxiety, perhaps because they were paying more attention to their use. Other studies of giving up social media have had mixed results, and effects depend on how people use it. But the findings suggest that there may be benefits to cutting back, particularly for those who feel worse after scrolling.

# What works for loneliness
@src Masi, Chen, Hawkley & Cacioppo, Personality and Social Psychology Review, 2011

Loneliness is painful, and it's linked to poor health. What can be done about it? In 2011 Christopher Masi and his colleagues combined the results of fifty studies of attempts to reduce loneliness. They compared four approaches. These were improving social skills, enhancing social support, increasing opportunities for contact, and addressing maladaptive social thinking.

The most effective approach was the last. Lonely people often expect rejection, and notice signs of it, and pull back, which makes more loneliness. Programs that helped them to question those expectations did better than those that only gave them more chances to meet people. It doesn't mean that contact doesn't matter. But it suggests that how we think about others is a key part of the problem, and of the solution.

# Exposure for obsessions
@src Foa et al., American Journal of Psychiatry, 2005

Obsessive compulsive disorder involves unwanted thoughts, and rituals that people feel driven to perform to reduce their anxiety, such as washing hands or checking a lock many times. The most effective psychological treatment is exposure and response prevention. The person is gradually exposed to the thing that triggers the fear, and prevented from doing the ritual. They learn that the anxiety falls on its own.

In 2005 Edna Foa and her colleagues compared this therapy with the antidepressant clomipramine, and with a placebo, in over a hundred adults. The therapy worked better than the drug, and the combination was not better than the therapy alone. Many patients had a major reduction in symptoms. The treatment is hard, since it asks people to face their fears. But it's a good example of the principle that facing a fear lets it fade.

# How much of happiness is in the genes?
@src Lykken & Tellegen, Psychological Science, 1996
@conf mixed

In 1996 David Lykken and Auke Tellegen studied thousands of pairs of twins in Minnesota. They asked them how happy they were, and compared identical twins, who share all their genes, with fraternal twins, who share half. They found that identical twins were much more alike in happiness. They concluded that about half of the differences between people's well-being, at a given time, could be due to genes.

The authors said that happiness is largely a matter of a stable personality, with a set point, and that people return to it after good or bad events. The idea has been used to say that we can't become happier. But heritability describes differences between people, in a group, and not what is fixed in a person. Studies also show that people's well-being can change a lot over years, and that it's affected by circumstances and by choices.

# The scar of losing a job
@src Clark, Diener, Georgellis & Lucas, Economic Journal, 2008

People adapt to many life changes, but not to all. In 2008 Andrew Clark and his colleagues used the German Socio-Economic Panel, a survey that followed thousands of people for up to twenty years. They looked at how life satisfaction changed before and after events such as marriage, divorce, widowhood and unemployment.

Marriage gave a short boost in satisfaction, which faded within a couple of years. Unemployment was different. Satisfaction fell sharply, and even years later, it had not recovered, even if the person had found a new job. The effect was not just due to a loss of income. Losing work seems to hit our sense of identity and purpose, and it leaves a scar. It's a good reason to take the emotional side of job loss seriously.

# The commuting paradox
@src Stutzer & Frey, Scandinavian Journal of Economics, 2008

Economists would expect people to choose a long commute only if they get something in return, such as a bigger house or a better salary. In 2008 Alois Stutzer and Bruno Frey analyzed a German survey, and found that people with longer commutes reported lower satisfaction with life. The extra pay or housing didn't seem to make up for the cost.

They called it the commuting paradox. People seem to underestimate how much a long daily journey will bother them, and overestimate the pleasure of the benefits, which we get used to. Commuting is stressful, since it's unpredictable and cannot be controlled. People who commute by walking or cycling are happier than those who drive. If you are weighing a house or a job, count the daily journey with care.

# How people spend their days
@src Kahneman, Krueger, Schkade, Schwarz & Stone, Science, 2004

In 2004 Daniel Kahneman and his colleagues developed a method for studying how people feel through the day, called the day reconstruction method. About nine hundred working women in Texas listed what they had done on the previous day, in short episodes, and rated how they felt in each one. The researchers added up the time spent, and the mood.

The most pleasant activities were intimate relations, socializing after work, and relaxing. The least pleasant were the morning commute, work, and the evening commute. Child care ranked lower than many expected, though it's often rated as meaningful. The study highlights the difference between how we feel moment by moment and how we judge our lives overall. It suggests that we might improve our days by arranging more time for the activities we enjoy.
