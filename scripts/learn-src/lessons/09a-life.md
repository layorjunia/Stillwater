# Cell theory
@src Schleiden, 1838; Schwann, 1839; Virchow, 1855

In 1838 the botanist Matthias Schleiden suggested that all plants are made of cells, and in 1839 Theodor Schwann said the same of animals. In 1855 Rudolf Virchow added the third part, that every cell comes from another cell. Together, the three ideas became the foundation of biology.

Before that, many people believed that living things could arise from nonliving matter, like flies from rotting meat. The cell theory ended that notion. A human body has around 30 trillion cells, and they all trace back to a single fertilized egg that divided again and again. Every cell in you is the descendant of a cell that was alive before it, an unbroken line stretching back billions of years.

# Mitochondria
@src Margulis (Sagan), 1967

Mitochondria are the small structures in your cells that turn food into usable energy. They have their own little ring of DNA, and you inherit them from your mother, because the egg supplies them and the sperm does not.

In 1967 the biologist Lynn Margulis argued that they began as free-living bacteria that were taken in by a larger cell, and the two ended up living together. Her idea was met with scorn at first. Most biologists now accept it, and it's called endosymbiosis. It also applies to plant chloroplasts, which are descended from captured photosynthetic bacteria. In a sense, every complex cell, including each one of yours, is a partnership that began more than a billion years ago.

# Photosynthesis
@src Campbell Biology

Plants, algae and some bacteria capture sunlight and use its energy to turn carbon dioxide and water into sugar, giving off oxygen as a byproduct. It's the process that feeds almost all life on Earth, directly or indirectly.

The pigment chlorophyll absorbs mostly red and blue light and reflects green, so leaves look green. In the fall, when the chlorophyll breaks down, the yellow and orange pigments that were hidden underneath show through. Tiny algae in the oceans produce roughly half of the oxygen we breathe, which means a lot of every breath comes from the sea. The sugar a tree makes is built largely from carbon dioxide in the air, and so a tree is mostly made of air.

# Cellular respiration
@src Campbell Biology

Your cells burn sugar with oxygen to make ATP, a molecule that works as the cell's energy currency. One molecule of glucose can yield about 30 ATP. It's the reverse of photosynthesis. The carbon dioxide you breathe out is the carbon from the food you ate.

The body uses ATP so quickly that it recycles about its own weight in it each day, rebuilding it again and again. It's the reason you need to keep breathing and eating. The supply of ATP in your cells lasts only seconds, so the machinery has to run all the time. When you hold your breath, you can feel the urgency, and that's your cells running low on the oxygen they need to make more.

# The double helix
@src Watson & Crick, 1953

In 1953 James Watson and Francis Crick published their model of DNA's structure in a one-page paper in Nature. It showed two strands twisted around each other like a spiral staircase. They noted, with some understatement, that the structure suggested a way for the genetic material to copy itself.

It was built using X-ray images made by Rosalind Franklin and her student Raymond Gosling, and measurements by Maurice Wilkins. One of Franklin's images, known as Photo 51, was shown to Watson without her knowledge. Watson, Crick and Wilkins won the Nobel Prize in 1962. Franklin had died in 1958, and the prize isn't given after death. Many people today feel that her contribution didn't get the credit it deserved.

# Base pairing
@src Chargaff, 1950

DNA is built from four letters, A, T, G and C. In 1950 Erwin Chargaff discovered that in any organism's DNA, the amount of A matches the amount of T, and the amount of G matches C. The amounts of the four letters differed from species to species, but the matching held.

Watson and Crick used this to see that the letters pair up, A with T and G with C, across the two strands. That means each strand holds the information to rebuild the other, which is how DNA can copy itself. When a cell divides, the strands separate, and each one serves as a template for a new partner. It's an elegant design, since the same structure that stores the information also gives the method for copying it.

# We are all mutants
@src Kong et al., 2012

Every baby is born with some brand-new changes in their DNA that neither parent had. A study in Iceland in 2012 counted about 60 new changes per person on average. They arise mostly from small errors in copying the genetic material when the egg and sperm are made.

Most land in parts of the DNA that don't matter. A few may be harmful, and a very few help. Over many generations, these random changes are the raw material that natural selection works on. The study found that the number of changes rose with the father's age, by about two per year. So in a very real sense, each of us is a little bit new, and the human species is a vast experiment that has been running for a very long time.

# Mendel's peas
@src Mendel, 1866

Gregor Mendel was an Augustinian monk in what is now the Czech Republic. In the 1850s and 60s, he crossed thousands of pea plants and carefully counted the traits, such as tall versus short, in the offspring. It was patient work, and it took years.

He found consistent ratios, often about 3 to 1, and concluded that traits are passed on in distinct units, which we now call genes. His 1866 paper was ignored until 1900, years after he died. Three scientists independently rediscovered his results around that time, and the field of genetics began. Mendel is a good example of a careful experimenter who was ahead of his time, and who never knew how important his garden had been.

# The genetic code
@src Nirenberg & Khorana, 1960s

DNA is read in words of three letters, called codons. There are 64 possible codons, and they spell out the 20 amino acids that are strung together to make proteins, plus signals for start and stop. Since there are more codons than amino acids, several codons can mean the same thing.

The code was worked out in the 1960s by Marshall Nirenberg, Har Gobind Khorana and others. The code is almost the same in every living thing, from bacteria to humans, which is strong evidence that we share a common ancestor. A human gene can even be put into a bacterium, and the bacterium will read it correctly and make a human protein. That's how insulin is made for people with diabetes today.

# CRISPR
@src Jinek et al., 2012

Bacteria have an immune system that cuts up the DNA of invading viruses, called CRISPR. In 2012 Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier showed that this system could be reprogrammed to cut DNA at any chosen spot, making gene editing much easier and cheaper than before.

They won the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. In late 2023, the UK and the US approved the first medicine based on CRISPR, for sickle cell disease. It's a rare case of a basic discovery about bacteria turning into a treatment within about a decade. Gene editing also raises serious ethical questions, especially when it comes to changes that could be passed to children, and scientists around the world are still debating where the lines should be.

# Epigenetics
@src Standard genetics text
@conf mixed

Your cells all carry the same DNA, yet a liver cell is very different from a neuron. The difference lies in which genes are switched on, and chemical tags on the DNA and its packaging help control that. These tags are called epigenetic marks.

Diet, stress and age can alter them. Some people claim these changes can be inherited by grandchildren, such as the effect of famine on later generations. Evidence for that in humans is limited and debated, though a few animal studies support it. So the idea is real in a narrow sense, since marks do shape how genes work, and uncertain in the broad sense that is often claimed. It's an exciting field, and one where headlines tend to run ahead of the evidence.

# Convergent evolution
@src Standard evolution text

Sometimes unrelated species end up with similar features because they face similar challenges. Eyes have evolved independently dozens of times. Dolphins, which are mammals, and sharks, which are fish, have similar streamlined bodies. Birds, bats and insects all developed wings separately.

It's strong evidence that natural selection is a powerful force, and that certain solutions work again and again. If you gave the same problem to evolution many times, it would often come up with similar answers. At the same time, the details usually differ. A bat's wing is made of stretched skin over long fingers, a bird's of feathers, an insect's of thin membranes. They fly in the same way for the same reasons, but they were each built in their own way.

# Homology
@src Darwin, On the Origin of Species (1859)

Look at the skeleton inside a human arm, a bat's wing and a whale's flipper. The same bones, a single upper bone, two lower bones, wrist bones and fingers, appear in all three, though used for very different purposes. A person can lift a cup, a bat can fly and a whale can steer.

Charles Darwin saw this as strong evidence of common descent: different jobs, same blueprint, modified over time. It's a bit like the same recipe adapted for different meals. If each had been designed from scratch, there would be no reason for a whale to have finger bones. The pattern makes sense if all three came from a shared ancestor that had a limb with those bones, and each descendant modified it in its own way.

# Evolution is not progress
@src Gould, Full House (1996)

It's common to imagine evolution as a ladder, with humans at the top. But natural selection only fits creatures to their local conditions. It has no goal, and it doesn't plan ahead. What works in one place and time may be a disadvantage somewhere else.

Cave fish living in total darkness have lost their eyes, and tapeworms have lost their guts, and both are perfectly suited to their lives. The biologist Stephen Jay Gould argued that life is better pictured as a bush with many branches. Bacteria, which are the oldest life, are still the most common form of it, and by many measures they're the most successful. Humans aren't the end of the story. We're one twig on a huge tree.

# Antibiotic resistance
@src Murray et al., Lancet, 2022

Bacteria reproduce so fast that they can evolve resistance to drugs within years. Each time antibiotics are used, they kill the vulnerable bacteria and leave the resistant ones to multiply. It's natural selection at work in a very short time, and in a very practical setting.

A 2022 study estimated that drug-resistant bacterial infections directly caused 1.27 million deaths worldwide in 2019. Using antibiotics only when needed, and completing the course, slows the process. Antibiotics don't work against colds or the flu, which are caused by viruses, so taking them for those only adds to the problem. New antibiotics are hard to develop, and the old ones are precious. Preserving them is a shared responsibility, and each of us has a part in it.

# Darwin's finches
@src Grant & Grant, 2002

On the Galápagos Islands, Peter and Rosemary Grant spent four decades measuring finches. They caught, measured and banded thousands of birds on a small island called Daphne Major. In 1977 a severe drought killed most of the small, soft seeds, leaving mainly big, hard ones.

Birds with larger beaks could crack them and survived better. The next generation had measurably larger beaks. It showed natural selection at work within a single human lifetime, not just over millions of years. When the rains returned, and the seeds changed again, the trend later reversed. The Grants' work is some of the clearest evidence of evolution in action, and it shows that the process is continuing around us today.

# Lenski's long experiment
@src Blount, Borland & Lenski, 2008

In 1988 the biologist Richard Lenski started twelve separate populations of E. coli bacteria in flasks in his lab. Every day since, a sample has been moved to fresh food. The flasks have been going for more than three decades, through tens of thousands of generations, which, if they were human generations, would stretch back about three quarters of a million years.

After more than 30,000 generations, one population suddenly evolved the ability to feed on citrate, which E. coli normally can't do. The experiment, still running, shows evolution as it happens, and it can be replayed with frozen samples to see whether it would happen again. It is a patient, simple and beautiful piece of science, and it has answered questions about how chance and necessity shape evolution.

# Our common ancestor
@src Theobald, Nature, 2010

All the life on Earth that we know of, from bacteria to blue whales, shares DNA, the same genetic code and similar basic cell machinery. That points to descent from a single ancestral population that lived somewhere between 3.5 and 4 billion years ago.

A formal statistical test in 2010, by the biologist Douglas Theobald, found that this common-ancestry idea fits the data better than the alternatives. The differences between species are just branches of one tree. It's a humbling and beautiful thought that you share a distant ancestor with every oak, mushroom and fish. If you went back far enough, you would find a single population of microbes that is the great-great-grandparent of everything alive today.

# Humans and chimps
@src Chimpanzee Genome Consortium, 2005

When scientists compared our genome with the chimpanzee's in 2005, they found that, where the sequences line up, about 98.8 percent of the letters are the same. That figure counts single-letter differences, and you might see it quoted as "we're 99 percent chimp."

When you include stretches that are inserted or missing, the difference is larger, about 4 to 5 percent. Either way, our closest living relatives are chimpanzees and bonobos, and our lines split roughly six to seven million years ago. Small changes in how genes are turned on, early in development, seem to matter more than the number of differences. A tiny difference in the plan can produce a very large difference in the animal.

# Neanderthal DNA
@src Green et al., 2010

In 2010 the Swedish geneticist Svante Pääbo and his team published a draft of the Neanderthal genome, pieced together from tiny fragments of ancient bone. It was an astonishing technical feat, since the DNA was thousands of years old and badly broken.

It showed that people whose ancestors lived outside Africa carry about 1 to 2 percent Neanderthal DNA. That means our ancestors met Neanderthals and had children with them. Some of the inherited genes affect things like the immune system and skin. Pääbo won the 2022 Nobel Prize in Medicine for this work. It overturned the old picture of Neanderthals as a separate and vanished branch. In a small way, they're still with us.

# Where our species began
@src Hublin et al., 2017

For a long time, we thought that modern humans emerged about 200,000 years ago. In 2017 researchers reported fossils from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, which they dated to roughly 300,000 years ago, with features of both modern humans and earlier forms.

The finding suggests that our species didn't appear in a single place and time. It evolved across the whole of Africa, in pieces, over a long period. Our species spread through Africa and left in waves, and the main migration out was about 60,000 years ago. The picture is still changing as new fossils and DNA come in. We know much more than we did a generation ago, and there are probably more surprises to come.

# Viruses and bacteria
@src Standard microbiology text

Bacteria are living single-celled organisms, and many are harmless or helpful, while a few cause disease. Viruses are much smaller and aren't cells at all. They're packets of genetic material that can only reproduce by hijacking a host's cells.

Antibiotics target bacteria, so they do nothing against colds or the flu, which are caused by viruses. For viruses, we use vaccines and, in some cases, antiviral drugs. It's a distinction that matters in daily life, since many people expect an antibiotic every time they're sick. Most of the bacteria in and on your body do you no harm, and some, such as those in your gut, are valuable. You carry roughly as many bacterial cells as human ones.

# Penicillin
@src Fleming, 1929; Florey & Chain

In 1928 the Scottish doctor Alexander Fleming came back from vacation to find that a mold had contaminated one of his bacteria dishes, and the bacteria around it had died. He noticed something that many people would have thrown in the trash.

He'd noticed the effect, but turning it into a drug took a decade of work by Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and their team at Oxford, who made it in the early 1940s. The three shared the 1945 Nobel Prize in Medicine. Penicillin saved many thousands of lives in the Second World War, and it has been one of the most important medical advances in history. It's a lasting lesson that discoveries need both the lucky observation and the hard work.

# Keystone species
@src Paine, 1966

In 1966 the ecologist Robert Paine experimented on the rocky coast of Washington state by removing all the starfish from a patch of shore. Without them, mussels took over and crowded out other species, and the variety of life dropped sharply.

A single predator turned out to be holding the whole community in balance. He called such species keystone species, like the stone at the top of an arch, which holds all the others in place. Sea otters, which keep sea urchins from destroying kelp forests, are another example. It's a way of seeing that not all species matter equally to an ecosystem, and that the loss of one may have effects far beyond what we'd expect.

# Wolves in Yellowstone
@src Ripple & Beschta, 2012
@conf mixed

Wolves were removed from Yellowstone National Park by the 1920s and brought back in 1995. Some of the effects are well documented, such as fewer elk in some areas, and the elk behaving more warily. They avoid places where they are easy to ambush.

A popular story says that the wolves also changed the courses of the rivers, by letting trees grow back along the banks. That part is debated, since the effects on plants vary and other factors matter. Scientists have found that willows grew back in some places, but not in all, and the connection to the rivers is less clear. So the wolves are a real success story with a more modest effect than the legend suggests.

# The ten percent rule
@src Lindeman, 1942

Energy is lost at each step up a food chain. A plant captures sunlight, a rabbit eats the plant, and a fox eats the rabbit. At each step only about a tenth of the energy gets passed on, since most is used for living and moving, and given off as heat.

That's why there are far fewer lions than zebras, and why food chains rarely have more than four or five links. The real figure varies between about 5 and 20 percent. The rule also explains why eating plants directly is a more efficient use of land than eating animals that have eaten plants. It's a simple idea from the ecologist Raymond Lindeman, and it explains a good deal about how nature is arranged.

# The Great Dying
@src Standard paleontology text

About 252 million years ago, the biggest mass extinction in Earth's history happened at the end of the Permian period. Massive volcanic eruptions in what's now Siberia pumped out huge amounts of carbon dioxide and other gases, and changed the climate and the chemistry of the oceans.

An estimated 80 to 96 percent of marine species, and about 70 percent of land vertebrates, died. It took millions of years for life to recover. The survivors then gave rise to a new set of animals, including the dinosaurs. It's a reminder that life on Earth has come close to ending, and that a large, fast change in the atmosphere can be deadly. The planet recovers, but on a timescale much longer than a human life.

# The largest organism
@src US Forest Service

The largest known living organism by area is a honey fungus, Armillaria ostoyae, in the Malheur National Forest in Oregon. Most of it is underground, spreading through the soil and the roots of trees as thin threads.

It covers about 2,400 acres, an area as big as well over a thousand football fields. It may be thousands of years old, and the part that you see is just a few mushrooms in the fall. The fungus is a parasite that slowly kills the trees it grows on. It's one single individual, with all of the same DNA. A walk through that forest is, in a way, a walk across the body of one very large and very old living thing.

# The wood wide web
@src Karst et al., 2023
@conf contested

Popular books have described forests as having an underground "wood wide web," in which trees share food and send warnings through fungi connected to their roots. It's a beautiful idea, and it has been repeated in films and talks, so many people believe it.

But in 2023 a team led by Justine Karst reviewed the research and found that the evidence for forest-wide sharing of nutrients and signals is far weaker than the stories suggest. Fungi do connect to roots, but what they do with the connection is less clear. Some of the best-known claims rest on a small number of experiments. It's a fine example of an appealing idea that has run ahead of the science, and of why it's worth asking how well something has been shown.

# Phototropism
@src Darwin & Darwin, 1880

Have you noticed a houseplant bending toward a window? That's phototropism. In 1880 Charles Darwin and his son Francis experimented with grass seedlings, covering different parts of them, and found that the tip senses the light and sends a signal to the part below, which bends.

The signal turned out to be a plant hormone, which was later named auxin. It was one of the first plant hormones to be identified. The side of the stem that's away from the light grows faster, and so the plant curves toward it. A simple experiment with a few seedlings and some bits of foil led to the discovery of one of the chemical messengers that control how plants grow, and it came from the same man who wrote On the Origin of Species.

# The waggle dance
@src von Frisch, 1967

When a honeybee finds a good source of flowers, it returns to the hive and dances on the honeycomb. The Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch decoded the dance in the 1940s. The angle of the straight run shows the direction of the food compared with the sun, and how long it lasts shows the distance.

The other bees follow the dancer in the dark, feeling the movements, and then fly off to the flowers. It's one of the few cases in nature of animals passing detailed, abstract information to one another in a kind of symbolic language. He won the 1973 Nobel Prize for it, one of the few Nobels in the study of animal behavior. Many people were skeptical at first, but later experiments confirmed his reading.

# The octopus
@src Hochner, 2012

An octopus has three hearts: two pump blood through the gills and one through the body. Its blood is blue, because it carries oxygen with copper instead of iron. Its body plan is utterly unlike ours, and yet it's very intelligent.

About two-thirds of its neurons are in its arms, and each can taste, touch and even make simple decisions on its own. Octopuses have been seen solving puzzles, opening jars and escaping from tanks. Their ancestors split from ours more than 500 million years ago, so their intelligence evolved on a separate path. Meeting an octopus is as close as we can come to meeting an intelligent alien on Earth.

# Tardigrades
@src Jönsson et al., 2008

Tardigrades are microscopic animals, less than a millimeter long, nicknamed water bears. They look like tiny plump creatures with eight legs, and you can find them in a clump of moss.

They can survive being dried out for years, frozen near absolute zero, heated above boiling and exposed to high radiation. In 2007 a European Space Agency experiment exposed them to the vacuum and solar radiation of space for ten days. Some survived and even laid eggs after returning to Earth. When they dry out, they curl up into a state called a tun and suspend their metabolism almost completely. Add water, and they get going again, as if nothing had happened.

# Naked mole rats
@src Buffenstein, 2008

Naked mole rats are small, hairless rodents that live underground in colonies in East Africa. They live about 30 years, far longer than mice of similar size, which live about three. They also rarely get cancer.

One reason may be that their cells make an unusually thick, sugary substance that stops cells from crowding together and forming tumors. They also live in colonies with a queen, rather like bees, which is unusual among mammals. Researchers study them for clues about aging and cancer. They're odd-looking animals, but they may have a few things to teach us about how to stay healthy for a long time.

# The Greenland shark
@src Nielsen et al., 2016

The Greenland shark is the longest-lived vertebrate we know of. In 2016 a team in Denmark used radiocarbon dating on proteins in the lens of the eyes of 28 sharks and estimated that the biggest was about 392 years old, give or take 120 years.

These sharks grow very slowly, only about a centimeter a year, and are thought to reach maturity at about 150 years of age. They live in the cold, deep water of the North Atlantic, where everything runs at a slow pace. A shark born in the 1600s might still be swimming today. The estimates have a wide margin of error, but even the lowest ones show an animal that lives for at least two hundred years.

# The immortal jellyfish
@src Piraino et al., 1996

Turritopsis dohrnii is a tiny jellyfish that, when it's injured or stressed, can turn back into an earlier stage of life. The adult reverts to a polyp, a kind of larval form, and starts its life cycle again.

In theory, it could do it indefinitely. In practice, most of them still get eaten or die of disease, so none of them is truly immortal. But they are a subject of intense study in aging research, because they seem to be able to reset the clock of the cell. The jellyfish is only about the size of a fingernail, and it lives in oceans around the world. It reminds us that aging, which seems a law of nature, may not be as fixed as it appears.

# Crows remember faces
@src Marzluff et al., 2010

In a 2010 study, John Marzluff and his colleagues at the University of Washington wore a rubber mask while they trapped and banded wild crows. For years afterward, crows scolded and swooped at anyone wearing the same mask, though not at people in other masks.

Even young crows that had never seen the trapping joined in, which means that crows can pass on information about dangerous people. They learn from their parents and from other crows in the flock. The scolding went on for many years. It's a striking sign of how smart crows are, and of how well they can recognize people. If you're nice to a crow, it may remember that too.
