# Thinking about your own thinking
@src Flavell, American Psychologist, 1979

In the 1970s the developmental psychologist John Flavell noticed that young children are poor at something older children do without effort. A five-year-old who is asked to memorize a list of toys will stare at it for a moment and announce that she is done. An older child studies the list, tests herself and goes back to the items that she keeps missing. In 1979 Flavell gave the skill a name, metacognition, which means knowing about your own knowing.

It has two halves. One is knowledge about how minds work, such as the fact that a long list is harder than a short one, and that you forget things you don't rehearse. The other is control, which means checking how a task is going and changing your approach when it isn't working. Most of us do the first reasonably well and the second badly, and that gap explains a surprising amount of wasted study time.

# Monitoring and control
@src Nelson & Narens, 1990

Two psychologists, Thomas Nelson and Louis Narens, described metacognition as a loop with two parts. Monitoring is the part that watches your own mind and reports back: this passage makes sense, that one doesn't, I know this name, I'm about to lose it. Control is the part that acts on the report, by rereading, slowing down, asking for help or moving on.

The loop works only as well as its monitor. If your sense of what you know is wrong, your control will be wrong too, however hard you work. A student who feels that a chapter is familiar will stop studying it, even though familiarity and knowledge are different things. That's why much of the research on learning is really about how to get a more honest report from the monitor, so that effort goes where it's needed.

# The feeling of knowing
@src Hart, 1965

Everyone has had the experience of being sure that they know something, like the name of an actor, without being able to produce it. In 1965 Joseph Hart turned this into an experiment. He asked people general-knowledge questions, and when they couldn't answer, he asked them how sure they were that they'd recognize the answer later on a multiple-choice test. Their hunches turned out to be better than chance. People who felt that they knew it were more likely to recognize it.

So the feeling isn't just noise. It's a rough signal that a memory is stored and that it's worth continuing to search, which is why the advice to give up on a tip-of-the-tongue word is half right. The signal can also be fooled, though, since a feeling of knowing can come from the mere familiarity of the question's words. It's a useful hint and a poor guarantee.

# Judging what you've learned
@src Nelson & Dunlosky, 1991

Students often decide that they've learned something right after reading it, and that is when the judgment is least accurate. In 1991 Thomas Nelson and John Dunlosky had people study word pairs and then rate how likely they were to remember each one. When the rating was made right after studying, it predicted the later test only weakly. When it was made a few minutes later, it was far more accurate. The gap was large, and it's now known as the delayed-JOL effect, one of the most robust findings in the study of memory monitoring.

The reason is that right after studying, the answer is still sitting in short-term memory, so everything feels known. After a delay, you have to pull it from long-term memory, and you find out what you really have. The practical lesson is simple. Don't judge your learning right away. Wait, then try to recall, and let that attempt tell you what to study next.

# The illusion of knowing
@src Glenberg, Wilkinson & Epstein, 1982

In 1982 Arthur Glenberg and his colleagues gave students short passages that contained an obvious contradiction, such as a statement in the middle of a paragraph that said the opposite of an earlier one. Many readers rated the passage as easy to understand and never noticed the problem. They'd been reading the words smoothly and taking the smoothness as a sign of comprehension.

Psychologists call this the illusion of knowing. Fluent reading feels like understanding, and rereading makes the text more fluent, so rereading feels productive even when it adds little. One way out is to put the text away and explain it in your own words, or to ask yourself a question about it that you can't answer without thinking. If you can't do that, you haven't understood it yet, however clear it felt.

# Knowing how much you know
@src Kruger & Dunning, 1999
@conf mixed

In 1999 David Dunning and Justin Kruger tested students on logic, grammar and humor, and asked them to estimate how they'd done. Those in the bottom quarter scored around the 12th percentile, but on average they thought they'd scored around the 62nd. The top performers were a little modest. The authors argued that skill and the ability to judge skill go together, so the least skilled people can't see their own mistakes.

The idea became famous, and it's often misused as a way to call people stupid. The evidence is more mixed than the legend. Some critics argue that much of the pattern comes from statistics, since anyone who scores very low has more room to overestimate, and that nearly everyone overestimates somewhat. The safe lesson is more modest and more useful. We are all poor judges of our own competence in areas that we know little about, and feedback from outside is the cure.

# Calibration: how sure should you be?
@src Lichtenstein, Fischhoff & Phillips, 1982

A person is well calibrated when their confidence matches their results. If you say that you're 80 percent sure of a hundred different things, you should be right about eighty times. Researchers who tested this in the 1970s and 1980s found that most people are overconfident. When people said that they were 90 percent sure, they were right about 75 percent of the time or less.

The pattern is easy to test on yourself. Write down a prediction with a percentage, such as "I'll finish this by Friday, 70 percent." After a few weeks, look at how often your 70s came true. Weather forecasters are among the few professionals who are well calibrated, because they get daily feedback on exactly this. Most of us never get it, so we never learn that our certainty is a bit too high.

# Delayed testing tells the truth
@src Karpicke & Blunt, Science, 2011

In 2011 Jeffrey Karpicke and Janell Blunt compared two ways of studying a science text. One group read it and then drew detailed concept maps, which is a popular and effortful method. The other group read it and then wrote down everything that they could remember, over and over. A week later, the second group answered more questions correctly, including questions that needed inference, and not only facts.

The students had predicted the opposite. Most thought that concept mapping would work better, because it felt more organized and thorough. Retrieval felt messy and frustrating, so they underrated it. It's a good example of a larger rule in learning: how easy a method feels is a poor guide to how well it works. The struggle of pulling something out of memory is part of what makes it stick.

# Desirable difficulties
@src Bjork, 1994

Robert Bjork, a psychologist at UCLA, coined the term "desirable difficulties" for conditions that make learning feel slower but make it last longer. Spacing out your practice, mixing different kinds of problems, and testing yourself instead of rereading all slow you down at first and cost you some accuracy during practice. They pay back later, in what you can still do weeks afterward.

The catch is that a difficulty is only desirable if you can overcome it. If a task is so hard that you can't make progress, it's just frustrating. Bjork's point is that learners and teachers tend to judge by performance during practice, which rewards the easy conditions. A smooth lesson can produce a lot of quick progress and not much memory, and a bumpy one can do the reverse.

# Mixing it up beats grouping
@src Rohrer & Taylor, 2007

Most students practice one kind of problem at a time: ten of one type, then ten of the next. In 2007 Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor tested a different way. Some students practiced four kinds of geometry problems in blocks, and others practiced the same problems mixed together. During practice, the blocked group did better. A week later, on a test, the mixed group scored 63 percent, and the blocked group scored 20 percent.

Mixing forces you to work out which method a problem needs, which is the skill that the test and real life demand. When problems come in blocks, you can just repeat the last move without thinking. Interleaving feels worse while you're doing it, and the effect has been found in sports, art and math. It's an easy change. Shuffle your flashcards, and mix topics in a study session.

# Why spacing works
@src Cepeda et al., 2008

The spacing effect is one of the oldest and most reliable findings in psychology: the same amount of study works better when it's spread over time. In 2008 Nicholas Cepeda and his colleagues tested more than a thousand people, with gaps between study sessions ranging from minutes to months, and with tests at different delays afterward. They found that the best gap depends on how long you need to remember. For a test in a week, a gap of about a day was best. For a test in a year, a gap of three or four weeks was best.

Too short a gap wastes the second session, because the material is still fresh. Too long a gap means you've forgotten it all. A decent rule is to review something when you've begun to forget it but can still dredge it up, which is just what a spaced-repetition schedule does.

# Learning styles do not hold up
@src Pashler et al., 2008
@conf mixed

Many teachers believe that people have a learning style, such as visual, auditory or hands-on, and learn best when teaching matches it. In 2008 four psychologists, led by Harold Pashler, reviewed the research. They looked for experiments that taught people in their preferred style and in another one and compared the results. They found almost none that were well designed, and the few that were didn't support the idea.

People do have preferences, and most enjoy variety. And some content suits a particular form, since you can't learn to read a map without a map or to play a note without hearing it. But the claim that an individual learns better when taught in their favorite style has not been shown. A better approach is to match the method to the material, and to give everyone the benefit of practice, feedback and good examples.

# Explaining it to yourself
@src Chi et al., 1989

In 1989 Michelene Chi and her colleagues asked students to study worked examples from a physics textbook, and recorded them as they spoke aloud. The students who did best on later problems weren't the ones who read more. They were the ones who stopped to explain each step to themselves: why this formula, what that number means, how this connects to the last page.

Poor students read the same lines and moved on. Researchers call it self-explanation, and its effect has been found in many subjects, from science to math to reading. It works because it forces you to notice gaps. When you can't say why a step follows, you've found something that you'd have missed by reading. A simple habit is to pause after each paragraph and say, in your own words, what it means and why it's true.

# The generation effect
@src Slamecka & Graf, 1978

In 1978 Norman Slamecka and Peter Graf gave people pairs of words in two ways. In one condition, they were simply told to read the pair, such as "hot, cold." In the other, they were given the first word and the first letter of the second, such as "hot, c," and had to produce the answer themselves. Later, the people who had generated the word remembered it better than the ones who had only read it.

It's now called the generation effect. Working out an answer, even a trivial one, leaves a stronger trace than being handed it. That's why it helps to try a problem before you look at the solution, to predict the ending before reading it, and to write a summary from memory instead of highlighting a page. The effort is the point, and a little struggle is worth more than a smooth read.

# Teaching makes you learn
@src Chase et al., 2009

If you've ever tutored someone, you may have noticed that you learned a lot yourself. In 2009 Catherine Chase and her colleagues tested the idea with schoolchildren, who were asked to teach a computer character, a virtual student named Betty, about a science topic. The students worked harder for Betty than they did for themselves. They prepared more, they were more careful, and they learned more than students who studied the same material for their own use.

Researchers call it the protégé effect. Teaching makes you organize what you know, find the holes in it and think about how the other person might misunderstand. You don't need a classroom for this. Explaining an idea to a friend, to a rubber duck or to an imaginary audience gives much of the benefit, and a failed explanation is a useful signal about what to study next.

# Practice that stretches you
@src Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer, 1993

In 1993 Anders Ericsson and two colleagues studied violin students at a music academy in Berlin. They found that the best students didn't just practice more. They spent more of their time on what Ericsson called deliberate practice: working alone, on specific weaknesses, at the edge of their ability, with clear goals and quick feedback. It wasn't much fun, and they could do it for only a few hours a day.

The idea has been very influential, and it's a good picture of what effective practice looks like. Playing through the pieces you already know is pleasant and teaches you little. The useful work is picking out the hardest bars, slowing them down, and repeating them until they work. The same applies to a speech, a sport or a language. A little time spent on what you can't yet do beats a lot of time on what you can.

# What the ten thousand hours really said
@src Ericsson, 1993; Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, 2014
@conf mixed

The "ten thousand hour rule" came from a bestselling book by Malcolm Gladwell in 2008, which took it from the violin study by Ericsson. In that study, the best violinists had accumulated about 10,000 hours of practice by age 20, the good ones about 8,000, and the future music teachers about 4,000. Ericsson later objected that the number was an average and not a magic threshold, and that quality of practice mattered more than the count.

In 2014 Brooke Macnamara and her colleagues combined many studies and found that practice explained about a quarter of the differences in skill in games, around a fifth in music and in sports, and far less in education and in professional fields. So practice matters, but not everything does. Talent, starting age, teaching and luck all play a part, and nobody is guaranteed mastery after 10,000 hours.

# How experts see problems
@src Chi, Feltovich & Glaser, 1981

In 1981 Michelene Chi and her colleagues gave physics problems to graduate students and to beginners and asked them to sort the problems into groups. The beginners sorted by what the problems looked like: these all have an inclined plane, those all have a spring. The experts sorted by the underlying principle: these are all conservation of energy, those are all about forces.

The experts weren't just faster. They saw different things. What they noticed in a problem was its deep structure, and that told them which tools to use. It's one reason that experience can't simply be replaced by a list of rules. If you want to learn like an expert, practice asking what a problem is really about and not what it looks like, and try grouping examples by the idea behind them.

# Comparing cases to learn the principle
@src Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson, 2003

In 2003 Dedre Gentner and her colleagues taught business students about negotiation using two short case studies. Some students read the cases separately and wrote about each one. Others were asked to compare the two and write down what they had in common. Later, in a real negotiation exercise, the students who had compared the cases were nearly three times as likely to use the strategy from the cases.

Reading one example teaches you about that example. Comparing two forces you to ask what's the same, which pulls out the principle and makes it portable. The method works for almost anything you want to learn. When you meet a new example of an idea, place it next to the last one, and ask what they share. That's how knowledge moves from one situation to another.

# Failing first, then learning
@src Kapur, 2008
@conf mixed

Manu Kapur, a researcher who began his work in Singapore, tested a counterintuitive idea in 2008. Instead of teaching students a concept and then giving them problems, he gave them the problem first, before any instruction, so that they struggled with it and mostly failed. Then he taught the concept. The students who failed first did better on later tests of understanding than those who were taught first.

He called it productive failure. Struggling with a problem seems to prepare you to notice what matters when the explanation comes, because you've felt the problem that the concept solves. The results aren't universal, and it works best when the struggle is guided and the explanation follows. But it's a good reminder that confusion is not always a sign of poor teaching. Sometimes it's the start of understanding.

# Trying and then checking
@src Roediger & Butler, 2011

When you study for a test, the simplest and best-supported technique is to close the book and try to recall what you've read. Henry Roediger and Andrew Butler reviewed the research in 2011 and found that retrieval practice improves long-term memory more than additional study, in laboratory studies and in real classrooms. It works with children and adults, with facts and with ideas.

It works best when you get feedback, so that you can correct what you got wrong. It works even when you fail, as we saw with pretesting. And it works better when the questions need some effort, such as writing a short answer, than when they're simple recognition. The one catch is that it feels harder than rereading, so many people avoid it. A good rule is to spend at least half of your study time on trying to remember, and not on looking again.

# Wise feedback
@src Yeager et al., 2014

In 2014 David Yeager and his colleagues studied what happens when students receive critical feedback on an essay. Seventh graders wrote essays, and their teachers wrote comments in the margins. Some students also received a note that said, "I'm giving you these comments because I have very high expectations and I know that you can reach them." The note was the only difference.

Among Black students, who had often had reason to doubt that critical feedback was fair, about 72 percent revised their essays when they got the note, compared with 17 percent of those who got a bland note. The words didn't make the comments any nicer. They made it clear that the comments were a sign of respect and belief. It's a lesson for anyone who gives feedback. People respond to criticism when they trust that it comes with high expectations and with confidence in them.

# Mindset: a good idea with modest effects
@src Dweck, 2006; Sisk et al., 2018
@conf mixed

Carol Dweck's research on mindset, which she described in her 2006 book, argued that people who believe that ability can grow tend to take on challenges and recover from setbacks better than people who think that ability is fixed. Her early studies with schoolchildren were striking, and the idea spread through schools and companies around the world.

Later work has been more sober. A 2018 analysis by Victoria Sisk and her colleagues combined hundreds of studies and found that the link between mindset and achievement is weak, and that short mindset programs have small effects on average, though they may help students who are struggling. The idea isn't wrong, and it's fine to believe that effort improves ability. But it's not a cure-all, and praising effort doesn't help much if the strategies that you teach don't work.

# Making a plan with if and then
@src Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006

Most people who set a goal fail to act on it, not because they've changed their minds, but because they never decide when and where to start. The psychologist Peter Gollwitzer found that a simple change closes much of the gap. Instead of "I'll exercise more," you write, "If it's 7 a.m. on a weekday, then I'll put on my running shoes and go out the door." He called them implementation intentions.

In 2006 Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran combined 94 studies, covering thousands of people, and found that if-then plans had a medium to large effect on reaching goals. The plan works because it hands the decision to the situation. When the cue appears, you don't have to decide or summon willpower. It's easy to try. Pick one goal, choose a clear cue, and write the sentence down.

# Picturing the obstacle
@src Oettingen, 2014

It feels motivating to picture yourself succeeding, but the psychologist Gabriele Oettingen found that positive fantasies about the future can lower effort. In studies of people trying to lose weight, find jobs or recover from surgery, those who dreamed most vividly of success did worse than those who were more sober about the road ahead.

What worked better was mental contrasting. You imagine the wish, such as passing an exam, and then you picture the main obstacle in your way, such as the temptation to scroll your phone, and then you plan how you'll deal with it. The sequence is sometimes called WOOP, for wish, outcome, obstacle and plan. It gives you the benefit of hope without letting hope stand in for work.

# Talking to yourself like a coach
@src Kross et al., 2014

When we're upset, most of us talk to ourselves in the first person: "Why do I keep making this mistake?" In 2014 Ethan Kross and his colleagues found that it helped to change the pronoun. People who prepared for a stressful speech by talking to themselves using their own name or "you," such as "Jacob, you can do this," were less anxious, performed better and looked back on it more calmly.

The explanation is distance. Talking about yourself as another person lets you see your situation more as you would see a friend's, with more calm and less drama. It's a simple, free technique. Next time you're stuck in a loop of worry, try describing the problem in the third person, and ask what advice you'd give someone else who was in the same position.

# Reflecting versus ruminating
@src Trapnell & Campbell, 1999

Thinking about yourself can help or hurt. In 1999 Paul Trapnell and Jennifer Campbell described two kinds of self-focus. Reflection is curiosity about yourself, such as wondering why you react as you do, and what you can learn from it. Rumination is going round the same worries and past mistakes, and it comes with a sense of being stuck and hurt. People who ruminate tend to be more anxious and more depressed, and those who reflect tend to be more open.

The difference lies in the questions. "Why am I such a failure?" invites rumination. "What happened, and what can I do differently next time?" invites reflection. When you notice a loop starting, try changing the question to one that points to action or to a lesson. It doesn't stop the feelings, but it points them somewhere useful.

# Reflection pays off
@src Di Stefano, Gino, Pisano & Staats, working paper, 2014

Does thinking about what you've learned help you to learn it? In 2014 Giada Di Stefano and her colleagues tested the idea in a training program for employees at a tech-support call center run by Wipro in Bangalore, India. Everyone did the same training. One group was asked to spend the last 15 minutes of each day writing down and reflecting on what they'd learned. The others kept practicing instead.

On a final test, the reflection group scored about 23 percent higher than the practice group. The extra practice would have felt more productive, but pausing to put the lessons into words did more. Reflecting makes you consolidate what you did, notice what worked and decide what to do next time. It's a cheap habit, a few minutes at the end of a day, and one of the few that has been shown to pay off in a real workplace. The result comes from a working paper that hasn't yet been repeated independently, so it's best seen as a promising finding.

# The mistake of choosing not to notice
@src Johansson & Hall, Science, 2005

In 2005 Petter Johansson and Lars Hall ran an experiment that has been called choice blindness. People were shown pairs of photographs of faces and asked to choose which one was more attractive. After each choice, the experimenter handed over the chosen photograph and asked them to explain why they'd picked it. On a few trials, a magician's trick swapped the photographs, so that people were holding the face that they'd rejected.

Most people didn't notice. And many of them went on to give detailed reasons for a choice that they hadn't made, such as "I like her smile." The result suggests that we often don't know why we do what we do, and that we build the reasons afterward. It's a humbling thing to know about your own explanations. A person's story about their motives can be sincere and still wrong.

# We know less about our reasons than we think
@src Nisbett & Wilson, Psychological Review, 1977

In 1977 Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson published a paper whose title gave the game away: "Telling more than we can know." They reviewed experiments in which people were influenced by things they didn't notice, such as the position of an item on a table, and then gave reasons for their choice that had nothing to do with it. Shoppers who picked the pair of stockings on the right side of the display, which most people did, said that they'd chosen on quality.

When people were asked why they'd done something, they didn't look inside and report. They did what an outside observer might do, and offered a plausible theory about themselves. Sometimes it was right and sometimes it wasn't. It's a warning about trusting a first answer when you ask yourself "why did I do that?" You are working out a likely story, and it's worth considering a few other explanations.

# Predicting how you'll feel
@src Gilbert et al., 1998; Wilson & Gilbert, 2005

Daniel Gilbert and Timothy Wilson have spent decades studying a mistake that nearly everyone makes: we're poor at predicting how we'll feel about future events. They call it the impact bias. We overestimate how happy a win will make us and how miserable a loss will, and we overestimate how long the feeling will last.

In one classic study from 1998, they asked junior professors how they'd feel if they were denied tenure, and checked on those who were. Some time later, the people who had been turned down were about as happy as those who had been promoted. We forget how skillful the mind is at making sense of bad news, and at getting used to good news. It's worth remembering when you're dreading a decision, or when you're sure that something will change your life.

# Seeing yourself from the outside
@src Bem, 1972

In 1972 the psychologist Daryl Bem proposed an idea that turned common sense upside down. We don't always know our attitudes by looking inside, he said. Often we learn them the way that we learn about other people, by watching what we do and drawing a conclusion. If you notice that you keep going to the gym, you decide you must like exercise. If you see yourself giving money to a charity, you decide you're a generous person.

The theory helps explain why small actions can shift how we see ourselves. A person who volunteers once may start to think of themselves as a volunteer. It's also a nice partner to cognitive dissonance, which explains some of the same findings in a different way. The practical side is that, if you want to see yourself differently, one of the best ways is to act differently first, and let your view catch up.

# Hearing yourself think
@src Schooler et al., Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 2011

Metacognition includes a skill that's easy to overlook: noticing that your mind has wandered. Psychologists call it meta-awareness. When you're reading and your eyes keep moving across the page while your mind is on dinner, there's a moment when you realize it. Researchers study this by stopping people at random moments and asking whether they were on task, or by having them press a button whenever they catch themselves drifting.

People vary a lot in how often they catch themselves, and a good deal of reading goes by without anyone noticing that the words aren't going in. Mindfulness training has been found to reduce mind wandering in some studies, and so has the simple habit of asking yourself from time to time, "Where is my mind right now?" That small act of noticing is itself metacognition. You can't steer your attention until you know where it has gone.

# The proofreading problem
@src Daneman & Stainton, 1993

Writers know that it's hard to find the mistakes in your own work. In 1993 Meredyth Daneman and Murray Stainton found that people spot fewer errors in a text they wrote themselves than in the same errors planted in somebody else's text. You can read a paragraph five times and still miss a repeated word. One reason is that you know what you meant to say, so your mind fills in the sense even when the page says something else. Your expectation is stronger than the evidence in front of you.

You can fight it with tricks that break the pattern. Read it aloud, since your mouth is less forgiving than your eyes. Read it backward, one sentence at a time. Change the font, print it out, or leave it for a day and come back with fresh eyes. These work because they make the text strange again. The same idea applies to checking math, code and plans. Review them in a different way from the one that you used to create them.

# Tired people can't tell how tired they are
@src Van Dongen et al., Sleep, 2003; Pilcher & Huffcutt, 1996

Sleep loss has a sneaky effect. It makes us worse at many tasks, and it also seems to make us worse at noticing that we are worse. In a 2003 study led by Hans Van Dongen, healthy adults were limited to four or six hours of sleep a night for two weeks. Their scores on tests of attention kept falling day after day, but their own ratings of how sleepy they felt rose only a little and then leveled off. They had gotten used to being tired and stopped seeing how much it was costing them.

A 1996 review by June Pilcher and Allen Huffcutt found that sleep loss has a large effect on mood and thinking. The lesson for metacognition is humbling. When you've been short of sleep, you can't trust your sense of how well you are doing, and it's better to rely on checklists, double checks and other people's opinions.

# Using the outside world as part of your mind
@src Sparrow, Liu & Wegner, Science, 2011
@conf mixed

In 2011 Betsy Sparrow and her colleagues reported that when people expected to be able to look something up later, they remembered less of it, but remembered better where to find it. The paper was called "Google effects on memory," and it was widely taken to show that the internet is making us forgetful.

The result has been hard to repeat. When a large team tried to replicate the study in 2018, it didn't get the same effect. Still, the broad idea holds some truth, and it's an old one. Humans have always used notebooks, calendars and other people to store what they don't need to carry in their heads. The metacognitive skill is knowing what you can safely offload, such as a phone number, and what you need to understand yourself.

# Thinking out loud
@src Ericsson & Simon, 1980

How can we study what goes on in a person's head? In 1980 Anders Ericsson and Herbert Simon described a method called the think-aloud protocol. You ask a person to say whatever's going through their mind as they solve a problem, without explaining or justifying it. The recordings show where people get stuck, what they notice and which strategies they try.

The method has been used to study chess players, doctors, readers and programmers. It also works as a learning tool. When you talk through a problem out loud, you slow down, you notice your assumptions and you catch mistakes that silent thinking skips over. It's why a friend's question, "What are you thinking?" is often useful. Putting thought into words makes it visible, and what's visible can be checked.

# Knowing when you're done
@src Dunlosky & Metcalfe, Metacognition, 2009

One of the quiet skills in studying is knowing when to stop. Students who stop too soon fail the test, and students who study far past the point of mastery waste time that they could spend on something else. Research by John Dunlosky and others shows that most learners don't stop at the right time, because they use poor signals, such as how familiar the material feels.

A better signal is a test. Try to recall everything without looking, and check the answers. Items that you get right on a delayed test can be set aside for a while, and the ones that you miss go back in the pile. The approach turns a vague feeling of readiness into a simple fact. It also makes studying shorter and more satisfying, since you spend your time on what you don't yet know.

# Mistakes are information
@src Gehring et al., Psychological Science, 1993

When you press a wrong key, your brain knows before you do. In 1993 William Gehring and his colleagues recorded brain activity from people doing a quick test, and found a distinctive electrical signal that appeared within a tenth of a second after an error, even when the person was not yet aware of making one. It was called the error-related negativity.

The signal seems to come from the brain's performance-monitoring system, which helps you slow down and correct course. People who respond to errors with curiosity, and not with shame, seem to learn more from them. In some studies, a stronger error signal goes with better learning from the mistake. The idea fits nicely with what teachers say. A mistake isn't just a failure. It's a message from your own brain about what to fix.

# Thinking about how you think about others
@src Premack & Woodruff, 1978; Wimmer & Perner, 1983

One of the first forms of metacognition is realizing that other people have minds that differ from yours. In 1978 David Premack and Guy Woodruff called this theory of mind, and later researchers found that children develop it around the age of four. In a classic test, a child sees a character hide a toy in one box and leave, then watches the toy be moved to another box. Four-year-olds can say that the character will look in the first box. Three-year-olds say the second.

The same skill underlies every conversation, since you can't explain something well or sense when you've offended someone without a model of what they know and feel. Adults still make errors, such as assuming that others know what we know. That's the curse of knowledge, and it's a reminder that thinking about other minds is a skill that can be practiced all through life.

# Good learners ask better questions
@src Rosenshine, Meister & Chapman, 1996

In 1996 Barak Rosenshine and his colleagues reviewed 26 studies in which students were taught to ask themselves questions as they read. The students might be told to ask, "Who is the main character?" or "Why did this happen?" or "How does this connect to what I already know?" They found that learners who generated their own questions understood and remembered more than those who simply read.

The questions work as a monitor. If you can't answer one, you've found a gap, and if you can, you've practiced retrieval. The most helpful questions begin with why and how, and not with what, because they push you to connect ideas. A simple routine is to turn each heading of a chapter into a question, read to find the answer, and then say it aloud without looking.

# Keeping a record of your own learning
@src Zimmerman, 2002

Barry Zimmerman, a psychologist who studied self-regulated learning for decades, described good learners as running a cycle. Before a task, they set a goal and make a plan. During it, they watch their own progress. Afterward, they judge how it went and decide what to change. This cycle of forethought, performance and reflection runs through everything from homework to athletic training.

The learners who do this tend to do better, and the cycle can be taught. A simple version is a notebook. Before you study, write down what you plan to do and how you'll know you've succeeded. After, note what worked and what didn't. Looking back over a few weeks, you can see patterns that you'd never notice day by day, such as which times of day you concentrate, and which methods actually help you.
