# Marie Curie
@src Nobel Prize records

Marie Sklodowska was born in Warsaw and moved to Paris to study, and she went on to a career that nobody has matched. She worked in poverty at first, and in a cold, cramped lab. In 1903 she shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with her husband Pierre and Henri Becquerel for their work on radioactivity, a word she coined.

In 1911 she won the Chemistry prize for discovering polonium and radium, which made her the first person to win two Nobel Prizes. In the First World War she helped equip vehicles with X-ray machines, which soldiers called "little Curies," and she drove some of them to the front herself. She died in 1934 of an illness that was almost certainly caused by her years of work with radiation. Her notebooks are still radioactive today.

# Galileo Galilei
@src Galileo, Sidereus Nuncius (1610)

In 1609 Galileo heard of a Dutch invention and built a better telescope himself, then turned it to the sky. What he saw was astonishing. There were mountains and craters on the Moon, four small moons circling Jupiter and the phases of Venus. All of this was evidence that not everything in the heavens circled the Earth.

He published his findings in 1610 in a short book, and he became famous. But he also became a target. In 1633 the Inquisition forced him to renounce the Sun-centered view, and he spent the rest of his life under house arrest. Even then, he kept working, and he wrote one of his best books while confined. Galileo's lasting legacy is the habit of looking and measuring, instead of simply trusting an authority.

# Nicolaus Copernicus
@src Copernicus, 1543

For more than a thousand years most people believed that the Sun and planets circled the Earth. It matched what anyone could see, and it was supported by the authority of ancient writers. Copernicus, a Polish astronomer and church official, worked out in detail a system with the Sun at the center.

His book On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres was printed in 1543, and according to tradition he was handed a copy on his deathbed. The idea wasn't entirely new, since an ancient Greek, Aristarchus, had suggested it, but Copernicus supplied the mathematics. He had waited decades to publish, perhaps from worry about how it would be received. The book started what we now call the Copernican revolution, a change in how we see our place in the universe.

# Johannes Kepler
@src Kepler, 1609, 1619

Kepler, a German astronomer, had access to the careful naked-eye observations of Tycho Brahe, and spent years trying to fit Mars's orbit to a circle. It wouldn't fit. The error was small, only about eight minutes of arc, but Kepler trusted the data and refused to ignore it.

Eventually he realized the orbit was an ellipse. He published three laws of planetary motion between 1609 and 1619, which Isaac Newton later used to arrive at his theory of gravity. Kepler also wrote an early work of science fiction about a trip to the Moon, called Somnium. It's a nice example of how a scientist can be both rigorous and imaginative, and of how a small discrepancy, honestly respected, can overturn a belief that had stood for two thousand years.

# Charles Darwin
@src Darwin, 1859

Darwin returned from his five-year voyage on HMS Beagle in 1836 with the beginnings of an idea. For more than twenty years, he gathered evidence and told only a few friends. He was a careful man, and he knew that his theory would be controversial.

In 1858 he received a letter from the naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace, who had independently come to the same idea. A joint paper was read to the Linnean Society, and Darwin's book On the Origin of Species came out in 1859. The first printing sold out in a day. He spent the rest of his life writing more, including books on orchids, earthworms and human emotion. He is buried in Westminster Abbey, near Isaac Newton.

# Alfred Russel Wallace
@src Wallace, 1858

Wallace was a self-taught naturalist who made a living collecting specimens in the Amazon and in Southeast Asia. He wasn't wealthy or well connected, and he sold beetles and birds to museums and collectors in order to pay for his travels.

In 1858, lying sick with a fever on an island in Indonesia, he thought of natural selection, wrote it up in a few days and sent it to Darwin. Darwin was alarmed to see his own theory, and the two agreed to publish together. Wallace also found that animals on either side of a line through Indonesia are strikingly different, now called Wallace's Line. He was generous about credit all his life, and he even titled one of his books Darwinism. Many consider him the unsung co-discoverer of evolution.

# Louis Pasteur
@src Pasteur, 1861, 1885

In the 1860s the French chemist Louis Pasteur showed that microbes, not "spontaneous generation," were responsible for fermentation and spoilage. He boiled broth in flasks with long swan-shaped necks, which let in air but trapped dust and germs, and the broth stayed clear.

That supported germ theory, the idea that germs cause disease. He went on to develop vaccines for animals, including chicken cholera and anthrax. In 1885 he gave a boy named Joseph Meister, who'd been bitten by a rabid dog, the first rabies vaccine, and the boy lived. Pasteur also invented a way of heating milk and wine to kill germs, which we still call pasteurization. He's a good reminder that the most useful science often starts with a practical problem.

# Joseph Lister
@src Lister, 1867

In the 1860s surgeons in hospitals operated in street clothes and wiped their instruments on their aprons, and many patients died of infection afterward. Many surgeons took pride in their blood-stained coats. Nobody understood why wounds became infected.

The British surgeon Joseph Lister read about Pasteur's work on germs and began to clean wounds and tools with carbolic acid. In 1867 he reported that deaths among his amputation patients fell from about 45 percent to about 15 percent. He also sprayed the operating room with the acid, which later proved unnecessary, but the principle was right. The mouthwash Listerine was named after him, though he had nothing to do with it. Modern surgery depends on the cleanliness that he insisted on.

# Ignaz Semmelweis
@src Semmelweis, 1861

In 1847 the Hungarian doctor Ignaz Semmelweis noticed that women giving birth in the hospital ward where doctors worked were dying of fever at a much higher rate than in the ward staffed by midwives. He puzzled over the difference, and when a colleague died after being cut during an autopsy, he saw a link.

He realized that doctors were coming straight from autopsies, and he told them to wash their hands in chlorine solution. Deaths fell from about 18 percent to about 2 percent. His colleagues rejected the idea, partly because it implied that doctors were killing their patients, and he ended his life in an asylum. Only after his death did germ theory vindicate him. His story is a lesson in how hard it can be to accept a truth that's uncomfortable.

# Edward Jenner
@src Jenner, 1798

In the 1790s the English country doctor Edward Jenner heard that milkmaids who'd caught cowpox seemed not to get smallpox. Smallpox killed and disfigured millions, and a way to prevent it would be a great gift. In 1796 he took matter from a cowpox sore and scratched it into the arm of an eight-year-old boy, James Phipps.

Later he exposed the boy to smallpox, and the boy didn't catch it. Jenner would not pass modern standards of research ethics, but his method worked. The word "vaccine" comes from the Latin word for cow, vacca. Smallpox was eventually wiped out worldwide, and the last natural case was in 1977. It is the only human disease so far to have been completely eradicated, and Jenner's experiment started the road to that victory.

# Florence Nightingale
@src Nightingale, 1858

Florence Nightingale is remembered as the "Lady with the Lamp," a nurse in the Crimean War, but she was also a brilliant statistician. She gathered data and drew striking charts, including a polar-area diagram, to show that far more soldiers died of disease than of battle wounds.

After sanitary reforms at the hospital at Scutari, the death rate dropped from over 40 percent to about 2 percent. She used her charts to persuade officials and the public, since a picture could succeed where a table of numbers could not. In 1858 she became the first woman elected to the Royal Statistical Society. She spent the rest of her long life as a reformer of nursing and public health, largely working from her bed.

# Ada Lovelace
@src Lovelace, 1843

Ada Lovelace was the daughter of the poet Lord Byron, and she studied mathematics. Her mother, worried that she would inherit her father's wildness, made sure she had a thorough training in science and numbers.

She translated an article about Charles Babbage's proposed Analytical Engine, a mechanical computer that was never completed, and added her own notes in 1843. They included a step-by-step method for the machine to calculate Bernoulli numbers, which is regarded as the first computer program. She also saw that such a machine could work with symbols and even music, not just numbers. That vision, of a computer as something more than a calculator, was a century ahead of its time.

# Alan Turing
@src Turing, 1936, 1950

In 1936 the young British mathematician Alan Turing described an imaginary machine that could carry out any calculation, a model of what we now call a computer. He was 24, and he made it up to solve a problem in pure logic.

In the Second World War, he worked at Bletchley Park and helped break the German naval Enigma code, work that is thought to have shortened the war. In 1950 he asked whether machines can think, and proposed a test. He was convicted in 1952 for being gay, and the British government pardoned him in 2013. He died in 1954, at the age of 41. The most important prize in computer science, the Turing Award, is named for him.

# Grace Hopper
@src Hopper, 1952

Grace Hopper was a mathematician who became a rear admiral in the US Navy. In 1952 she created one of the first compilers, a program that translates instructions into machine code, and she argued that programming should be done in words close to English, which led to the language COBOL.

In 1947, when a moth was found stuck in a relay of the Harvard Mark II computer, she taped it into the logbook as the "first actual case of bug being found." The term "bug" was already used in engineering, but the story helped make it famous in computing. She was known for handing out short lengths of wire, a nanosecond long, to show how far light travels in a billionth of a second. She was still on active duty in her late seventies.

# Katherine Johnson
@src NASA

Katherine Johnson was a mathematician at NASA who worked as a human "computer" at a time when Black women were segregated at the agency. She had been a gifted student, starting college at 15, and she brought exceptional skill to the job.

She calculated the flight path for Alan Shepard's 1961 mission. In 1962, before John Glenn's orbital flight, the astronaut asked for "the girl" to check the computer's numbers by hand, and he wouldn't fly until she had. President Obama gave her the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2015. She lived to the age of 101. Her story was told in the book and film Hidden Figures, which brought attention to the many women whose work helped put people into space.

# Rosalind Franklin
@src Franklin & Gosling, 1953

The British chemist Rosalind Franklin was an expert in X-ray crystallography. In 1952 she and her student Raymond Gosling produced "Photo 51," a sharp X-ray image of DNA that showed a helical pattern.

Without her knowledge, a colleague showed it to James Watson, and it gave him and Francis Crick crucial information for their 1953 model. She was close to solving the structure herself. She died of ovarian cancer in 1958, at 37, and the Nobel Prize isn't awarded posthumously. Her work before DNA, on coal and viruses, was also important. Today she is widely recognized as one of the key figures in the discovery of the double helix.

# Barbara McClintock
@src McClintock, 1950; Nobel, 1983

Barbara McClintock studied corn, and from the 1940s she noticed that certain genes could move from one place on a chromosome to another, changing the color patterns of the kernels. It was a radical idea, since genes were thought to be fixed in place like beads on a string.

Most scientists didn't believe her, and she stopped publishing about it for years. Decades later, other researchers found similar "jumping genes" in many organisms, including humans. She won the 1983 Nobel Prize in Medicine, alone, at the age of 81. Her story shows the value of patient observation, and the courage to keep believing in what the evidence shows.

# Henrietta Leavitt
@src Leavitt & Pickering, 1912

In the early 1900s, Henrietta Leavitt worked at Harvard College Observatory as one of the women hired to measure photographs of stars, for low pay. They were called "computers," and they did careful, tedious work that the male astronomers did not want to do.

Studying pulsing stars in the Magellanic Clouds, she noticed that the brighter ones took longer to pulse. That gave astronomers a way of measuring distances to faraway stars and galaxies. Edwin Hubble later used it to show that the Andromeda nebula is a separate galaxy. Leavitt died of cancer in 1921, and a Swedish mathematician who wanted to nominate her for a Nobel Prize learned that she had already died. She is a fine example of an overlooked person whose work changed the way we see the universe.

# Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin
@src Payne, 1925

In 1925 Cecilia Payne, then a graduate student at Harvard, concluded in her PhD thesis that stars are made mostly of hydrogen and helium, not of the same mix as the Earth, as scientists had assumed. It was a startling result, and it went against what everyone thought.

A prominent astronomer, Henry Norris Russell, persuaded her to describe the result as probably not real. Four years later, he reached the same conclusion by other means and published it, giving her credit. The astronomer Otto Struve called her thesis the most brilliant ever in astronomy. She went on to become the first woman to be a full professor at Harvard, and later the head of a department there.

# Chien-Shiung Wu
@src Wu et al., 1957

Physicists believed that nature doesn't distinguish left from right. If you looked at an experiment in a mirror, the laws would look the same. In 1956 Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang suggested that it might not hold for the weak nuclear force and needed someone to test it.

Chien-Shiung Wu, a Chinese-American experimentalist at Columbia, ran a delicate experiment on cobalt-60 at very low temperatures and found that it did break the symmetry. Lee and Yang won the 1957 Nobel Prize in Physics, but she did not. Many physicists have said that she should have shared it. She was nicknamed the "First Lady of Physics," and she also worked on the Manhattan Project. Her careful experiment overturned an idea that had seemed beyond question.
