# The brain at rest is busy
@src Raichle et al., PNAS, 2001; Buckner, Andrews-Hanna & Schacter, 2008

When researchers first used brain scans, they looked at which regions turned on during a task. They compared that with a resting baseline, as if the resting brain were switched off. In 2001 Marcus Raichle and his colleagues pointed out something odd. A group of regions was more active when people were doing nothing in particular than when they were focused on a task.

They called this a default mode of brain function, and the regions became known as the default mode network. It is active when we daydream, remember the past, imagine the future or think about other people. A task adds only a small amount to the brain's total energy use, because the brain is always working. The mind at rest isn't blank. It's busy with the story of who you are and what's going on.

# The London taxi drivers
@src Maguire et al., PNAS, 2000; Woollett & Maguire, Current Biology, 2011

To get a license, London taxi drivers must learn the Knowledge, a map of some twenty-five thousand streets, and it takes years. In 2000 Eleanor Maguire and her colleagues scanned the brains of licensed drivers. The back part of their hippocampus, a region involved in spatial memory, was larger than in other people, and it was larger in those who had been driving the longest.

That left a question. Were people with large hippocampi more likely to become taxi drivers, or did the training change the brain? In 2011 Katherine Woollett and Maguire scanned trainees before and after. Those who went on to qualify showed an increase in gray matter in the posterior hippocampus, and those who did not qualify did not. It's among the clearest demonstrations that adult brains can change with learning.

# Mirror neurons
@src di Pellegrino et al., 1992; Gallese et al., 1996
@conf mixed

In the early 1990s a team in Parma, Italy, led by Giacomo Rizzolatti, was recording from single cells in the premotor cortex of macaque monkeys. They found cells that fired both when a monkey grasped a piece of food and when it watched someone else do the same thing. The cells were named mirror neurons, and the discovery was widely celebrated.

Some people went further and said mirror neurons explain empathy, language and autism. Those claims are much shakier. Evidence for such cells in people is mostly indirect, and the link to understanding other people's minds has not been shown. Many neuroscientists think that mirror cells are real and interesting, and that they help to link seeing an action with doing it. They do not think that they explain the whole of social understanding.

# Where feelings live in the body
@src Nummenmaa et al., PNAS, 2014
@conf mixed

Where do you feel love, or shame, or anger? In 2014 Lauri Nummenmaa and his colleagues asked more than seven hundred people to color in outlines of a human body to show where they felt their sensations when they experienced different emotions. The maps for each emotion were distinct, and they were similar for people in Finland, Sweden and Taiwan.

Anger and fear showed up in the chest and head, and love and happiness spread through the whole body. Depression seemed to reduce sensations in the limbs. The results are based on what people said, not on measurements of the body, so they show what people believe and feel and not necessarily what the body does. But they suggest that emotions are tied to the body in ways that are shared across cultures, and that we may read our own feelings partly through our physical states.

# The placebo that releases opioids
@src Levine, Gordon & Fields, Lancet, 1978

In 1978 Jon Levine and his colleagues studied people who had had a wisdom tooth removed. Some were given a placebo, a harmless inactive injection. Many reported less pain after it. Then the researchers gave some of them naloxone, a drug that blocks opioid receptors, the sites where the body's own painkillers and morphine act.

People who got naloxone lost the relief that the placebo had given them. This suggested that the placebo had worked by releasing the body's own endorphins, and was not just a matter of people being polite or imagining it. It was one of the first findings to show that expectations can turn on real chemistry in the brain. Placebos can't shrink a tumor or cure an infection, but they can change how pain is felt.

# One in ten is left-handed
@src Papadatou-Pastou et al., Psychological Bulletin, 2020

In 2020 Marietta Papadatou-Pastou and her colleagues combined the results of a very large body of studies, covering more than two million people, to estimate how many people are left-handed. The answer was about ten percent. The number varies a little with how handedness is measured, and it differs slightly by sex, with more left-handed men than women.

Handedness appears early, before birth, since a fetus tends to suck one thumb more than the other. It runs in families, but not simply. Two right-handed parents can have a left-handed child, and genes explain only a part of the variation. The idea that left-handers are more creative or die younger has been tested, and it is not supported. Left-handedness is a normal variation that has been around for a long time.

# Faces that don't register
@src Bowles et al., Cognitive Neuropsychology, 2009

Imagine seeing your own friends and family and not being able to tell who they are by their faces. Prosopagnosia, or face blindness, can happen after a brain injury. But it also exists in people who have had it all their lives, and don't have any other problem with vision or memory. In 2009 Donna Bowles and her colleagues estimated that about two or three percent of the population had it.

Many people with the condition work out ways to cope. They use hair, voice, clothing or the way someone walks. They may not realize that other people see faces differently, until they are adults. It runs in families, which suggests a genetic part. The condition is a reminder that face recognition is a specialized skill, and that most of us do it so easily that we don't realize it is a skill.

# Perfect pitch
@src Deutsch et al., Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, 2006

A few people can name a note the moment they hear it, without a reference, like naming a color. This is absolute pitch, and it is rare in the West. For a long time, the explanation was that it needs early musical training in a critical window in childhood, and perhaps a gene for it.

In 2006 Diana Deutsch and her colleagues compared students at music conservatories in Beijing and in the United States. Among those who began training at a young age, absolute pitch was several times more common in the Chinese students than in the American ones. The authors suggested that growing up speaking a tonal language, where the pitch of a syllable changes its meaning, makes it easier to attach labels to pitches. Early exposure seems to matter along with whatever genes are involved.

# The Stroop effect
@src Stroop, Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1935

Look at the word RED printed in blue ink, and try to say the color of the ink, not the word. It is harder than it sounds. In 1935 John Ridley Stroop showed that people are slower and make more errors when the word and the color disagree than when they agree or when the word is simply a patch of color.

The reason is that reading is so automatic that it happens whether we want it to or not. The word interferes with the task of naming the ink. The Stroop task has been used for decades as a test of attention and cognitive control, since it shows how much effort it takes to override an automatic response. It's a good example of how much of what the mind does is not under our control.

# Dementia risks you can change
@src Livingston et al., Lancet Commission, 2020 and 2024

In 2020 an international commission led by Gill Livingston reviewed the evidence on dementia. It listed twelve risk factors that people can do something about. They include less education early in life, hearing loss, high blood pressure, obesity, smoking, depression, social isolation, physical inactivity, diabetes, heavy drinking, head injury and air pollution.

In 2024 an update added two more, high LDL cholesterol and untreated vision loss. The commission estimated that nearly half of the dementia cases in the world might be prevented or delayed if all fourteen could be removed. That is a theoretical ceiling and it assumes that the links are causal, but the direction is encouraging. Many of the steps, such as treating hearing loss, keeping active and staying connected to others, are also good for you in other ways.

# The bilingual advantage
@src Bialystok, Craik & Freedman, Neuropsychologia, 2007; Lehtonen et al., 2018
@conf contested

For years there was a popular claim that speaking two languages makes the brain sharper, because a bilingual person has to switch and hold back one language while using the other. In 2007 Ellen Bialystok and her colleagues reported that bilingual people in Toronto showed the first signs of dementia about four years later than people who spoke one language.

The claim has been challenged. Later studies found that bilingual children and adults often did not do better on tests of attention and control, and some of the early positive results may have been due to publication bias and differences between the groups. A large review in 2018 found little evidence for an advantage in adults. The dementia finding is also disputed. Learning a language is still worth it for many reasons. Whether it improves your brain in general is unclear.

# Do music lessons make you smarter?
@src Schellenberg, Psychological Science, 2004; Sala & Gobet, 2020
@conf mixed

In 2004 Glenn Schellenberg assigned six year olds to a year of keyboard or voice lessons, drama lessons or no lessons. At the end, children in all groups had gained in IQ, but those with music lessons gained a little more. The finding was widely taken to mean that music lessons make children smarter.

Later studies have found it hard to repeat. A large analysis in 2020 by Giovanni Sala and Fernand Gobet found that when studies were well controlled, music training had little effect on general thinking skills or school results. Children who take lessons often come from families that value education, and who may have higher scores to begin with. Music has its own value, in enjoyment and skill, and doesn't have to raise IQ to be worth learning.

# Action games and attention
@src Green & Bavelier, Nature, 2003
@conf mixed

In 2003 Shawn Green and Daphne Bavelier reported that people who played action video games had better visual attention. They were quicker to find a target among distractors and could keep track of more objects at once. In another part of the study, non-players who were trained for ten days on an action game improved, and those trained on a slow puzzle game did not.

The findings drew a lot of interest, and the idea that games could train attention was taken up by educators. Later work has been more mixed. Some studies found that gamers' advantage is partly due to who chooses to play, and not all training studies could repeat the benefit. Effects on everyday life are less clear than effects on the tasks that were used. It remains a topic where the results are suggestive and not settled.

# The 25-year-old brain
@src Gogtay et al., PNAS, 2004; Sawyer et al., Lancet Child & Adolescent Health, 2018
@conf mixed

You may have heard that the brain isn't fully developed until age twenty-five. The idea comes from imaging studies, such as one in 2004 by Nitin Gogtay and his colleagues. They scanned the same young people over years, and found that gray matter in the brain's frontal regions thinned out later than in other regions, continuing into the early twenties.

But the number twenty-five is not a cutoff. Different parts and different abilities mature at different rates, and the brain keeps changing throughout life. Some researchers have argued that the claim is used too freely, for example to argue about when someone is an adult. A 2018 paper by Susan Sawyer and her colleagues proposed that adolescence be seen as lasting from ten to twenty-four. The fair summary is that young adults are still developing, without a sudden finish line.

# A mosaic, not a male or female brain
@src Joel et al., PNAS, 2015
@conf mixed

Are there male brains and female brains? Many studies have found average differences between the sexes in some brain regions. In 2015 Daphna Joel and her colleagues asked a different question. They looked at MRI scans of more than 1,400 brains and asked whether an individual brain was consistently at the male end or the female end of every feature that differs on average.

They found that most brains were a mosaic. A person could be at the typical female end for some features, the typical male end for others, and in between for the rest. Brains with consistently one set of features were rare. Other researchers criticized the way the team defined consistency. The study is a useful reminder that a difference between averages does not tell you what any one person's brain is like.

# Why music gives you chills
@src Blood & Zatorre, PNAS, 2001; Salimpoor et al., Nature Neuroscience, 2011

Some pieces of music make the hairs on the back of your neck rise, or send a shiver down your spine. In 2001 Anne Blood and Robert Zatorre asked people to bring in music that gave them chills, and scanned their brains as they listened. The chills went along with changes in the same brain regions that respond to food, sex and drugs.

In 2011 Valorie Salimpoor and her colleagues went further. They found that the brain released dopamine, a chemical linked to reward, both during the peak moments and in the seconds before, as people anticipated a favorite passage. The pleasure of music seems to come from prediction as well as from the notes. A piece builds an expectation and then meets it, delays it or surprises us, and the brain's reward system follows along.

# The dress
@src Lafer-Sousa, Hermann & Conway, Current Biology, 2015

In 2015 a photograph of a dress went around the internet. Some people saw it as blue and black. Others saw it as white and gold. Each group was sure that the other was wrong. Scientists took notice, because the photo was an ordinary picture, and yet people couldn't agree about its color.

The explanation that most researchers favor is about assumptions. Our brains automatically discount the color of the light, so we can see an object's color as constant under different lighting. In this photograph, the lighting is unclear. If your brain assumes that the dress is in shadow, it takes away blue and you see white and gold. If it assumes warm light, it takes away yellow and you see blue and black. The difference is in how the brain solves an ambiguous problem.

# Alone with their thoughts
@src Wilson et al., Science, 2014
@conf mixed

In 2014 Timothy Wilson and his colleagues asked people to sit alone in a plain room for between six and fifteen minutes, with nothing to do but think. Most said that they didn't enjoy it, and found it hard to concentrate. In one study, people could choose to give themselves a mild electric shock, something that they had already said they would pay to avoid.

About two thirds of the men, and a quarter of the women, did so at least once. The authors concluded that many people find it unpleasant to be alone with their thoughts. Critics have said that the setting was strange, and that people reported mixed feelings, and not just boredom. It would be wrong to take it as proof that we can't tolerate quiet. But it suggests that when a phone is easy to reach, we may be giving up more stillness than we know.

# Yawns are catching
@src Provine, Bulletin of the Psychonomic Society, 1986
@conf mixed

Have you yawned while reading about yawning? In 1986 Robert Provine showed that seeing, hearing or reading about a yawn can make people yawn. In his work, about half of the people who watched a video of someone yawning yawned themselves within minutes. Contagious yawning isn't unique to people. It has been seen in chimpanzees and dogs.

A popular explanation is that it reflects empathy, since people who score lower on empathy tests seem to yawn less in response. Not all the studies agree, and some researchers think that it may be more a matter of attention and arousal, or of copying behavior without feeling anything. Young children don't catch yawns until about the age of four or five. The reason for yawning itself is also still debated.

# The tickle you can't give yourself
@src Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith, Nature Neuroscience, 1998

Why can't you tickle yourself? In 1998 Sarah-Jayne Blakemore, Daniel Wolpert and Chris Frith studied this with a brain scanner. They compared the brain's response when a person touched their own palm with a soft object, and when a machine did it with the same movement. The self-touch caused less activity in the region that processes touch and pain, and it felt less ticklish.

The brain predicts the sensory results of its own movements, and it turns down the signal when the result is as expected. The cerebellum seems to be involved in making that prediction. This is useful, because it lets us ignore the constant feeling of our own movements and notice things that come from outside. If a delay was added between the movement and the touch, the sensation grew ticklish again.

# The rubber hand
@src Botvinick & Cohen, Nature, 1998

In 1998 Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen did a simple experiment. A person sat with one hand hidden behind a screen, and a rubber hand placed in front of them in a natural position. The experimenter stroked the hidden real hand and the visible rubber hand at the same time with a brush. After a minute or so, many people felt as if the rubber hand were their own.

When the strokes were out of sync, the illusion didn't happen. Some people even flinched when the rubber hand was threatened. The experiment shows that the sense of owning a body part is built by the brain from signals that match, such as sight and touch. It isn't fixed. The brain keeps working out what belongs to you, and with the right cues, it can be fooled.

# Why your recorded voice sounds strange
@src Pörschmann, Acta Acustica, 2000

When you hear a recording of yourself, the voice sounds higher, thinner and not quite yours. Many people dislike it. It isn't that the recording is wrong. When you talk, you hear your voice in two ways. One is through the air, which is how other people hear it. The other is through the bones of your skull, which carry vibrations from your vocal cords straight to the inner ear.

Bone conduction adds low frequencies and makes your voice sound deeper and fuller to you. A recording can't capture that, and so it sounds different from what you're used to. What you hear on the recording is closer to what everyone else hears. It's also why familiarity helps. People who hear their own recorded voice more often tend to get used to it.
