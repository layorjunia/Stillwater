# Arguing it yourself
@src Janis & King, Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1954

If you want to change someone's mind, should you give them an argument to listen to, or ask them to make the argument? In 1954 Irving Janis and Bert King tested this. College students were given a topic, such as the future of movie theaters. Some students listened to a talk. Others were asked to give a talk on the same side themselves, from an outline, as if they were trying to persuade someone else.

The students who gave the talk changed their own opinions more than those who only listened, and they kept the change longer. Speaking for a view, even a view that you were assigned, makes you search for reasons, and you hear yourself say them. It's a reminder that a good way to persuade people is to get them to say the words, and a good way to open your own mind is to argue the other side.

# Saying is believing
@src Higgins & Rholes, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 1978

When you tell a story, you shape it for your listener. In 1978 Tory Higgins and Williams Rholes asked students to read a description of a person whose behavior was ambiguous, with some good points and some bad. They were asked to write a summary for another student who, they were told, either liked or disliked this person. Later, they were asked to recall the original description.

Students who had written for a listener who liked the person gave a positive summary, and those who wrote for a listener who disliked the person gave a negative one. When they recalled the original, their memories had shifted to match what they had said. The saying had changed the believing. Every time we retell something to suit an audience, we may be rewriting our own version of events. It's useful to know when you're talking about something that matters.

# Persuasion in a metaphor
@src Sopory & Dillard, Human Communication Research, 2002

Do metaphors make arguments more persuasive? In 2002 Pradeep Sopory and James Dillard combined the results of dozens of experiments in which the same message was written with a metaphor or in literal language. A message might call a problem a war, or just describe it plainly.

On average, messages with metaphors were somewhat more persuasive. The effect was modest, and it depended on the kind of metaphor and the situation. A good metaphor helps people see a new idea in terms of something familiar. But a metaphor can also hide the weak points of an argument, so it's useful to ask what it leaves out.

# Persuaded over lunch
@src Janis, Kaye & Kirschner, JPSP, 1965

For centuries, deals have been made over meals. In 1965 Irving Janis and his colleagues tested whether eating makes people more open to persuasion. College students were asked to read persuasive articles on several topics. Some of them were given peanuts and a soft drink while they read. The others read without refreshments.

The students who ate while they read were more persuaded by the articles than those who didn't. The researchers suggested that the pleasant feeling of eating became attached to the message. It may also have kept students from arguing back in their heads as they read. The idea that a good meal softens people has some support, and it's known as the luncheon technique. If you want to be wary of persuasion, it helps to know how much of it is the setting.

# Distracted people are easier to persuade
@src Festinger & Maccoby, Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1964

In 1964 Leon Festinger and Nathan Maccoby studied how people react to a speech that goes against their beliefs. Fraternity members listened to a talk that argued against fraternities. For some, the talk was accompanied by an amusing film that had nothing to do with the topic. Others just listened to the talk.

The students who were distracted by the film were persuaded more by the talk than those who listened carefully. The theory was that people who listen closely come up with counterarguments, and the distraction left them less able to think of any. The effect isn't universal, since a distraction can also reduce how much of a message people take in. But it's a clue to a common tactic. A message that is hard to answer back to is often a message that comes with distractions.

# Happy people check less
@src Bless, Bohner, Schwarz & Strack, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 1990

Does your mood affect whether a weak argument persuades you? In 1990 Herbert Bless and his colleagues put people in a good or a sad mood by asking them to recall a happy event or a sad one. Then they listened to a talk that was either made up of strong arguments or of weak ones.

People in a sad mood were persuaded by the strong arguments and not by the weak ones. People in a good mood were persuaded by both. They didn't examine the arguments as carefully. A good mood seems to say that all is well, so there is no need to check. It doesn't mean that a happy mood makes you foolish. It means that when you feel upbeat, it's worth taking a second look before you say yes, and that it's also an effective time for others to ask you.

# Gain or loss?
@src Rothman & Salovey, Psychological Bulletin, 1997; O'Keefe & Jensen, 2007 and 2009
@conf mixed

Health messages can be framed in two ways. A gain frame says what you will get if you act, such as "using sunscreen keeps your skin healthy." A loss frame says what you will lose if you don't, such as "skipping sunscreen puts your skin at risk." In 1997 Alexander Rothman and Peter Salovey proposed that gain frames work better for preventing problems, and loss frames work better for encouraging people to get screened for a problem.

The idea was influential, but later reviews have found less support. Large analyses by Daniel O'Keefe and Jakob Jensen, in 2007 and 2009, found that gain-framed messages were slightly better for prevention, and that there was no clear advantage for loss frames in screening. The differences are small. How a message is framed matters less than we might think, and whether the message is clear and relevant to the person may matter more.

# Predicting your own behavior
@src Greenwald, Carnot, Beach & Young, Journal of Applied Psychology, 1987; Sherman, JPSP, 1980

Suppose that someone calls the day before an election and asks if you plan to vote. In 1987 Anthony Greenwald and his colleagues tried this with registered voters. Some were asked to predict whether they would vote, and others weren't contacted. Later, the researchers checked public records.

Turnout among those who were asked was about twenty-five percentage points higher. In 1980 Steven Sherman found an even bigger effect. When people were asked if they would volunteer three hours for the American Cancer Society, nearly half of those who had been asked to predict actually volunteered, compared with a handful of those who were never asked. Almost everyone says yes to a nice request. Having said it, they feel the pull of their words. This is called self-prophecy, and it works best for socially desirable acts.

# The message that was overheard
@src Walster & Festinger, Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1962

Are we more persuaded by something we overhear? In 1962 Elaine Walster and Leon Festinger tested this. Students listened to a conversation between two graduate students about a topic. Some were told that the conversation was a private one, and that the talkers didn't know they could be overheard. Others were told the talkers knew that they were being listened to.

The listeners who believed that they were overhearing were more influenced by the message, as long as the topic mattered to them. The reason seems to be that when we think someone is trying to persuade us, we put up our guard. When they aren't, we don't. This is why a recommendation from a friend's casual remark can be more persuasive than an advertisement. It is also why a message that seems candid should still be weighed carefully.

# Seventeen cents
@src Santos, Leve & Pratkanis, Journal of Applied Social Psychology, 1994
@conf mixed

In 1994 Michael Santos, Craig Leve and Anthony Pratkanis tested an unusual way of asking for money. A person asked passersby for spare change in a normal way. In another condition the person asked for a specific, odd amount, such as seventeen cents or thirty-seven cents.

The people who were asked for the odd amount were more likely to give, and they often gave more. The researchers called it the pique technique. The unusual number stopped the usual script of brushing off a beggar, and made people think. Later studies have been mixed, and the effect may depend on the situation. But it's a good example of how a small surprise in a request can get attention, and change a reflex into a decision.
