# Three routes to persuasion
@src Petty & Cacioppo, 1986

In the 1980s Richard Petty and John Cacioppo proposed that there are two ways that a message can change a mind. The central route is slow and thoughtful: a person weighs the arguments and evidence and is moved by their quality. The peripheral route is quick and shallow: the person is swayed by cues such as how attractive or confident the speaker seems, how many people agree, or how pleasant the message sounds.

Their model, called the elaboration likelihood model, says which route people take depends on their motivation and ability to think. When the topic matters to someone and they have time, strong arguments win. When they're busy, tired or uninterested, the cues matter more. Attitudes formed by the central route last longer and resist attack better, which is why a patient explanation can be more durable than a slick pitch.

# The Coke that bought raffle tickets
@src Regan, 1971

In 1971 the psychologist Dennis Regan staged a small experiment. Two people, a participant and a confederate who seemed to be another participant, were supposed to be rating paintings. During a break, the confederate left the room and came back, in one version, with two bottles of Coke, one for himself and one for the participant, saying that he'd gotten one for each of them. In the other version, he returned empty-handed.

Later he asked the participant to buy some raffle tickets for a prize. Those who'd been given the Coke bought about twice as many tickets, and this held even for people who said that they didn't much like the man. The favor created a feeling of obligation. Reciprocity is among the strongest social rules, and it works even when the gift is small and unasked for.

# The extra mint
@src Strohmetz, Rind, Fisher & Lynn, 2002

Restaurant servers have a lot to teach us about reciprocity. In 2002 David Strohmetz and his colleagues arranged for a server to leave diners one mint with the check, two mints, or none. Compared with no candy, tips rose by about 3 percent with one mint, and by about 14 percent with two mints. The biggest gain came from a different move. When the server left one mint, started to walk away, and then came back and offered a second one, tips rose by about 21 percent.

The size of the gift mattered less than how personal and unexpected it felt. A small, thoughtful extra beat a larger routine one. In business, it's a reminder that generosity works best when it's visible and seems chosen for the person, not when it looks like part of the process.

# The jar with two cookies
@src Worchel, Lee & Adewole, 1975

In 1975 Stephen Worchel and his colleagues gave people a chocolate chip cookie from a jar and asked them to rate it. For some people, the jar had ten cookies, and for others, only two. The cookies were identical, but those from the nearly empty jar were rated as more desirable, and people said that they'd pay more for them.

Scarcity makes things seem valuable. When something is rare, we assume that other people want it, and we fear missing out. A further finding was that cookies that had just become scarce, because others had taken some, were prized even more than cookies that had always been scarce. It's the logic behind "only three left" and "limited edition." The cue is useful when it's true, and it's worth asking whether it is.

# Why "because" works
@src Langer, Blank & Chanowitz, 1978
@conf mixed

In 1978 Ellen Langer and her colleagues tested the word "because" at a library photocopier. A person waiting in line asked to go ahead. In the figures usually quoted, "Excuse me, I have five pages. May I use the Xerox machine?" got a yes from about 60 percent of people. "Excuse me, I have five pages. May I use the Xerox machine, because I'm in a rush?" got about 94 percent. Then came the odd result. "...because I have to make copies" gave 93 percent, though it gave no real reason.

The researchers concluded that people respond to the form of a reason, without analyzing it, at least when the request is small. For a larger request, with 20 pages, only a real reason helped. A later study by Marjorie Folkes in 1985 didn't get the same results, and researchers still debate how much "automatic" processing is involved. The practical lesson stands. When you ask for something, give a reason, and make it a real one.

# Even a penny will help
@src Cialdini & Schroeder, 1976

In 1976 Robert Cialdini and David Schroeder sent people to knock on doors and ask for donations to the American Cancer Society. Some of the canvassers added a simple sentence: "Even a penny will help." The people who heard it were much more likely to give, about 50 percent compared with about 29 percent for those who got the plain request.

The extra givers weren't just giving pennies. The total raised was higher in the "even a penny" group, too. The phrase legitimized a small gift, and made it harder to refuse by saying that you couldn't afford it. Later reviews of many similar experiments found the same effect. A request that makes it clear that a small contribution is welcome can raise the number of people who say yes.

# That's not all
@src Burger, JPSP, 1986

You've heard it on infomercials: "But wait, there's more!" In 1986 Jerry Burger tested the technique at a bake sale. Some customers were shown a cupcake and told the price was 75 cents. Others were first told it cost 75 cents, and then, before they answered, the seller added that it came with two cookies, at the same price. A lot more of the second group bought.

In the figures usually reported, about three quarters of the people who got the added bonus bought, compared with about 40 percent of those who were offered the same package at once. The sweetener, introduced after the price, seems to seem like a concession. Customers feel that they've got a deal, and they reciprocate by agreeing. It works best when the extra is small and comes right after the price, and it's easy to see through once you know it.

# The $49 dress that outsold the $44 dress
@src Anderson & Simester, 2003

Retailers love prices that end in 9, such as $39 and $4.99. In 2003 Eric Anderson and Duncan Simester tested the practice in a field experiment with a women's clothing catalog. They sent different versions of the catalog to customers, with the same dress priced at $44, $49 or $54. Demand for the dress at $44 and at $54 was almost the same, but at $49 it was significantly higher. Adding five dollars to the price sold more dresses than the lower price did, and more than the higher one.

Part of the answer is the left-digit effect. We read numbers from left to right, and $49 feels like it's in the forties, while $50 feels like a fifties price. The leading digit does the work, and the rest gets rounded down. The authors also found that the 9 ending helped most for new items, and less when the catalog added a sale sign. The result is also a warning to anyone who is buying. The price that you see isn't always the price that you feel.

# Why free is different
@src Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely, 2007

In 2007 Kristina Shampanier, Nina Mazar and Dan Ariely offered people a choice between a Hershey's Kiss and a Lindt truffle, which is a much better chocolate. When the Kiss cost one cent and the truffle cost 15 cents, most people took the truffle. Then both prices were lowered by one cent, so the Kiss was free and the truffle cost 14 cents. The preference flipped, and most people took the free Kiss.

The gap between the prices hadn't changed, and the truffle was still a bargain. But "free" is special. It removes the possibility of loss, and we feel an extra glow about getting something for nothing. Stores use this with free shipping and free gifts. It also explains why a small fee can seem to loom much larger than the money alone would suggest.

# The pain of paying
@src Prelec & Simester, 2001; Knutson et al., 2007

In 2001 Drazen Prelec and Duncan Simester ran an auction for tickets to a sold-out Boston Celtics game among business school students. Some were told that they'd have to pay in cash, and others that they could pay by credit card. The people who were bidding with credit cards were willing to pay far more, on average more than twice as much, for the same tickets.

Paying with cash is painful because you see the money leave. A card hides it. In 2007 Brian Knutson and his colleagues scanned the brains of shoppers and found that excessive prices activated a region linked to pain, the insula, before a person decided not to buy. It helps explain why gift cards, cards and phone payments make spending feel easy. If you want to spend less, a good trick is to pay in cash.

# Losses hurt more than gains please
@src Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992
@conf mixed

In their theory of choices, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky proposed that people dislike losing more than they like winning the same amount. In 1992 they estimated that a loss feels about twice as large as the equivalent gain. Offered a coin flip in which you can lose 100 dollars or win 150, most people turn it down, though the odds are in their favor.

The idea is called loss aversion, and it helps explain why free trials work. Once you have the product, giving it up counts as a loss. It's a leading idea in behavioral economics, though some researchers have argued that it's weaker or more limited than first thought, and that it depends on the situation. A safe conclusion is that people are more sensitive to downsides than to upsides, and a sale that's framed as avoiding a loss can move them more than one that's framed as a gain.

# The paradox of the winning review
@src Spiegel Research Center, Northwestern University, 2017

You might think that a product with a perfect five-star rating would be the most appealing. In 2017 the Spiegel Research Center at Northwestern University analyzed data from the online sales of many products, and found that purchases were most likely when the average rating was between about 4.0 and 4.7 stars. Past that, and especially at 5.0, they declined.

Shoppers seem to trust a rating that is very high but not perfect, because a perfect score looks fake or based on too few reviews. The study also found that the number of reviews mattered, with the most gain in the first few, and that reviews were especially persuasive for expensive and unfamiliar products. A flawless record may seem suspicious, and an honest mixture of voices is more believable.

# Telling people what others do
@src Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius, 2008

Hotels leave cards asking guests to reuse towels to save the environment. In 2008 Noah Goldstein, Robert Cialdini and Vladas Griskevicius tested different wordings in a real hotel. The standard card about the environment led to about 35 percent of guests reusing their towels. A card saying that most guests in the hotel reuse them did better. And one saying that most guests who'd stayed in that very room reused them did best, with about 49 percent.

People look to what others like them do. The closer the comparison group, the stronger the effect. The finding has been used to get people to pay taxes on time and to use less energy. When a message says "most people do this," and it's true, it's a gentle and honest way to move people in a good direction.

# Warmth first, then competence
@src Fiske, Cuddy & Glick, 2007

When we meet someone, we ask two silent questions: are they friendly, and are they capable? Susan Fiske, Amy Cuddy and Peter Glick found in the 2000s that these two dimensions, warmth and competence, explain most of how we judge individuals and groups across many cultures. Warmth comes first, because it tells us whether the person means us harm.

For anyone who wants to persuade, the order matters. A person who is seen as competent but cold can be admired and not trusted, and one who is warm but seen as incompetent may be liked and not followed. People need both, and they tend to check warmth first. If you want to make a good first impression, show goodwill before you show off your skills.

# The charm of a small mistake
@src Aronson, Willerman & Floyd, 1966
@conf mixed

In 1966 Elliot Aronson and his colleagues made recordings of a person answering quiz questions. In one version, the person gave brilliant answers. In another, the same person gave brilliant answers and then clumsily spilled coffee over himself. Listeners who heard the version with the spill liked him more than the one without. When an average person spilled the coffee, though, he was liked less.

It's called the pratfall effect. A small blunder by someone who seems very competent makes them seem human and more approachable. It doesn't work for everyone, and later studies show that it depends on who the person is and who is watching. It's an idea that gives some comfort. Being perfect is not required, and an admitted mistake may win you more goodwill than a flawless surface.

# The ambivert advantage
@src Grant, Psychological Science, 2013

We often assume that the best salespeople are extroverts. In 2013 Adam Grant measured the personality of 340 call center sales employees and tracked their earnings over three months. The extroverts did not come out on top. The ambiverts, who are in the middle and show both qualities, earned the most, about $155 an hour in sales, compared with about $125 for the extroverts and about $120 for the introverts.

Grant suggests that ambiverts know when to talk and when to listen. Extroverts may be so enthusiastic that they overwhelm customers, and introverts may not ask enough. The finding is a nice reminder that a trait that helps in one setting can hurt in another, and that balance often matters more than a high score on one scale.

# Asking the right questions
@src Rackham, SPIN Selling, 1988
@conf mixed

In the 1980s the British researcher Neil Rackham led a long study of sales calls, observing thousands of them, to find out what separated top sellers from the rest. He noticed that in small sales, such as a low-priced item, many techniques worked, but in large and complex ones, the best sellers asked a certain kind of question. He summarized it as SPIN: situation, problem, implication and need-payoff questions.

Situation questions gather facts. Problem questions bring out the difficulties. Implication questions ask what the problem costs, and need-payoff questions invite the customer to say how a solution would help. In big sales, the customer needs to see the value for themselves. Some critics doubt how well the study generalizes, but the lesson is a good one. Selling works better when it begins with questions and not with a pitch.

# The same story that moves us when it is one person
@src Small, Loewenstein & Slovic, 2007

In 2007 Deborah Small, George Loewenstein and Paul Slovic asked people to donate to a charity. One group read about a single girl, Rokia, a seven-year-old in Mali who faced hunger. Another group read statistics, such as the number of millions of people who faced hunger in several African countries. Donations were much higher for the single child.

The most striking result was that, when the statistics were added to Rokia's story, donations fell. The numbers made people think analytically, and dampened their feelings. The effect is called the identifiable victim effect, and it explains why one person's story can move us more than a large tragedy. For anyone who wants to inspire giving, the message is to tell the story of a person. For a donor, it's worth remembering that feeling and effectiveness are not the same.

# Fear works when it comes with a way out
@src Witte & Allen, Health Education & Behavior, 2000

Do scary messages change behavior? In 2000 Kim Witte and Mike Allen reviewed more than 90 studies of fear appeals, such as warnings about smoking or unsafe driving. They found that fear can work, and that the stronger the fear, the more people changed their attitudes, but there was a catch. The messages worked best when they also told people that they could do something about the danger, and how.

When people felt threatened but didn't feel able to respond, they often did something else instead. They denied the threat, avoided the message or got angry. The lesson applies to health campaigns and to everyday talk. If you want to alarm someone into acting, give them a clear and doable next step, or the alarm will just make them look away.

# The cash that made the pill work
@src Waber et al., JAMA, 2008

In 2008 Rebecca Waber, Baba Shiv, Ziv Carmon and Dan Ariely tested whether the price of a pill affects how well it works. Volunteers were given what they were told was a new painkiller, and then were given electric shocks. All the pills were placebos. Half were told that the pill cost $2.50 per dose, and half that it had been discounted to 10 cents.

Among those who got the regular-priced pill, 85 percent reported pain relief. Among those who got the cheap one, 61 percent did. The expectation that something is expensive, and therefore better, helps it to work. It's a powerful illustration of why price is also a message about quality. It also raises ethical questions, since a sense of value can improve results without any medicine.

# Why the expensive wine tastes better
@src Plassmann et al., PNAS, 2008

In 2008 Hilke Plassmann and her colleagues had people taste wines while lying in a brain scanner. They were told the price of each one, such as five dollars or ninety. In truth, the same wine was sometimes presented with different prices. People rated the wine as more pleasant when it was said to be expensive, and the brain's pleasure areas, in the front of the brain, were more active.

The price changed the experience and not only the opinion. It's an effect that applies far beyond wine, to food, art and music. The brain combines what the senses report with what it expects. When you're told that something is excellent, you're more likely to feel that it is. That doesn't make the pleasure false, but it does show how much it depends on the story that comes with it.

# Ending well
@src Redelmeier & Kahneman, Pain, 1996

In 1996 Donald Redelmeier and Daniel Kahneman studied patients undergoing colonoscopies, which are unpleasant. Patients reported their pain every minute and rated the whole procedure afterward. The overall rating was predicted not by the total pain, or the length, but by two moments: the worst one, and the last one. It's called the peak-end rule.

In a follow-up experiment, some patients had an extra minute added at the end of the procedure, during which the instrument was left in place but not moved, which was mildly uncomfortable. They rated the whole experience as less bad, and were more willing to have another one. The memory of an experience is made from its high point and its ending. If you serve customers, plan a good ending, because that is what they'll remember.

# After the sale, the doubt
@src Knox & Inkster, 1968

The psychologists Robert Knox and James Inkster made a curious observation in 1968 at a racetrack. They approached bettors and asked them how confident they were that their horse would win, either just before they placed their bet, or just after. Those who had just placed a bet were more sure of their horse than those who were about to.

Nothing about the horse had changed. What changed was the commitment. After making a decision, we tend to feel more sure that it was the right one, because doubts about a choice that we've made are uncomfortable. It's a form of cognitive dissonance. It explains why buyers so often defend their purchases, and why a salesperson's work isn't over when the contract is signed.

# You and your name
@src Pelham, Mirenberg & Jones, JPSP, 2002
@conf mixed

Do people like what resembles themselves? In 2002 Brett Pelham and his colleagues reported that people are more likely than chance to choose a city, a street or a career that matches their names. Men named Dennis or Denny, for instance, were found more often than expected as dentists, and women named Laura or Lauren as lawyers. They called it implicit egotism.

The claims are controversial. Later researchers, including Uri Simonsohn, argued that many of the patterns come from other causes, such as ethnic and family differences in names and careers. But there's a more solid finding behind it, the name-letter effect, which is that people tend to prefer the letters of their own names. For salespeople, it's a small reminder that people like things that feel familiar, and that your name is the most familiar word of all.
