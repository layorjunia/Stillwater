# The galley slaves of the movies
@src Studies of ancient warships and rowers; Ben Hur, 1959

In the film Ben Hur, a young man is chained to an oar in the bottom of a Roman ship, rowing until he drops. It has become the standard picture of an ancient galley. It is mostly wrong. In ancient times, galleys were not commonly rowed by chained slaves or prisoners. The rowers were usually paid workers or soldiers.

Slaves were used on warships only in times of crisis, such as when a fleet had lost many men, and in some cases they were set free afterward, for their service. Ptolemaic Egypt may be an exception to the rule. Other kinds of vessels were different, and Roman merchant ships were often manned by slaves, in some cases even with a slave as captain.

Free citizens rowing in the Athenian fleet, for example, were proud of their part in defending the city. The picture of men in chains came from later periods, such as the galleys of the Mediterranean in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, in which convicts did the rowing. The film mixed the two ages together.

# The volcano, the Minoans and Atlantis
@src Eruption of Thera; Akrotiri; Minoan Crete

About 3,600 years ago the volcano of Thera, now the island of Santorini in Greece, erupted in one of the greatest explosions in human history. The town of Akrotiri on the island was buried under ash, in the way that Pompeii was later, and it is preserved with paintings and houses. A popular story says that the eruption destroyed the Minoan civilization on the nearby island of Crete, and that it was the origin of Plato's tale of Atlantis, the island that sank into the sea.

Neither idea is supported by the evidence. The eruption did destroy settlements such as Akrotiri, and it may have helped to bring about changes in society in the region. But its direct effect on Crete was limited. The period after the eruption is often considered the high point of the Minoan culture, with great palaces and wide trade.

The Minoans did decline, but later, and for reasons that are still discussed. Atlantis was told by Plato about 360 BC, as a moral tale about an arrogant empire. A connection to Thera is an attractive guess, and has no proof. It shows how a dramatic event and a famous legend are tempting to link.

# Where the Roman salute came from
@src The Oath of the Horatii, Jacques-Louis David, 1784

The raised right arm, straight out with the palm down, is called the Roman salute. It is commonly shown in films about ancient Rome, with legionaries greeting their general. There is no evidence that the Romans used it. It does not appear in ancient writings or art as a form of greeting.

The gesture was first linked with ancient Rome in the 1780s. The French painter Jacques-Louis David showed it in his painting The Oath of the Horatii, shown in 1784, in which three brothers swear to defend Rome with outstretched arms. The painting was very influential. Later artists, and then the theater and early film, repeated the pose when they wanted to show classical Rome.

It was this tradition that the twentieth-century dictators drew on when they adopted the salute. The Nazi salute, in particular, was said to be Roman, though the claim was an invention. A gesture that people took for ancient was therefore designed for the stage, and it has taken on later meanings that are far from anything in Rome.

# Three Roman things that never happened
@src Roman sources; Latin etymology; accounts of the Third Punic War

The first is that rich Romans used a room called a vomitorium to be sick at a feast so that they could keep eating. They did not. A vomitorium was a passage in a theater or stadium, through which the crowd poured out at the end of a show. The word comes from a Latin verb that means to spew forth, in that sense.

The second is that when the Romans defeated Carthage in the Third Punic War, in 146 BC, the general Scipio Aemilianus sowed the ground with salt, so that nothing could grow. There is no ancient evidence for this. Carthage was destroyed and burned, and it was later refounded as a Roman city, a sign that the soil was not poisoned.

The third is that Roman soldiers were paid in salt, which is the origin of the word salary. The word does come from the Latin salarium, which has a link to salt, and it may have been an allowance for salt. But even that is not supported by ancient writers. The soldiers were paid in coin. The stories are good ones, and each of them was told long after the events.

# Caesar's birth and his last words
@src Plutarch; Suetonius; Shakespeare, Julius Caesar

The Caesarean section is said to be named for Julius Caesar, who is supposed to have been born that way. It is almost certainly not true. In his day the operation was nearly always fatal for the mother, and was done only if she was already dead or dying. Caesar's mother, Aurelia, lived on for many years. She was still alive when he was in his forties.

The other well-known story about Caesar is that when he was stabbed, he turned to his friend Brutus and said, Et tu, Brute, which means you too, Brutus. There is no proof that he said it. The ancient writers give other accounts. One is that he said nothing, and covered his face with his toga. Another, in Suetonius, is that he spoke in Greek, and said you too, child.

The Latin words that we know come from the play Julius Caesar, by William Shakespeare, written about 1599. Shakespeare used them as a dramatic climax, and they stuck. The word Caesarean probably comes from the Latin verb caedere, to cut, and from a law of Roman kings that required a baby to be cut from a dead mother. Both the name and the dying words are popular in a way that history is not.

# Were the Dark Ages dark?
@src Medieval demography; life expectancy studies

The Middle Ages are often called the Dark Ages, and pictured as a time of ignorance, barbarism and superstition. Modern historians reject the term. In the period there were new universities, improved farming tools, fine buildings in the Gothic style, and writers such as Dante and Chaucer. The label was first used by Renaissance writers, who wanted to praise their own age.

Another idea is that medieval people died in their thirties or forties. This comes from reading figures of life expectancy. Life expectancy at birth was low, because so many children died in infancy and childhood. It does not mean that adults died at that age. A man who reached 21 in medieval England could expect to live to about 64.

The mistake is to take an average that is pulled down by early deaths as the length of a typical adult life. People in the Middle Ages grew old, and the old had a place in society. Disease, famine and war caused many early deaths, and life was far more dangerous than today. But the picture of an age in which a man of 40 was ancient is a distortion.

# The king who told the tide to stop
@src Henry of Huntingdon, History of the English People, about 1130

King Canute, or Cnut, ruled England, Denmark and Norway in the early eleventh century. A famous story says that he sat on the shore and commanded the tide to stop, and when it came in and wet his feet, he was shown to be a fool. It is often used as an example of foolish arrogance, as in the saying about being like Canute.

The original tale says the opposite. It was first told in about 1130 by the chronicler Henry of Huntingdon. According to the story, Canute was tired of the flattery of his courtiers, who said that he was so powerful that even the sea would obey him. He had his chair set by the water, and gave the command, to show that no man is all-powerful and that all people must yield to forces beyond their control.

The point of the story is his humility, and it was told to show a pious king. Over time, the moral was lost, and the king became a figure of ridicule. It is an example of a story that has been turned upside down by its retelling, and that people now cite for the reverse of what it meant.

# Three medieval horrors that were invented later
@src Historians of medieval law and torture; museum research

A well-known idea is that a medieval lord had the right of the first night, or jus primae noctis, to sleep with a bride on her wedding night. There is no evidence that such a right ever existed in the Middle Ages. It seems to be a later invention, used to show how brutal the age was.

The iron maiden is a cabinet with spikes, said to be used to torture people. There is no evidence that it was used in the Middle Ages, or was even invented then. Instead, the ones in museums were pieced together in the eighteenth century from several objects that had been found in museums and arsenals, to make spectacular items for exhibition.

The chastity belt is a device that is said to have been used to lock up women while their husbands were away on crusade. Whether it was invented in medieval times is disputed by modern historians. Most of the surviving examples are now thought to be deliberate fakes from the nineteenth century. In each case the Middle Ages are made to bear the cruelty that came from the imaginations of later centuries.

# Armor, staircases and beaks
@src Arms and armor scholarship; castle studies; history of plague medicine

People often think that a knight in armor was so weighed down that he had to be lifted onto his horse with a crane. It is false. Plate armor was made to fit and to allow movement. Soldiers had to be able to fight on foot in case they lost their horse, and they could mount and dismount without help. The armor used in tournaments in the late Middle Ages was much heavier than the armor used in war.

Another idea is that the spiral staircases in castles turned clockwise as they went up, to give the defender a better swing of his sword arm against a right-handed attacker. Clockwise stairs are indeed more common in castles than anticlockwise ones. But they were even more common in buildings that had no military purpose, such as churches, so it was probably not for defense.

The plague doctor, with a long beak stuffed with herbs, is a symbol of the Black Death in the fourteenth century. The costume was not worn then. It came later, in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and even then it was not common. The three images are part of a picture of the Middle Ages that was put together in later times.

# Did the Middle Ages think the Earth was flat?
@src Medieval astronomy; Washington Irving, A History of the Life and Voyages of Christopher Columbus, 1828

Medieval European scholars did not believe that the Earth was flat. It had been known to be a sphere since at least the sixth century BC, when Greek thinkers argued for it, and Eratosthenes measured its size. In the Middle Ages the educated knew it, and they taught it in the universities. What most believed was that the Sun went around the Earth, an idea first challenged by astronomers such as Copernicus and Galileo.

The story that Columbus proved the Earth round is also a myth. His difficulty in getting support was not because people feared he would sail off the edge. It was that they thought, rightly, that the East Indies were much farther than he supposed. Columbus greatly underestimated the circumference of the Earth, because of two errors in his calculation. The idea that he proved it was spread by writers such as Washington Irving, in his 1828 biography.

A last notion is that map makers wrote here be dragons on the edges of their maps. They did not, as a rule. The phrase is found on only two known objects, the Hunt-Lenox Globe and the Ostrich Egg Globe, next to a coast in Southeast Asia. Maps of the period did sometimes have pictures of real or mythical animals. The sea monsters on old maps are more often a matter of decoration than of warning.

# Why the Dutch wore black
@src Dutch Golden Age portraiture; dye history

The paintings of the Dutch Golden Age of the seventeenth century, by Rembrandt, Frans Hals and others, show merchants and their wives dressed in black. It is often said that this showed Puritan restraint, a desire to be modest and plain. The usual idea of a sober, thrifty Dutch society fits a plain view of dress.

But the black clothes were mostly a sign of status. Black was hard to make with the dyes then available, and a deep black required costly dyeing, often done several times. It was a mark of wealth to wear it. The clothes were often elaborately made, with fine lace and embroidery, and the cost of those trimmings added to their prestige.

The wearer of black in a Dutch portrait was therefore not saying, I am humble. He was saying that he could afford the best. It is a good case of how an image can be read in the wrong way if we apply later values to it. The sober look was a display of means, in a culture that had become rich through trade.

# The princess who may never have said it
@src Rousseau, Confessions, written about 1765 to 1770; first attributed to her in 1843

When the poor of France had no bread, Marie Antoinette is supposed to have said, let them eat cake. It is told as a sign of how out of touch the royal family was. There is no evidence that she ever said it.

The phrase first appears in the Confessions of Jean-Jacques Rousseau, in a passage written when Marie Antoinette was about nine years old, and living in Austria. Rousseau did not name the person who said it. He wrote that it was said by a great princess, when she heard that country people had no bread. The French word he used was brioche, a rich bread, and not cake.

It was not until 1843, about fifty years after her death, that the saying was attributed to her. By then she had become a symbol of the extravagance of the old regime. A line that suited her reputation was linked to her, and it has stayed. The story tells us more about how history is written by the victors than about what she said.

# The housewife who never was
@series people
@src Economic and social history of women's work

A common picture of the past is that in the old days women stayed at home as housewives while men went to work. The image is of a traditional family, and it is often used to say how things used to be. In fact, it is a modern idea, and a social ideal more than a long-standing reality.

Throughout history women have worked. They worked in farming, in manufacturing, in family businesses, and as servants and traders. Often their working days were twelve to fifteen hours long. The picture of a woman who did not work outside the household was true only for some families, in some classes and periods, and mainly in the middle class of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

It also varied widely by culture and by the family. Poorer women had to earn money, and the wives of farmers and shopkeepers worked alongside their husbands. The idea of the housewife as the traditional woman was really a recent development, which was possible only when many families could live on one wage. It is a reminder that a tradition that feels very old may be fairly new.

# The Pilgrims did not dress in black and buckles
@series history
@src Plymouth Colony records; nineteenth-century illustrations; Salem witch trials

The picture of the Pilgrims who founded Plymouth Colony in 1620 is of people dressed all in black, with buckles on their hats and shoes. It is the image on many Thanksgiving cards. It is not accurate. The Pilgrims usually did not wear all black, and the hats they wore did not have buckles. Their clothes followed the fashions of the late Elizabethan age, and had many colors. Buckles were a symbol of quaintness added in the nineteenth century. Most Pilgrims also did not carry blunderbusses.

The Puritans of the Massachusetts Bay Colony, nearby, did often dress in black. The two groups are often confused.

There is a similar confusion about the Salem witch trials of 1692. People accused of witchcraft were not burned at the stake. Nineteen people were hanged, and at least five died in prison. One man, Giles Corey, was pressed to death with heavy stones as the authorities tried to make him confess. Burning was a punishment used for witchcraft in parts of Europe, but not in Salem.

# The honest boy and the cherry tree
@series history
@src Mason Locke Weems, The Life of Washington, 1806 edition

Most Americans have heard that young George Washington chopped down his father's cherry tree, and when asked, said, I cannot tell a lie. It is used to show his honesty. The story is not true. Neither the tree nor the words are in any record from his time.

The story was invented by one of his biographers, Mason Locke Weems, who was a traveling bookseller and a minister. Weems wrote a life of Washington that appeared soon after his death in 1799, and in a later edition added the tale of the cherry tree. He wanted to hold up Washington as a model of virtue for young readers, and the book sold very well.

The tale was reprinted in school readers for generations, and became part of the national story. Washington did have a reputation for honesty, and was widely admired for it, but the scene was an invention. It was a work of fiction presented as a family memory. It is one of the most famous examples of how a legend can be made up, to teach a lesson, and still be repeated as history.

# The vote that German lost by one
@series history
@src Petition of 1794; Frederick Muhlenberg

A story that is often told is that German nearly became the official language of the United States, and that it lost in Congress by a single vote. The story has several forms. In one, the vote was in the House of Representatives, and the deciding vote was cast by Frederick Muhlenberg, the Speaker, who was of German descent. It is called the Muhlenberg legend.

It did not happen. No bill to make German the official language was ever defeated by one vote, in the House or in any state. What actually took place was this. In 1794 a group of German immigrants in Virginia sent a petition asking that some federal laws be published in German as well as English. The House voted on whether to take up the matter, and it was set aside by a procedural vote of 42 to 41. Muhlenberg abstained.

Some Americans use the story to argue about languages in the country. In truth the United States has never had an official language set down by Congress. The legend is a good example of a small event that grew into a big story, because it fits a pattern that people like, a nation that turned on a single vote.

# The ninja who dressed in blue
@series history
@src Studies of ninja history; Edo-period theater

The popular image of a ninja is of an assassin, dressed in a black suit with only his eyes showing, who climbs walls and throws stars. There is little or no evidence that it is true. The ninja of Japan's age of civil war were mainly spies and infiltrators, who gathered information and slipped into enemy places.

When they dressed to fight, and not in disguise, their costumes were typically dark brown. In peacetime they wore dark indigo work clothes, much like those of farmers. A person who was trying to pass unnoticed would not wear a black suit that made him conspicuous.

The black outfit comes from the theater. In the Japanese stage of the Edo period, in the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries, stagehands wore black and were supposed to be invisible, and the convention was used for characters who were meant to be hidden. The costume then spread to the portrayal of ninja. Later films, comics and games reinforced it. The ninja of the stage and screen are a creation of entertainment, which grew from a real tradition of spies.
