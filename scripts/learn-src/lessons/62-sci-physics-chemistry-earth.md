# Entropy and the arrow of time
@src Clausius, 1865; Boltzmann, 1877

If you watch a film of an egg breaking, and then a film of it running backward, you know which is real. Most laws of physics don't distinguish between forward and backward in time. A collision between two billiard balls looks fine in reverse. What gives time a direction is the second law of thermodynamics, which says that entropy, a measure of disorder, tends to increase in a closed system.

In the 1860s Rudolf Clausius coined the word entropy, and in the 1870s Ludwig Boltzmann explained it statistically. There are far more ways for things to be mixed up than ordered, so the system drifts toward mixed-up states, simply by probability. A cup of hot coffee cools down, and never heats up by itself. The reason why entropy was low in the beginning, in the early universe, is still an open question.

# Why you can't build a perpetual motion machine
@src Carnot, Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire, 1824

For centuries, inventors tried to build machines that run forever without fuel. In 1824 a young French engineer, Sadi Carnot, studied steam engines. He wanted to know the limits of their efficiency. He showed that any engine that produces work from heat must also give off waste heat to a colder place. There's a maximum fraction of the heat that can be turned into work, which depends only on the temperatures of the hot and cold sides.

His result became the basis of the second law of thermodynamics. No engine can be perfectly efficient, and none can run forever. Carnot died of cholera at thirty-six, and much of his work was lost, since his papers were burned along with his belongings. Some patent offices, including the American one, refuse applications for perpetual motion machines unless a working model is provided.

# Half-lives and radiocarbon dating
@src Libby, Radiocarbon Dating, 1952

Cosmic rays striking the upper atmosphere create a radioactive form of carbon, carbon-14. It mixes into carbon dioxide, and is taken up by plants, and by the animals that eat them. A living thing has a steady proportion of carbon-14 to ordinary carbon. When it dies, it stops taking in carbon, and the carbon-14 decays, with a half-life of about 5,730 years. Half of it is gone after that time, and half of what remains after the next.

In 1949 the chemist Willard Libby realized that measuring what's left could date organic remains. It was tested on objects of known age, such as wood from an Egyptian tomb. He won the Nobel Prize in 1960. The method works for things up to about fifty thousand years old, and it has been calibrated using tree rings, which show that the amount of carbon-14 in the air has varied over time.

# The Higgs boson
@src ATLAS and CMS collaborations, 2012

In 1964 several physicists, among them Peter Higgs, proposed a mechanism to explain why some particles have mass. An invisible field fills all of space. Particles that interact with it strongly are heavier, and those that don't, such as the particle of light, have no mass. The field should have a particle of its own, a boson.

On July 4, 2012, two teams of thousands of scientists at CERN, near Geneva, announced that they had found a particle that fits. It took the Large Hadron Collider, a ring seventeen miles around, which smashes protons together at nearly the speed of light. Higgs and François Englert won the Nobel Prize in 2013. The nickname God particle, which came from a book title, is disliked by many physicists.

# The ghost particles
@src Cowan & Reines, Science, 1956; Kajita & McDonald, 2015 Nobel

Neutrinos are particles with almost no mass and no electric charge. They interact so weakly that most pass straight through the Earth. About sixty-five billion of them from the Sun pass through every square centimeter of your body each second, and you don't feel a thing. Wolfgang Pauli predicted them in 1930, to save the law of conservation of energy in radioactive decay, and he said that he had done a terrible thing, since he'd proposed a particle that could not be detected.

In 1956 Clyde Cowan and Frederick Reines detected them from a nuclear reactor. In 1998 an experiment in Japan showed that neutrinos change from one type to another as they travel, which means that they have mass, though a tiny amount. Takaaki Kajita and Arthur McDonald shared the Nobel Prize in 2015. Neutrinos from distant supernovas have been used to study stars.

# Antimatter
@src Dirac, 1928; Anderson, Physical Review, 1933

In 1928 the physicist Paul Dirac wrote an equation that described the electron with relativity. It had a strange feature. It predicted a particle just like the electron, with the opposite electric charge. Dirac was at first reluctant to say that it was real. In 1932 Carl Anderson, studying cosmic rays in a cloud chamber at Caltech, saw a track that curved the wrong way. It was the positron, the antielectron.

Every kind of particle has an antiparticle. When matter and antimatter meet, they annihilate in a burst of energy. This is used in medicine. In a PET scan, a patient is given a tracer that emits positrons, which annihilate with electrons in the body, and detectors pick up the flashes. A mystery remains. The Big Bang should have made equal amounts of matter and antimatter, but the universe is almost all matter.

# The laser
@src Maiman, Nature, 1960; Einstein, 1917

A laser makes a beam of light in which all of the waves are in step, with the same color and direction. The idea came from Albert Einstein, who in 1917 described stimulated emission. An atom that has been excited can be triggered by passing light to emit a second particle of light, identical to the first. If many atoms are excited, a chain reaction of light builds up.

In 1960 Theodore Maiman, a physicist at Hughes Research Laboratories in California, built the first working laser, using a ruby crystal and a flash lamp. Some people called it a solution looking for a problem. It's now in barcode scanners, in surgery, in the fiber-optic cables that carry the internet, and in the gravitational wave detectors that measure changes smaller than an atom.

# Superconductors
@src Kamerlingh Onnes, 1911; Bednorz & Müller, 1986

In 1911 the Dutch physicist Heike Kamerlingh Onnes cooled mercury to a temperature of about four degrees above absolute zero, and found that its electrical resistance suddenly dropped to zero. A current in a loop of superconducting wire can flow without loss, and, in experiments, for years. He had also been the first to liquefy helium, which made the cold temperatures possible.

Superconductors also push out magnetic fields, so that a magnet floats above one. This is the Meissner effect. Superconducting magnets are used in MRI scanners and in particle accelerators. For decades, the materials had to be kept very cold. In 1986 Georg Bednorz and Alex Müller found a ceramic that worked at a higher temperature, and the record at normal pressure is now about minus one hundred and forty degrees Celsius. A material that superconducts at room temperature and normal pressure would transform the electric grid, and has not been found.

# Making a star on Earth
@src Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, 2022

Fusion is the reaction that powers the Sun. When light atoms such as hydrogen are forced together at very high temperature and pressure, they merge into helium and release a great deal of energy. If it could be done on Earth, it would give a clean power source with fuel from seawater. But the conditions are extreme, and for seventy years, the experiments used more energy than they gave out.

On December 5, 2022, the National Ignition Facility in California fired 192 lasers at a pellet of hydrogen fuel the size of a peppercorn. It produced about three megajoules of fusion energy, from about two megajoules of laser light that reached the target. It was the first time that a fusion experiment got more energy out than was put in, counting the laser energy at the target. The lasers themselves used far more power from the grid, so a power plant is still a long way off.

# How far is a star?
@src Bessel, Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society, 1838

For thousands of years, astronomers did not know how far away the stars are. If the Earth goes around the Sun, nearby stars should seem to shift very slightly against the background of more distant stars as we move, an effect known as parallax. Astronomers looked for it for centuries, and found none. The stars were too far away, and the shifts too small to measure.

In 1838 the German astronomer Friedrich Bessel measured the parallax of a faint star, 61 Cygni. He found that it was about ten light-years away. That's about sixty trillion miles. It was the first distance to a star ever measured, and it confirmed that the Earth moves around the Sun. It also showed how large the universe is. Even the nearest stars are so far away that their light takes years to reach us.

# The first picture of a black hole
@src Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration, 2019 and 2022

A black hole gives off no light, so how do you photograph it? In April 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration released an image of a glowing ring around a dark center. It was the shadow of a supermassive black hole at the center of a galaxy called M87, some fifty-five million light-years away. The black hole has a mass of about six and a half billion Suns.

To make the picture, radio telescopes in several places on Earth were linked, so that together they acted as a single telescope as large as the planet. The data from the observations filled so many hard drives that they were flown to a central lab. In 2022 the team released an image of Sagittarius A*, the black hole at the center of our own galaxy. The images match the predictions of Einstein's general relativity.

# Why muons reach the ground
@src Rossi & Hall, Physical Review, 1941

When cosmic rays hit the upper atmosphere, they create particles called muons. A muon is unstable, and lives for about two millionths of a second before it decays. Even at nearly the speed of light, that is only long enough to travel about six hundred meters. Yet the muons are created about fifteen kilometers up, and plenty of them reach the ground.

The explanation is time dilation, one of the predictions of Einstein's theory of special relativity. To an observer on Earth, a muon moving at high speed experiences time more slowly, so it lasts longer than it would at rest. In 1941 Bruno Rossi and David Hall measured the decay of muons on a mountain top and at sea level, and the results agreed with relativity. It's one of the clearest everyday tests of the theory.

# The failed experiment that changed physics
@src Michelson & Morley, American Journal of Science, 1887

In the nineteenth century, physicists believed that light is a wave, and that waves need a medium. They imagined an invisible substance called the ether, which filled the universe. The Earth, moving through it, should feel an ether wind, and light should travel at different speeds in different directions.

In 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley built a very sensitive device in Cleveland, in which two beams of light were sent at right angles, and compared. They floated it on a pool of mercury so that it could be rotated smoothly. They found no difference. The result puzzled physicists for almost two decades. It was one of the clues that led Einstein to special relativity in 1905, in which there's no ether, and the speed of light is the same for all observers.

# Graphene and a roll of tape
@src Novoselov et al., Science, 2004

Graphite is the material in a pencil. It's made of sheets of carbon atoms, in a honeycomb pattern, stacked loosely together. A single sheet is called graphene, and for a long time, it was thought that such a thin material could not exist on its own. In 2004 Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov, at the University of Manchester, isolated it. They used a piece of sticky tape.

They pressed the tape onto a lump of graphite, and peeled it off, again and again, until they had flakes just one atom thick. Graphene is the thinnest material known. It's about two hundred times stronger than steel by weight, and conducts electricity and heat very well. Geim and Novoselov won the Nobel Prize in 2010. Many applications are being developed, though making it cheaply in large quantities has been a challenge.

# Why the ocean is blue
@src Smith & Baker, Applied Optics, 1981

You might think that the ocean is blue because it reflects the sky. The sky does play a small part, but the main reason is that water itself absorbs light. Pure water takes up red, orange and yellow light much more strongly than blue light. The longer wavelengths are absorbed within a few meters. The blue light travels farthest, and is scattered back to our eyes.

It's why deep water looks darker blue, and why divers lose the colors, red first, as they descend. In coastal seas, algae and sediment can change the color to green or brown. Chlorophyll in the algae absorbs blue and red. A swimming pool looks light blue for the same reason, though the effect is less strong in a thin layer. The color of the sea is the color of the light that water does not absorb.

# Why stars twinkle and planets don't
@src Standard astronomy; Kitchin, Astrophysical Techniques, 2013

On a clear night, the stars seem to twinkle, shimmering and changing color slightly. Planets shine with a steadier light. The reason has nothing to do with the stars themselves. It comes from the Earth's air. The atmosphere is full of pockets of air at different temperatures and densities, which bend light a little differently, and move around in the wind.

A star is so far away that it looks like a single point of light, and so the bending of that point makes it dance. A planet is much closer, and appears as a tiny disk, made up of many points. The twinkling of the different points averages out. Astronomers call it scintillation, or seeing. It's the reason that telescopes are placed on high mountains, and why the Hubble Space Telescope, above the atmosphere, can take sharper pictures.

# The Carrington Event
@src Carrington, Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society, 1859

On the morning of September 1, 1859, an English amateur astronomer named Richard Carrington was drawing sunspots, when he saw a patch of intense white light flare up in the spots. It lasted a few minutes. About eighteen hours later, the biggest geomagnetic storm on record hit the Earth. Auroras were seen as far south as the Caribbean, and in Hawaii.

Telegraph systems failed across Europe and North America. Operators reported sparks, and shocks, and paper catching fire. Some were able to send messages for a time without a battery, using only the current induced by the storm. A solar storm of this size today could damage satellites and power grids over a wide area. A smaller one in 1989 knocked out the power in Quebec for nine hours.

# Snowball Earth
@src Kirschvink, 1992; Hoffman et al., Science, 1998
@conf mixed

About seven hundred million years ago, according to one hypothesis, the Earth was frozen from the poles to the equator. Ice sheets covered the land, and perhaps the seas too, a state called Snowball Earth. It would have lasted millions of years, until volcanoes released enough carbon dioxide to warm the planet and melt the ice.

The idea was put forward by Joseph Kirschvink in 1992, and developed by Paul Hoffman and others. The evidence is rocks that were laid down by glaciers near the equator, as shown by their magnetism, and layers of rock that formed just after the ice. Some scientists think that the oceans were never fully frozen, and that a slushy belt stayed open near the equator. The question is still debated. Soon after the thaw, complex animals first appear in the fossil record.

# The Cambrian explosion
@src Walcott, Smithsonian Miscellaneous Collections, 1909; Gould, Wonderful Life, 1989

For most of the history of life, organisms were small and simple. Then, about 540 million years ago, in a geological moment of perhaps ten to twenty million years, the fossil record shows a burst of new kinds of animals. Most of the major groups that exist today appear, with hard shells, eyes, legs and jaws. This is the Cambrian explosion.

The best fossils come from the Burgess Shale in the Canadian Rockies, found in 1909 by Charles Walcott. It preserved soft-bodied animals, some of them very strange, such as Opabinia, which has five eyes. What caused the explosion is debated. Possible factors include a rise in oxygen, the evolution of predators that set off an arms race, and the development of genes for body plans. Some life existed before, but the Cambrian was when animals took off.
