# What you can and cannot shout in a theater
@src Schenck v. United States, 1919; Brandenburg v. Ohio, 1969

It is often said that shouting fire in a crowded theater is illegal in the United States, and that it is the standard example of speech that the First Amendment does not protect. The line is an old one, but it was never the law. It comes from a decision of the Supreme Court in 1919, Schenck v. United States, written by Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.

The case had nothing to do with theaters or fires. Charles Schenck had been convicted for sending out leaflets opposing the draft during the First World War, and the Court upheld the conviction. Holmes used the example of a false cry of fire in a crowded theater as an illustration of how speech may cause harm. It was an aside, and not the ruling.

The decision was later overturned. In 1969, in Brandenburg v. Ohio, the Court held that the government may only punish speech that is directed to inciting imminent lawless action, and is likely to produce it. The test is much harder to meet than the one in Schenck. The phrase is still repeated, and it has taken on a life of its own that is far from what the Court said.

# The red phone and the briefcase with no red button
@series tech
@src Moscow-Washington hotline, 1963 to the present; nuclear football

In films, the president has a red telephone that connects directly to the leader of the Soviet Union. The real hotline was set up after the Cuban Missile Crisis, in 1963, to prevent a war from beginning by mistake. It was never a telephone. At first it used teletype machines, which print messages, and in 1988 these were replaced by fax machines. Since 2008 it has been a secure computer link on which the two countries exchange email.

It also did not connect to the White House. The line ran between the Kremlin and the Pentagon, which is in Arlington County, Virginia. The red phone is a piece of movie symbolism, which was never accurate.

Another object of legend is the nuclear football, the briefcase that follows the president, so that he can order a nuclear attack. It does not hold a big red button. Its main use is to confirm the identity of the president, and to help him to communicate with the military, through the Pentagon. The decision to attack is made by a process that involves codes and orders, and does not depend on a button.

# Two things that almost never happen
@series history
@src Documented cases of concrete used in killings; Federal Trade Commission Funeral Rule, 1984

Gangsters in films kill their victims by fitting them with cement shoes, and throwing them into a river. It is a staple of crime stories. It seems almost never to have happened. There are only two documented cases of the method, one in 1964 and one in 2016, and in the first, the victim had concrete blocks tied to his legs, which is not the same as being set in cement. The French army did use the method on Algerians killed in the so-called death flights during the Algerian war.

Another common belief is that embalming is required by law in the United States. It is not. The funeral industry long encouraged people to think that it was. In 1984 the Federal Trade Commission passed a rule, the Funeral Rule, which forbids funeral homes to say that embalming is required, when it is not, in most cases.

Both examples show how a belief can spread because someone gains from it. The crime stories gain by being vivid, and the funeral industry gained from selling a service. Neither was based on common practice. When something is often said, it is worth asking who benefits from it.

# The frights of Halloween night
@series history
@src Studies of Halloween sadism by Joel Best; missing-person investigation guidance

Every year parents are warned that strangers may hide poison or razor blades in the candy that children collect at Halloween. No case has been proven in which a stranger killed or badly hurt a child in that way. In one well-known case, an adult family member spread the story to cover up the fact that he had himself killed a child.

There are also stories that people torture black cats around Halloween, which is why some shelters will not let them be adopted at that time. There are no documented cases of pet black cats being tortured or sacrificed. Where black cats have been found dead at Halloween, the cause has been natural predators, such as coyotes or birds of prey.

A related belief, from crime shows, is that you must wait 24 hours before reporting that someone is missing. There is no such rule. When there are signs of violence or an unusual absence, it is important to start an investigation at once. Experts say that the first 72 hours are the most critical. In all three cases, a common fear has been shaped by a story, and the effect is that people worry about the wrong things.

# What the international police cannot do
@series history
@src Interpol; Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations; legal procedure

Interpol is the International Criminal Police Organization, and it is often shown in films as a force of agents who chase criminals across borders. In fact its staff cannot conduct investigations, make arrests, or use false passports. Its role is to help the police of different countries to communicate and share information. It is the police of each country that investigates and arrests. Interpol does issue travel documents to its own officials, in some cases.

A second idea is that an embassy is the territory of the country that it represents. In general it is not. In only a minority of countries does the host nation give the embassy a sovereign status. The Vienna Convention gives embassies special privileges, such as immunity from most local laws, but the building remains under the authority of the country where it stands.

Courtroom dramas bring a third. The surprise witness who clears a defendant at the last moment is called a Perry Mason moment, after a television lawyer. They are rare. Nearly all evidence is exchanged in pretrial discovery, and if new information appears at a trial, the case is usually paused so that both sides can examine it. Real law is built on preparation, and not on the courtroom surprise.

# Three American legal ideas that are widely misunderstood
@series history
@src R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul, 1992; Miranda v. Arizona; First Amendment doctrine

A defendant in the United States cannot have the case dismissed just because the police did not read him his Miranda rights. If a person is not given the warning before being questioned, what he says in the interrogation may not be allowed as evidence at the trial. But the prosecution can still use other evidence, and can use statements made when the warning had been given.

Undercover police officers are not required to say that they are police, and may lie when they are working in disguise. The limit is entrapment. A defendant may claim it if officers improperly pushed him, with great pressure, to commit a crime that he would not have committed otherwise.

The First Amendment generally prevents only the government from restricting speech, religion, the press, assembly and petition. It does not stop private companies or individuals from setting their own limits, though other laws may limit what they can do. Even hate speech is generally protected from government action, as the Supreme Court held in R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul in 1992. But some categories, such as true threats and incitement to imminent lawless action, can be restricted.

# Chewing gum, caning and divorce
@series history
@src Singapore gum regulations, 1992; U.S. divorce statistics

In Singapore, it is said, you can be caned for chewing gum. It is not true. Singapore did ban the import and sale of chewing gum in 1992. And caning is still a punishment for certain crimes there. But the two facts are separate. Gum offenses have only ever carried fines or jail, and owning or chewing gum is not illegal in itself.

The belief that half of all marriages end in divorce is also wrong, at least in the United States. The rate rose to a peak of about 41 percent in the 1970s, and it has fallen since. Couples who married in more recent decades have stayed together longer, and the rate for those who married in the 2000s was the lowest since the 1970s. Experts predicted that if trends continue, about two thirds of marriages will last.

The half figure probably came from comparing the marriages and the divorces in a single year, which can be misleading. Because people marry at different times, the number of divorces in a year says little about the share of marriages that end. Both stories are a good example of how a striking claim, once repeated, resists corrections.

# The defense that blamed a snack
@series history
@src Trial of Dan White, 1979; murders of George Moscone and Harvey Milk, 1978

The Twinkie defense is a phrase that is used to ridicule a legal argument, in which a defendant says that junk food drove him to crime. It is thought to come from the trial of Dan White, who in November 1978 shot and killed the mayor of San Francisco, George Moscone, and a city supervisor, Harvey Milk. White was convicted of voluntary manslaughter, and got a short sentence. The outcome caused an outcry.

But the defense did not claim that Twinkies caused the murders. The argument was one of diminished capacity. White's lawyers said that he was suffering from severe depression, and that his mental state meant that he could not have planned the killings with the intent that murder requires. A psychiatrist said that White's change in habits, including eating junk food and sweets, was a sign of how depressed he was.

Reporters took up the idea of a sugar defense, and the phrase stuck. It has often been used to criticize the outcome. The case led California to abolish the diminished capacity defense. A phrase that was meant as a joke has obscured what the argument was about.

# Quotes that were never said
@series words
@src Quote misattribution; Mary Shelley, Frankenstein, 1818; the baby shoes story

Many of the quotations that we pass around were not said by the people to whom they are attributed. Quotations from obscure or unknown writers tend to be given to famous ones. Among the most commonly misquoted are Mark Twain, Oscar Wilde, Albert Einstein, Winston Churchill, Abraham Lincoln, William Shakespeare, Bill Gates, Steve Jobs, Confucius, Sun Tzu and the Buddha.

One example is the six-word story, For sale, baby shoes, never worn. It is often credited to Ernest Hemingway, who is said to have written it on a bet. There is no evidence for that. The story was not attributed to him until decades after he died, and similar newspaper notices were printed earlier.

A different kind of mix-up is about a monster. In Mary Shelley's 1818 novel, Frankenstein is not the creature. It is the name of the scientist who creates him, Victor Frankenstein. The creature is never given a name in the book. Later films and plays began to call the creature Frankenstein, and the usage is so well established that many people no longer treat it as a mistake. Language, like memory, moves with the way it is used.

# The deaf composer and the poisoned one
@series history
@src Beethoven's deafness; Pushkin, Mozart and Salieri, 1832; Shaffer, Amadeus, 1979

Beethoven began to lose his hearing in his late twenties, and in his last years he could hear almost nothing. It is often said that he was completely deaf when he composed his greatest works. He was not entirely deaf even at the end of his life. He could hear certain frequencies, though only in a very reduced way.

Another legend is that Mozart was poisoned by his rival, the composer Antonio Salieri. He was not. Mozart died in 1791, at the age of 35, of an illness. The rumor of poison arose soon after Salieri died in 1825. It was made into a play by Alexander Pushkin in 1832, Mozart and Salieri, and much later into the play Amadeus by Peter Shaffer in 1979, and the film of 1984.

Neither story is meant to be history. Salieri was a respected composer who taught many pupils, including Beethoven, and there is no evidence that he hated Mozart. The tales make Mozart a victim and Salieri a villain, and they have been so well told that they seem real. The facts about Beethoven are, if anything, more moving than the myth. He heard something of the world, and went on writing.

# Three classical music mix-ups
@series history
@src Guido of Arezzo; Notebook for Anna Magdalena Bach; Chopin's Minute Waltz

The tritone is an interval that sounds tense, and it is said to have been banned by the medieval Church as the devil in music. There is no evidence of such a ban. Early medieval music used it in Gregorian chant, in some of the modes. The first theorist to discourage it was Guido of Arezzo, who lived about 991 to 1033, as one that is hard to sing.

The Minuet in G major, which is played by almost every piano student, is often credited to Johann Sebastian Bach. It is not by him. In the 1970s it was found to be a movement from a harpsichord suite by Christian Petzold. It was in the Notebook for Anna Magdalena Bach, a book of pieces by various composers, mostly Bach, and so was assumed to be his. It is listed in the catalog of Bach's works in an annex for doubtful pieces, as BWV Anh. 114.

Chopin's Minute Waltz does not take one minute to play. It takes about two. The title uses the word minute in the sense of small, as in the adjective that is pronounced my-NOOT. It is a little waltz, not a sixty-second one.

# Three pop music legends
@series history
@src The Sound of Music, 1959; Shea Stadium, 1965; Michael Nesmith interview, 1977

Edelweiss is often taken to be the national anthem of Austria, or a traditional Austrian folk song. It is not. It was written for the 1959 musical The Sound of Music, as an original song, and it was written in the style of a folk song. The real national anthem of Austria is Land der Berge, Land am Strome, which means land of mountains, land by the river.

The Beatles' concert at Shea Stadium in 1965 is often said to be the first time that a rock group played in a big outdoor sports stadium. It was not. Elvis Presley had played such venues in the 1950s, and the Beatles themselves had done so in 1964. Shea Stadium was notable for its crowd of some 55,000, and for being a start to the era of stadium concerts.

Another claim is that in 1967 the Monkees outsold the Beatles and the Rolling Stones put together. They did not. The idea began as a joke. Michael Nesmith, a member of the group, said it in an interview in 1977, as a prank, and it has been repeated. Concept albums were also not an invention of rock in the 1960s. Frank Sinatra had made them in the 1940s and 1950s.

# The night at Altamont and the ham sandwich
@series history
@src Altamont Free Concert, December 6, 1969; Cass Elliot, 1974

At the Altamont Free Concert in California, on December 6, 1969, a young man named Meredith Hunter was stabbed to death by a member of the Hells Angels, who were acting as security. It is often said that it happened as the Rolling Stones were playing Sympathy for the Devil. The killing did take place at the concert, but it happened later, while the band was playing Under My Thumb.

The detail is a small one, but the myth makes the event seem to be a judgment on the song, as if the devil's music had called up violence. That is a better story than a confusing night in which the crowd was large, the security was poor, and a series of fights had broken out during the day.

A different myth concerns the singer Cass Elliot, of the Mamas and the Papas, who died in London in 1974. For years it was said that she choked on a ham sandwich. This was not true. The story was started by her manager, who wanted to avoid any suggestion that her death was linked to drugs. In both cases a plain fact was replaced by a neater tale.

# Songs and clubs that are not what people say

Phil Collins's 1981 song In the Air Tonight is said to be about a man who watched someone drown, and later confronted a person in the audience of one of his concerts who had let it happen. The story is not true. Collins has said that the song was about his feelings during the divorce from his first wife.

Another song that carries a rumor is Puff, the Magic Dragon. Many people believe that it is about marijuana. Both of its writers, Peter Yarrow and Leonard Lipton, denied it throughout their lives, and said that the song is about the loss of childhood innocence. And there is the 27 Club, the idea that popular musicians are more likely to die at that age. It arose after the deaths of Jimi Hendrix, Janis Joplin and Jim Morrison in a short time, and later of Kurt Cobain and Amy Winehouse. Statistical studies show no scientific basis for it. Musicians die at all ages, and the myth is built from memorable cases, while many deaths at other ages go unremarked.
