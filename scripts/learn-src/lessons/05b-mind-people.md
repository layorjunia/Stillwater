# Allostatic load
@src McEwen, 1998

Bruce McEwen introduced the term in the 1990s for the wear and tear that builds up when the body's stress systems are working too much, or never switch off. A stress response is meant to be brief, a surge that rises and then settles. When it stays on, the surge becomes a drain.

Over time that can show up as higher blood pressure, weakened immunity and trouble sleeping. It isn't just about how big a stressor is. How long it lasts and how well you recover also matter. That means rest and recovery are part of managing stress, not a reward for finishing it. If you're going through a long hard stretch, treating sleep, meals and quiet time as part of the job is not self-indulgence. It's maintenance.

# Burnout
@src Maslach & Jackson, 1981; WHO ICD-11

Christina Maslach, a psychologist, developed the best-known measure of burnout in the early 1980s. It has three parts. The first is exhaustion. The second is cynicism, or a feeling of detachment from the work. The third is a feeling of being ineffective, that nothing you do makes a difference.

It usually grows out of chronic stress at work, including overload, little control and a lack of recognition. The World Health Organization calls it an occupational phenomenon and not a medical condition. That wording points to something useful. Burnout is often addressed by changing the conditions, such as workload and support, and not only by asking the person to be more resilient. A tired person who is told to try harder is usually being given the wrong advice.

# The impostor phenomenon
@src Clance & Imes, 1978

In 1978 Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes described high-achieving women who felt like frauds. They believed they had fooled everyone, and they expected to be found out at any moment. Later work showed that it happens to men too, and that it's very common.

It isn't a diagnosis, but it is a familiar feeling. People who feel it often attribute their success to luck and discount their abilities, while treating their failures as proof of the truth. One of the best remedies is talking about it with others. You'll often discover that people you admire feel exactly the same, which is a relief. It also helps to keep a record of what you've actually done, since the feeling tends to ignore it.

# Cyclic sighing
@src Balban et al., 2023

The technique is two breaths in through the nose, the second a small top-up, followed by a long slow breath out through the mouth. You repeat it for a few minutes. It sounds almost too simple to matter.

In a 2023 Stanford trial with 108 people, those who did five minutes of it a day for a month improved their mood and lowered their resting breathing rate more than people who did mindfulness meditation. The long exhale seems to be what calms the body. It's easy to try. A few rounds when you notice tension can take the edge off, and it costs nothing, needs no equipment and can be done at a desk or in a waiting room.

# Therapy works
@src Smith & Glass, 1977; Wampold, 2015

In 1977 Mary Lee Smith and Gene Glass combined the results of 375 studies of psychotherapy in one of the first meta-analyses. They found that the average person who had therapy ended up better off than about 75 percent of people who hadn't. At that time, some critics were still claiming that talk therapy did nothing.

Later reviews have reached similar conclusions. It doesn't mean that every therapy works for every person, and some people don't improve. It does mean that, on average, talking with a trained person helps, and it helps by a meaningful amount. If you've been wondering whether it's worth the time and cost, the evidence says that it usually is. The harder part, in practice, is finding someone who suits you.

# The therapeutic alliance
@src Flückiger et al., 2018

Researchers have found that the quality of the bond between client and therapist, including agreement on the goals and the tasks of therapy, predicts how well the treatment works. A 2018 review of nearly 300 studies found a consistent link, whatever type of therapy was being used.

So if you're thinking about seeing someone, it matters that you feel understood and comfortable. A therapist can be well trained and still not be right for you. It's fine to try a different one if the first isn't a good fit, and doing so is not a failure on your part. It's the same way you'd choose a doctor or a teacher. The method matters, and so does the person delivering it.

# The Dodo bird verdict
@src Luborsky et al., 1975; Wampold, 2015
@conf mixed

In Alice in Wonderland, after a race, the Dodo declares, "Everybody has won, and all must have prizes." In 1936 Saul Rosenzweig borrowed the line to describe a puzzling finding that different forms of psychotherapy seem to work about equally well.

Later researchers, including Lester Luborsky, found similar results. Not everyone agrees with the verdict. For some conditions, such as phobias and panic, particular treatments do seem to do better. But the overall similarity suggests that shared ingredients, such as the relationship, a believable explanation and a plan, matter a lot. The debate hasn't been settled. A fair summary is that the method matters less than we might expect, and that the fit between the person and the approach matters more.

# Defense mechanisms
@src A. Freud, 1936; Vaillant, 1977
@conf mixed

In 1936 Anna Freud, Sigmund's daughter, described the mind's habits for protecting us from painful feelings, such as denial, projection, displacement and rationalization. These are things we do without deciding to, and often without knowing.

The psychiatrist George Vaillant later ranked them from immature, like denial, to mature, like humor and sublimation. The ideas are useful for describing how people cope, and many therapists use them. But the research behind them is mostly observation and not controlled experiment, so they're better thought of as helpful descriptions than as proven mechanisms. It's still worth asking, when you've reacted strongly, whether you were protecting yourself from something you didn't want to look at.

# Displacement
@src A. Freud, 1936
@conf mixed

When we can't safely express a feeling at its source, we sometimes aim it at someone safer. A man is humiliated by his boss, says nothing, and then snaps at his family when he gets home. The anger was real, but it landed on the wrong people.

It's one of the classic defense mechanisms, and almost everyone has seen it at work. Spotting it can help. When your reaction seems bigger than the situation, ask yourself who or what the feeling is really about. Often the answer is somewhere else. If you can recognize it in the moment, a short apology and an explanation can repair the damage, and you can find a better way to deal with the real source.

# Freud's legacy
@src Standard psychology reference
@conf contested

Sigmund Freud shaped the vocabulary of psychology, and we still talk about the unconscious, repression and the importance of early childhood. Many of his ideas entered everyday speech so thoroughly that we forget where they came from.

Some of those ideas hold up in modified forms. Others, such as the Oedipus complex or the idea that dreams are disguised wishes, have not been supported by evidence. A big problem is that many of his claims can't be tested, since any result can be explained within the theory. He's more important as a historical figure than as a scientific authority. It's fair to say that he asked some good questions, and that most of his answers have been replaced.

# The Myers-Briggs test
@src Pittenger, 1993; McCrae & Costa, 1989
@conf contested

The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator sorts people into 16 types, such as INFJ or ESTP, from four either-or choices. It's very popular in workplaces, and many people enjoy finding out their type. It gives you a vocabulary for talking about differences.

But scientists generally don't regard it as reliable. In some studies, about half of people got a different type when they retook the test five weeks later. The reason is that traits like introversion fall along a continuum, with most people in the middle, and the test cuts the line in half. A person just under the line and a person just over it are labeled as opposites. The Big Five model has a much better record, and it describes people with scores and not boxes.

# The Big Five
@src Costa & McCrae, 1992

After decades of research, many personality psychologists settled on five broad dimensions: openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism. Researchers have found the same five in many countries and languages, which suggests that they capture something real.

They stay fairly stable through adult life, and they predict real outcomes, such as job performance, relationship satisfaction and health. Unlike the Myers-Briggs types, each is measured on a scale, so most people are somewhere in the middle. Nobody is simply an extravert or an introvert. They're somewhere along the line, and that can vary a little with the situation. None of the five is good or bad in itself, though each has its own strengths and costs.

# Traits are partly heritable
@src Polderman et al., 2015

In 2015 Tinca Polderman and her colleagues combined nearly fifty years of twin studies, covering over 14 million pairs of twins. Across all the traits measured, about 49 percent of the differences between people could be traced to genes.

The other half came from the environment, mostly the kind that siblings in the same family don't share. That is a surprise to many, since we expect that growing up in the same house should make children alike. It means that neither genes nor upbringing alone decide who we become. It also reminds us that "heritable" doesn't mean "fixed." A trait can be partly genetic and still respond to what we do and what happens around us.

# The Flynn effect
@src Flynn, 1987

James Flynn discovered that the average score on IQ tests rose about three points every decade through most of the twentieth century, in many countries. That's a big change in a few generations, and it's too fast to be genetic.

Possible causes include more schooling, better nutrition and growing familiarity with abstract thinking and with tests. In some countries, scores have stopped rising or have even gone down in recent years, and experts aren't sure why. The Flynn effect is a good reminder that test scores are not a fixed measure of something inborn. They respond to the world a person grows up in, and a score from one generation can't be directly compared with one from another.

# Multiple intelligences
@src Gardner, 1983; Visser et al., 2006
@conf contested

In 1983 the psychologist Howard Gardner proposed that people have several separate kinds of intelligence, such as musical, bodily, interpersonal and logical. The idea is very popular among teachers, and it feels generous, since it says that everyone is smart in some way.

But researchers haven't found good evidence that the types are independent. People who score high on one kind of mental test tend to score higher on others, which supports the idea of a general factor. The ideas are better seen as a way of appreciating different strengths than as a scientific theory of the mind. It's true that people have different talents. It's less clear that sorting students by "learning style" helps them learn.

# The marshmallow test
@src Mischel et al., 1972; Watts, Duncan & Quan, 2018
@conf mixed

In the 1960s and 70s Walter Mischel offered young children a choice: one marshmallow now, or two if they could wait. Follow-up studies suggested that the children who waited did better in life, with better test scores and fewer troubles. It became a famous story about willpower.

In 2018 Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan and Haonan Quan repeated the study with a larger and more varied group. After they took family background into account, the link was about half as strong, and mostly disappeared. Waiting seems to reflect circumstances as much as willpower. A child who has learned that adults keep their promises has good reason to wait, and one who has learned otherwise has good reason to eat the marshmallow.

# Grit
@src Duckworth et al., 2007; Credé et al., 2017
@conf mixed

In 2007 Angela Duckworth and her colleagues defined grit as passion and perseverance for long-term goals. They found that it predicted who stayed at West Point and who won the national spelling bee, and the idea quickly became popular.

Later reviews found that grit overlaps a lot with conscientiousness, a long-known trait, and predicts success only modestly. Perseverance does matter, but the idea that grit is a separate and powerful predictor has been oversold. It's still a useful thing to value in yourself. Just don't expect it to explain everything, and remember that sticking with something isn't always wise. Sometimes the grittiest thing to do is to stop and try something else.

# Maslow's hierarchy
@src Maslow, 1943; Tay & Diener, 2011
@conf mixed

In 1943 Abraham Maslow proposed that human needs form a pyramid, from physical needs and safety at the bottom to belonging, esteem and self-fulfillment at the top. You were supposed to satisfy each level before moving up.

The idea that needs matter is useful. The strict order isn't supported. A survey across more than 120 countries found that people pursue love and purpose even when basic needs aren't fully met, and that the needs seem to matter in parallel. Think of the starving artist, or the family who share what little they have. The pyramid is better thought of as a rough guide to the kinds of things that people need than as a ladder.

# The self-esteem myth
@src Baumeister et al., 2003
@conf mixed

In the 1980s and 90s it was widely believed that raising children's self-esteem would improve their grades and behavior. Schools introduced programs and praise on that basis. In 2003 Roy Baumeister and his colleagues reviewed the research and found little to support it.

They found that self-esteem tends to follow success more than cause it, and that boosting it directly did little for performance. Praise for effort and strategy works better than praise for being smart, because it teaches that the outcome depends on what you do. That doesn't mean being harsh. It means that real accomplishments and honest feedback are a better foundation than hollow compliments. Self-respect that's earned lasts longer than self-esteem that's handed out.

# The Milgram experiments
@src Milgram, 1963; Haslam et al., 2014
@conf mixed

In 1961 Stanley Milgram, at Yale, had volunteers play the part of a teacher who gave electric shocks to a "learner" for wrong answers, with the shocks increasing to a dangerous 450 volts. The shocks were fake, and the learner was an actor. The volunteers did not know this.

In the baseline version, 65 percent of participants went all the way to the maximum. Later analyses question how realistic the setup was, and how many participants really believed it. Still, it showed how far ordinary people may go under instruction from an authority, and many of the participants were visibly distressed as they did it. It's a sober reminder that "I was told to" is a weaker defense than it feels like.

# The Asch conformity experiments
@src Asch, 1951

In the early 1950s Solomon Asch asked people to say which of three lines matched a standard line, an easy task. But everyone else in the room was an actor and had been told to give the wrong answer. The real participant was the last to answer.

About 75 percent of the participants went along with the group at least once, and about a third of all their answers conformed. If one other person gave the right answer, conformity dropped sharply. A single ally makes it easier to speak up. That's a useful thing to know in a meeting or a family. If you disagree and are afraid to say so, someone else probably does too, and your voice may give them theirs.

# The Stanford prison experiment
@src Zimbardo, 1971; Le Texier, 2019
@conf contested

In 1971 Philip Zimbardo had college students play guards and prisoners in a mock prison in a basement at Stanford. It was said to show that a situation turns ordinary people cruel, and it appeared in textbooks for decades.

But later evidence showed that the guards were coached on how to behave, and some participants were acting. In 2019 the researcher Thibault Le Texier published an analysis of the archives that raised serious doubts. Most psychologists now regard it as a vivid demonstration and not as proof of what happens naturally. It shows how powerful a setup can be, but it's a shaky basis for conclusions about human nature.

# The Robbers Cave experiment
@src Sherif et al., 1954
@conf mixed

In 1954 Muzafer Sherif ran a summer camp for twenty-two boys in Oklahoma. He split them into two groups, the Rattlers and the Eagles, and set up competitions with prizes. Hostility rose quickly, with name-calling, raids and fights.

Later he created problems that could only be solved by working together, such as fixing the water supply, and the animosity eased. The study suggests that rivalry creates conflict and shared goals reduce it. Some critics have noted that Sherif tried other approaches before he got the result he wanted, so the study is less clean than its reputation. Still, it fits what many people have seen, that a common task can bring opponents together.

# The foot-in-the-door effect
@src Freedman & Fraser, 1966

In 1966 Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser asked homeowners to put a large, ugly "Drive Carefully" sign on their lawns. Only about 17 percent agreed. That isn't surprising, since the sign was big and unattractive.

But when another group had earlier been asked to display a small sign, and had agreed, 76 percent said yes to the large one. A small yes changes how you see yourself, as someone who helps with this kind of thing, and it makes the next request easier. It's a technique that salespeople and fundraisers know well. If you notice that you're being led through a series of small agreements, it's fair to ask where the series is heading.

# The door-in-the-face effect
@src Cialdini et al., 1975

In 1975 Robert Cialdini and his colleagues asked college students to spend two years as volunteer counselors for juvenile offenders. Almost everyone said no, as you'd expect. The request was enormous.

When the same students were then asked to spend two hours taking the kids to the zoo, about half agreed, compared with 17 percent of students who were asked only about the zoo trip. After turning down a big request, people feel a pull to meet you partway. It's the opposite of the foot-in-the-door effect, and it relies on a sense of reciprocity: you gave a little, so I should give a little. It's worth knowing when you're negotiating.

# Cialdini's principles
@src Cialdini, Influence (1984)

In his 1984 book Influence, Robert Cialdini described six levers that make people say yes. Reciprocity means that we repay favors. Commitment means that we stay consistent with what we've said. Social proof means that we follow what others do. The others are authority, liking and scarcity.

He worked out the list by training with salespeople, fundraisers and advertisers, and observing what they did. Knowing the levers doesn't make you immune. But it helps you notice when someone is pulling one, and ask yourself whether you would agree without it. A "limited time only" sign is scarcity. A free sample is reciprocity. Once you can name the lever, it's easier to decide for yourself.

# The Hawthorne effect
@src Levitt & List, 2011
@conf contested

The name comes from experiments in the 1920s and 30s at the Hawthorne Works, a Western Electric factory near Chicago. Workers' output seemed to rise when the lighting was changed, whichever way, which was taken to mean that people work harder when they know they're being watched.

In 2011 Steven Levitt and John List went back to the original data and found little evidence of such an effect in those studies. Being observed may well change behavior, and it probably does in some situations. But this famous example isn't solid, and the story has grown in the retelling. It's a case where a catchy name outlived the evidence, and it's worth remembering whenever a neat story is repeated without a check.

# Rosenhan's pseudopatients
@src Rosenhan, 1973; Cahalan, 2019
@conf contested

In 1973 David Rosenhan published "On Being Sane in Insane Places." He said that eight healthy people faked one symptom, hearing voices, to be admitted to psychiatric hospitals, and that once inside they acted normally but weren't discovered for weeks.

A 2019 investigation by the journalist Susannah Cahalan found evidence that he misreported some of the data and details, and that some of the accounts did not match his own notes. Doubts about how reliably psychiatric diagnosis was made then were reasonable, and the field did change. But the study itself isn't trustworthy. It's a useful case of a dramatic result that people wanted to believe, and that few ever checked.

# Harlow's monkeys
@src Harlow, 1958

In the 1950s Harry Harlow raised baby rhesus monkeys with two artificial mothers. One was made of wire and held a bottle, and the other was covered in soft cloth but gave no milk. You might expect the babies to prefer the one that fed them.

The babies clung to the cloth mother and went to the wire one only to feed. It showed that attachment grows from comfort and contact, not just from food. It changed how child-care experts thought about babies, and it helped end the advice to avoid cuddling children. The experiments were cruel and would not be done today, but their lesson, that closeness is a need and not a luxury, has lasted.

# The Strange Situation
@src Ainsworth et al., 1978

In the 1970s Mary Ainsworth devised a short laboratory test. A one-year-old is in a playroom with a parent. A stranger comes in, the parent leaves, and then the parent comes back. What the baby does at the reunion shows a pattern.

About 65 percent were securely attached, upset when the parent left and quickly comforted on return. The rest showed avoidant or anxious patterns, either ignoring the parent or clinging and not settling. The test has been used for decades to study attachment, and its patterns have been linked to later development. It doesn't label children for life, since the relationship can change. But it shows that a baby's trust in a caregiver is something real and measurable.

# Adult attachment styles
@src Hazan & Shaver, 1987
@conf mixed

In 1987 Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver proposed that adult romantic relationships follow the same patterns as the bond between a baby and a caregiver. People are described as secure, anxious or avoidant in how they handle closeness.

An anxious person worries about being left, and an avoidant person feels uneasy when things get close. Attachment styles are partly stable but not fixed, and they can change with new relationships or therapy. Researchers now measure them on spectrums and not as fixed types. The idea is useful as a way of understanding your own patterns. But it's best used gently, since it's a tendency and not a destiny, and it says more about how you respond than about who you are.

# Romanian orphans
@src Rutter et al., 2007

After the fall of the Ceaușescu regime in 1989, thousands of Romanian children were found in overcrowded orphanages with very little care. The English and Romanian Adoptees study followed those who were adopted into British families.

Children adopted before six months of age mostly caught up. Those adopted later had more lasting difficulties with attention, relationships and thinking. It showed that severe early neglect has real effects, and that earlier rescue helps. It also showed that many children recovered a great deal in loving homes, even after a terrible start. Both parts matter. The first years are important, and children can heal, which is a message of real hope for anyone who works with children who have had a hard beginning.

# Classical conditioning
@src Pavlov, 1927

Ivan Pavlov, a Russian physiologist studying digestion, noticed that his dogs started to salivate when they heard the footsteps of the assistant who fed them. The dogs were responding to something that merely came before the food.

He then paired a bell with food and found that the bell alone would produce salivation. A neutral signal that's linked with something meaningful comes to trigger the same response. The same thing happens in people. A song linked to a memory can bring back the feeling, and the smell of a hospital can make your stomach tighten. We rarely choose these links. They form on their own, and it's helpful to know that they have an ordinary explanation.

# Operant conditioning
@src Thorndike, 1898; Skinner, 1938

In 1898 Edward Thorndike put cats in puzzle boxes and found that actions that led to escape were repeated. He called it the law of effect. B. F. Skinner developed the idea in the 1930s with his "Skinner box," a cage in which animals could press a lever for food.

Behavior that's followed by something rewarding becomes more likely, and behavior followed by something unpleasant becomes less likely. Rewards usually change behavior faster and more lastingly than punishment does, since punishment tells you what not to do and not what to do instead. It's the idea behind training a dog and also behind a lot of advice for parents. Catching someone doing something right works better than only noticing mistakes.

# Variable reinforcement
@src Ferster & Skinner, 1957

B. F. Skinner found that how often you reward matters. When rewards come on an unpredictable schedule, like a slot machine, animals keep pressing the lever the longest, even after the rewards stop altogether.

We can see the same thing in many places: checking your phone to see whether anything new has arrived, or pulling to refresh a feed. The unpredictability makes the habit stick, which is why it's so hard to break. Many apps are designed with this in mind. A useful trick is to remove the trigger, by turning off notifications or putting the phone in another room, because it's easier to avoid the urge than to resist it once it has started.

# Extinction
@src Bouton, 2004

When a learned response stops being rewarded, it fades. A dog stops salivating to a bell that no longer predicts food. But it's not erased. After a rest, the response often returns, which is called spontaneous recovery.

In people, a fear or habit that has faded can come back with stress, or in a new place. That can feel like starting over, but it isn't. The new learning is still there, and it usually returns more quickly. Researchers like Mark Bouton find that practicing the new learning in many different settings makes it last longer. If an old fear or craving comes back, it doesn't mean the progress was fake. It means the old pattern is not gone, only quiet.

# Little Albert
@src Watson & Rayner, 1920
@conf mixed

In 1920 John Watson and Rosalie Rayner at Johns Hopkins showed a nine-month-old baby a white rat while making a loud noise behind him. After several pairings, the baby cried at the sight of the rat, and at other furry things.

It's a famous example of conditioned fear, and it appears in nearly every textbook. It's also an ethically troubling study with weak methods, and later accounts of what it proved are often exaggerated. The details have been disputed, and even who the child really was has been argued over since. It wouldn't be allowed now. It's worth knowing as a piece of history, and as a reminder that a very famous finding isn't always a strong one.

# The Bobo doll
@src Bandura, Ross & Ross, 1961

In 1961 Albert Bandura and his colleagues showed nursery-school children an adult either hitting a large inflatable doll or ignoring it. Later, the children were left alone in a room with the doll.

Those who had seen the adult attack it copied the same actions and even the same words. The study showed that children can learn by watching, without being rewarded themselves. It started a long debate on media violence, which hasn't been settled. What it clearly showed is how much young children learn from what the adults around them do, and how little of that has to be taught on purpose. Children are always watching, and what we do in front of them often speaks louder than what we say.

# Cognitive maps
@src Tolman, 1948

In 1948 Edward Tolman showed that rats don't simply learn a chain of left and right turns. After they had learned a maze, he blocked the usual route. Instead of getting confused, they chose a new route in the direction of the goal, as if they had a map in their heads.

The finding was important because it showed that animals, and so people, build mental representations of space and not only habits. It came at a time when many psychologists thought that behavior was only a chain of responses. Decades later, researchers found cells in the brain that fire at particular places, which gave Tolman's idea a physical basis. You carry a map of your neighborhood, and it isn't a list of turns.

# Latent learning
@src Tolman & Honzik, 1930

In 1930 Edward Tolman and Charles Honzik put hungry rats in a maze. One group was fed at the end every time and got better quickly. A second group was never fed. A third wasn't fed until the eleventh day.

On the day food appeared, the third group suddenly performed as well as the rewarded rats. They had been learning the layout all along but showed it only when it became worth showing. We learn a lot without reward, and we may not know how much until something calls on it. It suggests that curiosity alone teaches. A child exploring a new house or an adult wandering a new city is learning, even though nothing seems to come of it.

# Contact comfort and touch
@src Coan, Schaefer & Davidson, 2006

In 2006 James Coan and his colleagues put married women in a brain scanner and told them they might get a mild electric shock. Those who held their husband's hand showed less activity in brain areas linked with threat than those who held a stranger's hand or no one's.

Physical contact with someone you trust seems to calm the body's alarm system, and the effect was bigger in happier marriages. It's a small, striking piece of evidence that comfort isn't only a feeling. It has measurable effects on the body. That's worth remembering when someone near you is frightened or grieving. A hand on a shoulder or a long hug can do something that words often can't.
