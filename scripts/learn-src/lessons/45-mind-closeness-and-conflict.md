# How couples explain each other's behavior
@src Bradbury & Fincham, Psychological Bulletin, 1990

Two people forget to call. In one marriage, the other thinks, they were busy and it slipped their mind. In another, the other thinks, they just don't care about me. In 1990 Thomas Bradbury and Frank Fincham reviewed research on how couples explain each other's behavior. They found a clear difference between happy and unhappy couples.

Distressed couples tend to explain a partner's bad behavior as a trait, and as lasting and widespread. It shows what they're like and is how they will always be. They give less credit for good behavior, explaining it as an accident or a one-time thing. Happy couples do the opposite. The pattern can feed itself, since the explanation shapes how we react. Noticing the story you tell about a partner's behavior, and asking whether another could be true, is a useful habit.

# Seeing your partner a little better than they are
@src Murray, Holmes & Griffin, JPSP, 1996

It might seem best to see your partner clearly, with all their flaws. In 1996 Sandra Murray, John Holmes and Dale Griffin asked dating and married couples to describe themselves and their partners, and to say how they thought their partners saw them. Then they compared the ratings.

People who saw their partner in a more positive light than the partner saw themselves, which the researchers called idealization, were more satisfied with the relationship. They were also less likely to see their satisfaction fall over time. The partners who were idealized often came to see themselves more like the picture. It suggests that a little generosity in the way you see someone may help to build the relationship you want. It's not about denying problems. It is about giving the person the benefit of the doubt.

# Doing something new together
@src Aron, Norman, Aron, McKenna & Heyman, JPSP, 2000

What helps long relationships to stay alive? In 2000 Arthur Aron and his colleagues tested the idea that novelty and excitement can bring back some of the feeling of the early days. Couples were brought into a lab, and some were given a silly, physically challenging task. They had to crawl on mats, with their wrists and ankles tied to their partner's with Velcro, while pushing a foam cylinder with their heads. The other couples did a mild task.

The couples who had done the exciting task reported a larger increase in satisfaction with the relationship afterward than those who had done the mild one. Later studies found that couples who regularly did new and arousing things together became happier over time. It seems that shared novelty gives people a feeling of growing together. It's a reason to try a new place, or new things, and not just stick with the same routine.

# The Michelangelo effect
@src Drigotas, Rusbult, Wieselquist & Whitton, JPSP, 1999

Michelangelo said that he saw the statue inside the stone, and chipped away until it was free. In 1999 Stephen Drigotas and his colleagues used his name for a pattern in close relationships. A partner who sees you as you would like to be, and who treats you that way, helps you to become that person.

They followed couples over several months. People who felt that their partner saw their ideal self and supported it were more likely to move toward that ideal, and were happier and more committed in the relationship. The idea is that we are sculpted by those who are close to us, and that we shape them too. It's a reason to notice the best in people, and to say it. What we expect of someone can pull them toward it.

# The five love languages
@src Impett, Park & Muise, Current Directions in Psychological Science, 2024
@conf contested

The idea of five love languages is popular. It says that people give and receive love in five ways: words of affirmation, quality time, gifts, acts of service and physical touch. It claims that each person has one main way, and that a couple is happier if they speak the same one. In 2024 Emily Impett and her colleagues reviewed the research on these ideas.

They found little support for the claims. The evidence did not clearly support that there are five languages, that each person has one, or that matching leads to happier relationships. Instead, people seem to value all of them. The authors suggested thinking of relationship needs more like a balanced diet. The idea has helped many couples talk about what they need, which is valuable. But there's no scientific reason to think that a couple must match.

# Holding hands calms the brain
@src Coan, Schaefer & Davidson, Psychological Science, 2006

In 2006 James Coan and his colleagues invited sixteen married women to take part in a brain scan. They told each woman that a mild electric shock might come after a cue, and then tracked how her brain responded to the threat. Sometimes the woman was alone. Sometimes a stranger held her hand. Sometimes her husband held it.

When the husband held her hand, the brain's response to the threat was much quieter than when she was alone. A stranger's hand helped a little. The calming effect was greatest for the women who rated their marriages as the happiest. The result suggests that a trusted person can act as a kind of regulator of our stress, so that we don't have to handle everything alone. Touch from someone who feels safe is a strong signal.

# Warm touch and blood pressure
@src Grewen, Anderson, Girdler & Light, Behavioral Medicine, 2003

In 2003 Karen Grewen and her colleagues studied couples who lived together. Some of the couples were asked to spend a short time in warm contact. They held hands while they watched a romantic video, and then shared a brief hug. Other people rested alone. Afterward, everyone had to give a short speech, a task that reliably raises blood pressure and heart rate.

The people who had warm contact with a partner beforehand showed smaller rises in blood pressure and heart rate during the speech than those who had rested alone. The effect was similar for men and women. A later study of fifty-nine women found that more frequent hugs from a partner went along with higher oxytocin and lower blood pressure. These are modest effects. But they suggest that everyday affection may help the body to cope with stress.

# Do couples who move in first divorce more?
@src Kuperberg, Journal of Marriage and Family, 2014
@conf mixed

For a long time, studies showed that couples who lived together before marriage were more likely to divorce. It was often taken as a warning. In 2014 Arielle Kuperberg looked at a large national survey in the United States, and measured the age at which couples first began to live together, and not just the age at which they married.

When she took that age into account, most of the extra risk disappeared. The couples who moved in together early, especially in their early twenties, were at higher risk, and many of them also married early. So it may be the age at which people commit, more than living together itself. Other researchers dispute this, and argue about how to measure it. The question is not settled, but the old warning looks too simple.
