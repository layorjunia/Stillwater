# How much exercise?
@src WHO, 2020; Moore et al., PLoS Medicine, 2012

The World Health Organization's 2020 guidelines say that adults should aim for 150 to 300 minutes a week of moderate activity, such as brisk walking, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening work on two days. But the guidelines also stress that any activity is better than none. If you can't hit the target, doing even a little less still brings most of the benefit, so don't treat it as all or nothing.

The payoff is real. In 2012 researchers pooled data from six large studies covering about 650,000 people. They found that people who got about 150 minutes a week of brisk walking, the recommended minimum, had a life expectancy 3.4 years longer than those who got none. That's an association, not proof of cause, since healthier people may exercise more. But the pattern is strong and consistent across many studies.

# The 10,000 steps story
@src Paluch et al., Lancet Public Health, 2022

The round number didn't come from research. It seems to have started with a Japanese pedometer sold in the mid-1960s, called Manpo-kei, which means "10,000 steps meter." It's often said that the number was chosen partly because the character for 10,000 looks like a person walking.

When researchers looked at actual health data in 2022, they found that the risk of early death fell until about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day for adults over 60, and 8,000 to 10,000 for younger adults. More is fine, but the gains level off. So if 10,000 feels out of reach, there's no need to feel that you've failed. A smaller number is still very valuable, and the biggest gains come from moving from very little to a moderate amount.

# Muscle: aging and soreness
@src Volpi et al., 2004; Cheung, Hume & Maxwell, 2003

After about age 30, we gradually lose muscle mass, at a rate of roughly 3 to 8 percent per decade, unless we do something about it. The loss is called sarcopenia, and it speeds up in later life, which affects balance, strength and independence. Strength training, such as lifting weights or using resistance bands twice a week, can slow or even reverse it, at any age, and it's linked with a lower risk of early death.

The ache that peaks a day or two after an unfamiliar workout is called delayed-onset muscle soreness. It used to be blamed on lactic acid building up, but lactate clears from muscles within an hour or so of exercise. The soreness is thought to come from tiny tears in muscle fibers and the inflammation that follows. It fades as your body adapts, which is why the same workout hurts less the next time.

# Sitting and moving
@src Dunstan et al., 2012
@conf mixed

Long stretches of unbroken sitting have been linked to health risks. In one 2012 experiment, people who got up for just a couple of minutes of light walking every 20 minutes had lower blood sugar and insulin levels after meals than those who sat the whole time.

How much of sitting's harm is separate from simply not exercising is still debated, and the experiments so far have been small. Either way, getting up often does no harm. A few easy habits can help: stand while on the phone, take a short walk after a meal, or set a timer to remind you to move. You don't need a gym for any of it. The aim is to break up the long, still stretches of the day.

# Better sleep: what the evidence supports
@src Qaseem et al., ACP, 2016; Roenneberg et al., 2006

For chronic insomnia, the best-supported first treatment isn't a sleeping pill. It's a form of talking therapy called CBT for insomnia, which the American College of Physicians recommended as first-line treatment in 2016. It includes keeping a fixed wake-up time, limiting the time you spend awake in bed, and changing anxious thoughts about sleep. It helps most people, and its benefits last longer than those of sleeping pills.

If you could change just one thing about your sleep habits, a good choice might be to wake at the same time every day, including weekends. It anchors your body clock, which makes it easier to feel sleepy at the right time in the evening. Most sleep experts also recommend a cool bedroom, about 60 to 67 degrees Fahrenheit, since your core body temperature naturally falls as you go to sleep. Comfort varies from person to person, so it's a guide and not a rule.

# Alcohol and sleep
@src Ebrahim et al., 2013

A drink or two may make it easier to fall asleep, which is why many people use it. But as your body breaks down the alcohol during the night, it disrupts sleep. It reduces REM sleep, the stage when most dreaming occurs, and it fragments the second half of the night.

You may not remember waking, but the sleep is lighter and less restful, and you often feel tired the next day. The effect is larger with more alcohol, and it's stronger in people who aren't used to drinking. If you do have a drink in the evening, it helps to have it a few hours before bed, to give your body time to process it. A "nightcap" is a poor sleep aid, however relaxing it may feel at first.

# Blue light and sleep
@src Chang et al., 2015
@conf mixed

It's a common worry that the glow from phones and tablets is ruining our sleep. Bright light late in the evening does delay the body clock, and blue-rich light does so a bit more. A 2014 study found that reading on a light-emitting e-reader before bed delayed the release of melatonin.

But the effect of typical screens in real life seems small, and what you're doing on the screen, whether it's exciting or stressful, may matter as much. Doomscrolling is probably worse for sleep than the color of the light. A sensible approach is to dim your screens in the evening, put them away for the last half hour before bed, and keep the bedroom dark. Those habits help, whatever the exact role of blue light turns out to be.

# Melatonin
@src Herxheimer & Petrie, 2002; Cohen et al., 2023

Melatonin is a hormone your brain releases at night to signal that it's dark, and the pills are often taken as sleep aids. But it isn't really a sedative. It works best for shifting the body clock, as with jet lag or for people who go to sleep very late.

Small doses, around 0.5 to 3 milligrams, work as well as large ones, and tests have found that the amounts in supplements vary widely from what's on the label. A 2023 study of gummies found that some contained several times the amount listed. It's sensible to talk with a doctor before giving it to children, since little is known about long-term use. It's a signal for timing, not a knockout drug.

# The eight glasses myth
@src Valtin, 2002

You've probably heard that you should drink eight glasses of water a day. There's no scientific basis for it. It may trace to a 1945 recommendation of about 2.5 liters a day, which also noted that most of that is already in prepared foods.

Needs vary with your size, the weather and how active you are. For most healthy people, thirst is a good guide, and tea, coffee and fruit all count. A simple check is the color of your urine, which should be a pale yellow. Older people feel thirst less, and so may need to drink more deliberately. But for most of us, there's no need to carry a gallon jug around.

# Sugar and hyperactivity
@src Wolraich et al., 1995

Many parents are sure that sugar makes children hyper. But when scientists tested it in controlled trials, giving some children sugar and others a sugar-free placebo without anyone knowing which, there was no effect on behavior. A 1995 review of 23 such studies found nothing, even in children who were said to be sensitive.

In another experiment, parents who were told that their child had had sugar rated the child as more hyperactive, though they hadn't. Expectation shaped what they saw. This doesn't mean that sugar is good for children. It has other well-known downsides, such as tooth decay. But the idea that it makes them wild at a birthday party is more likely to be the effect of the party.

# Protein and fiber
@src US Dietary Reference Intakes; USDA Dietary Guidelines

The official recommended minimum for adults is 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, which works out to about 56 grams for a person of 70 kilograms, or 154 pounds. Some experts argue for more, especially for older adults, who absorb protein less efficiently, and for people who lift weights. It's easy to meet from foods like beans, eggs, fish, dairy and meat.

Fiber is the part of plants that your body can't digest, and it's good for your gut and your heart. Experts recommend about 25 grams a day for women and 38 for men, but the average American eats only about 16. Beans, oats, vegetables, fruit and whole grains are good sources. Diets high in fiber are linked with a lower risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and some cancers. Most people get plenty of protein, but not enough fiber.

# Ultra-processed food
@src Hall et al., Cell Metabolism, 2019

In a 2019 study at the US National Institutes of Health, 20 volunteers lived in a research center for a month. For two weeks they ate meals made mostly of ultra-processed foods, and for two weeks, mostly unprocessed foods, with the menus matched for calories, sugar, fat and fiber on offer.

On the ultra-processed diet, they ate about 500 more calories a day, and gained about a kilogram in two weeks. On the unprocessed diet, they lost about a kilogram. They also ate faster on the ultra-processed diet. It was a small study, but a carefully controlled one. It suggests that the way food is made may affect how much we eat, and so it's a reason to eat more food that's close to its natural form.

# The Mediterranean diet
@src Estruch et al., NEJM, 2013, 2018

A pattern of eating rich in vegetables, olive oil, fish, nuts and legumes, with little red meat, has among the best evidence for protecting the heart. The 2013 PREDIMED trial in Spain found that it cut major heart events by about 30 percent.

The paper was retracted in 2018 because of problems with how some participants had been randomized, then reanalyzed and republished with similar results. That kind of correction is how science is supposed to work, and the conclusion held up. The diet isn't exotic or expensive. It's mainly about eating more plants, using olive oil as the main fat, and eating fish more than red meat. The pleasures of sharing meals are part of the tradition.

# Breakfast and fasting: what the trials show
@src Sievert et al., BMJ, 2019; Liu et al., NEJM, 2022
@conf mixed

"Breakfast is the most important meal of the day" was popularized, in part, by cereal marketing. When researchers reviewed the trials in 2019, they found that eating breakfast didn't help people lose weight, and that those who ate it tended to consume slightly more calories overall. Whether you eat breakfast matters less than what you eat during the day. If you're hungry in the morning, eat. If not, there's no need to force it.

Eating only during a limited window, say eight hours a day, has also become very popular. In a 2022 trial of 139 people with obesity, one group ate within an eight-hour window and another simply counted calories. After a year, there was no difference in weight loss. Time-restricted eating works for people who find it easier, but the best plan is the one that you can stick with.

# Vitamin D and vitamin C
@src Manson et al., NEJM, 2019; Hemilä & Chalker, Cochrane, 2013

Vitamin D is essential, and many people take supplements hoping to prevent disease. But in the large VITAL trial, 25,871 healthy adults took 2,000 units a day or a placebo for about five years. There was no reduction in cancer or heart disease. People who are actually deficient do need vitamin D, which a blood test can show, but for everyone else, extra pills don't seem to add protection.

Vitamin C has a long reputation as a cold preventer, popularized by the chemist Linus Pauling. A Cochrane review of dozens of trials found that taking it regularly doesn't prevent colds in most people. It does shorten them slightly, by about 8 percent in adults. The one exception is people under heavy physical stress, such as marathon runners and skiers, among whom it halved the risk.

# Multivitamins and fish oil
@src USPSTF, 2022; ASCEND, 2018
@conf mixed

A multivitamin is a popular daily habit. In 2022 the US Preventive Services Task Force reviewed the evidence and concluded that there isn't enough to say whether most vitamin and mineral supplements prevent cancer or heart disease in healthy adults. They haven't been shown to do harm at normal doses, but a balanced diet comes first. Some people, such as pregnant women, do need particular supplements.

Eating fish twice a week is linked with better health, so fish oil capsules seem like a natural idea. But in large trials of healthy people, including VITAL and ASCEND, omega-3 supplements didn't reduce heart attacks or strokes. It may be that the benefit comes from eating fish in place of other foods, or from other nutrients in it. For people with very high triglycerides, a prescription form can help.

# Handwashing and colds that aren't from cold
@src CDC; Eccles, 2002; Johnson & Eccles, 2005
@conf mixed

The CDC recommends washing your hands with soap and water for at least 20 seconds, which is about as long as humming "Happy Birthday" twice. Scrub the backs of your hands, between your fingers and under your nails. Soap and friction lift germs from the skin, so they're rinsed away. If you can't wash, an alcohol-based sanitizer with at least 60 percent alcohol is a good second choice.

Your grandmother may have told you to dry your hair or you'd catch a cold. Colds are caused by viruses, not by chill. But there may be a grain of truth. Cooling of the nose can weaken its defenses against viruses such as rhinovirus, and in winter, people gather indoors, where germs spread. Going out with wet hair doesn't cause an infection by itself, but it may make you more vulnerable if a virus is present.

# Herd immunity for measles
@src WHO

When enough people in a community are immune, a disease can't spread easily, and even those who can't be vaccinated are protected. This is called herd immunity. For measles, which is extremely contagious, about 95 percent of people need to be immune.

One infected person can pass it on to 12 to 18 unprotected people. That's why even a small fall in vaccination rates can lead to outbreaks. It also explains why babies who are too young for the vaccine, and people with weakened immune systems, depend on the people around them. Vaccination protects the person who gets it, and it also protects the people around them, which is part of what makes it a community effort.

# Sunscreen
@src Green et al., J Clin Oncol, 2011

A sunscreen of SPF 30 blocks about 97 percent of the burning UVB rays, and SPF 50 blocks about 98 percent, so the gain beyond 30 is small. In an Australian trial in the 1990s, people told to use sunscreen daily had about half as many new melanomas, the most dangerous skin cancer, as the others, after about ten years of follow-up.

Reapply every two hours and after swimming, as no sunscreen blocks everything. A broad-brimmed hat, a shirt and shade work as well, and they don't wash off. Most of the damage to the skin comes from sun exposure over many years, so it's worth building good habits early. The sun is also the main way our skin makes vitamin D, so the aim is to avoid burns, and not to avoid the sun entirely.

# Quitting smoking
@src Jha et al., NEJM, 2013

In a large study published in 2013, researchers followed more than 200,000 American adults. Smokers who quit between the ages of 25 and 34 gained about ten years of life compared with those who kept smoking, essentially reaching the life expectancy of those who'd never smoked. Quitting between 35 and 44 gained about nine years, and between 45 and 54, about six.

Even those who quit at 65 gained some years. It's never too late to get something out of quitting. It often takes several tries, and that's normal. Nicotine replacement, medicines and counseling can each improve the chances of success, and a doctor can help to find the right combination. Each attempt teaches you something that can be used next time.

# Alcohol and health
@src WHO, 2023; Stockwell et al., 2016
@conf mixed

In 2023 the World Health Organization stated that no level of alcohol consumption is entirely safe for health. For years, studies suggested that moderate drinking might protect the heart. But a 2016 analysis found that the apparent benefit mostly disappeared when it was taken into account that the "non-drinkers" in many studies included former drinkers who'd quit because of illness, which made abstainers look unhealthy.

The risks rise with the amount, and they include several cancers, liver disease and accidents. It doesn't mean that one glass of wine is a disaster. It means that no one should take up drinking for their health, and that drinking less is generally better than drinking more. Science on this is still moving, and the best advice is to know the numbers and make your own informed choice.

# Normal body temperature
@src Wunderlich, 1868; Protsiv et al., eLife, 2020

The "normal" body temperature of 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit came from a huge study of about 25,000 people by the German doctor Carl Wunderlich in the 1850s. Modern measurements find the average is lower, about 97.5 to 97.9, and a 2020 study found that the average in the US has been drifting downward, by about 0.03 degrees Celsius for each decade of birth.

It also varies by time of day and between people. It tends to be lowest in the early morning and highest in the late afternoon. Possible reasons for the drift include less infection and inflammation in modern life. The lesson is that "normal" is a range and not a single number. What matters is how your own temperature compares with your usual, and how you feel.

# Blood pressure
@src American Heart Association

Blood pressure is written as two numbers, such as 120 over 80. The top number is the pressure in your arteries as the heart beats, and the bottom is the pressure between beats. Normal is below 120 over 80.

High blood pressure usually has no symptoms, so the only way to know is to have it measured. It's a major risk factor for heart disease and stroke, and it's treatable. A single reading can be misleading, since pressure rises when you are stressed or have just been hurrying, so doctors look at several. Less salt, more exercise, a healthy weight and, where needed, medicine can bring it down. It's among the most useful numbers to know about your own body.

# Three body myths: knuckles, gum and nails
@src Unger, 1998; Standard medical references

The idea that cracking your knuckles causes arthritis is a persistent myth. The popping sound comes from gas bubbles in the joint fluid. A doctor named Donald Unger cracked the knuckles of his left hand, but not his right, at least twice a day for more than 60 years, to test it. He found no arthritis in either hand, and he received an Ig Nobel Prize in 2009.

You may have heard that swallowed gum stays in your stomach for seven years. That's false. Gum base isn't digested like other food, but the gut moves it along like other indigestible things, such as seeds or fiber, and it passes out within a few days. And it's a classic scary story that hair and nails keep growing after death. They don't. Growth requires the energy supply of living cells. What happens is that skin dries out and pulls back, which exposes more of the nails and the stubble on a face, and makes them look longer.

# CPR and the Bee Gees
@src American Heart Association; Matlock et al., 2012

In cardiopulmonary resuscitation, you push hard and fast on the center of the chest, about 100 to 120 times a minute, to keep blood flowing to the brain. If someone's heart stops, the first few minutes are critical, and what a bystander does can decide whether they live.

The Bee Gees song "Stayin' Alive" has about 103 beats per minute, and in one study, people who practiced to it kept good pace. If you see someone collapse, call emergency services first, and use an automated external defibrillator if there's one nearby. These machines give spoken instructions, and they won't shock someone who doesn't need it. Many communities offer short courses, and learning takes only an afternoon.

# Stroke: FAST
@src American Stroke Association

A stroke happens when blood flow to part of the brain is blocked or a vessel bursts. The acronym FAST helps you recognize it: Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, and Time to call emergency services. Treatment works best within a few hours.

On average, the brain loses about 1.9 million neurons for each minute that a stroke goes untreated, which is why doctors say "time is brain." If you see any of the signs, don't wait to see if they go away. Note the time that the symptoms began, because it affects which treatments can be used. A call made quickly can mean the difference between full recovery and lasting disability.

# Heart attacks differ in women
@src American Heart Association

The classic picture of a heart attack, crushing chest pain and pain down the left arm, is more typical of men. Women, while they often do have chest discomfort too, are more likely to have other symptoms: unusual tiredness, nausea, shortness of breath, or pain in the jaw, neck or back.

Because the signs can be less obvious, women sometimes delay getting help, and doctors sometimes miss it. Any of these symptoms deserves a call for help. Don't drive yourself if you suspect a heart attack. An ambulance crew can begin treatment on the way. It's one of those areas of medicine where research has long focused on men, and where awareness has been slowly catching up.

# Drowning is quiet
@src Pia, 1974

In films, drowning looks like a person thrashing and shouting. In reality, it's usually silent. A person in trouble can't call out because they need all their breath just to breathe. Their arms tend to press down on the water, they stay upright, and it can take as little as 20 to 60 seconds.

It's why parents are advised to watch children near water constantly, and not to rely on hearing a splash or a cry. A child who has gone quiet in the water is a reason for alarm. For adults, the signs to look for include a head low in the water, eyes that look glassy, and no kicking. Lifeguards are trained to recognize it, and it's worth knowing for anyone who spends time near water.

# Pain is not the same as damage
@src Melzack & Wall, 1965; Moseley & Butler, 2015

How much something hurts depends on much more than how badly the body is hurt. The brain decides how to interpret signals from the body, and mood, attention, past experience and the situation all change the result. Soldiers with serious wounds sometimes report little pain, and people with no visible damage can have severe pain.

Both are real. Understanding this can be a helpful part of managing chronic pain. It doesn't mean that the pain is "all in your head," since the pain is produced by the brain and body together, and it feels exactly as bad as it feels. It means that the nervous system can become oversensitive, and that things like gentle movement, good sleep and reassurance can help to turn the volume down.

# Time in nature and bright light
@src White et al., Scientific Reports, 2019; Rosenthal et al., 1984

In a 2019 survey of nearly 20,000 people in England, those who spent at least 120 minutes a week in green spaces, such as parks and woods, were significantly more likely to report good health and high well-being than people who spent none. There was no benefit with less than that. It didn't matter if the time was in one long visit or several short ones. It's a measure of association, but it fits other research.

Some people become depressed each winter, when the days are short. In 1984 the psychiatrist Norman Rosenthal and his colleagues described the condition, which they called seasonal affective disorder. A common treatment is to sit in front of a special lamp that gives off 10,000 lux of bright light for about 30 minutes in the morning. It has helped many people, and is considered a first-line treatment for the condition.

# Probiotics
@src Hill et al., 2014
@conf mixed

Probiotics are live bacteria in foods like yogurt or in supplements, and they're marketed for all kinds of health benefits. The evidence is good for a few things, such as preventing diarrhea that's caused by antibiotics.

For general gut health in healthy people, though, results are mixed and depend on the specific strain. Claims on labels often run ahead of what research can support. A strain that helps with one condition may do nothing for another, and the bacteria in a capsule may not survive the stomach. Fermented foods like yogurt, kefir and sauerkraut are inexpensive and can be a pleasant part of a varied diet, though the evidence for their benefits is still being collected.

# Small habits: posture, eye breaks and stretching
@src Slater et al., 2019; Sheppard & Wolffsohn, 2018; Behm et al., 2016
@conf mixed

We're often told to sit up straight to avoid back pain. But reviews of the research haven't found a strong link between slouching and pain. A more useful rule may be that the best posture is the next one: changing position regularly, instead of staying in any one posture for a long time. And for back pain that you already have, staying active generally helps more than resting in bed.

To reduce eye strain from long stretches at a screen, eye doctors suggest the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something at least 20 feet away for 20 seconds. Remembering to blink fully also helps. And holding a stretch for a long time before lifting weights or sprinting has been found in some studies to briefly reduce muscle power. A dynamic warm-up, with light movements like leg swings, is a better way to prepare. The research behind each of these is limited, but all three are sensible.
