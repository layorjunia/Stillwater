# Counting in Latin and Greek: uni-, mono-, bi-, tri-

English borrowed its number prefixes from two languages, and once you notice them, you start to see them everywhere. Uni- is Latin for "one." A unicorn has one horn, a uniform is made of one form so that everyone looks alike, and unity is the state of being one. Even "universe" comes from words meaning "turned into one," and "unique" has the same root.

Mono- is the Greek word for "one" or "alone." A monologue is a speech by one person, a monotone stays on one note, and a monopoly is a market with a single seller. Bi- means "two," so a bicycle has two wheels and a bilingual person speaks two languages. Tri- means "three," from both Latin and Greek, which is why we have triangles, tricycles and trilogies. When a word begins with one of these, a number is probably hiding inside it.

# Many and all: poly-, multi-, omni-, pan-

Two prefixes mean "many," one from each language. Poly- is Greek, and it gives us polygon, a shape with many angles, and polyglot, someone who speaks many languages. Polytheism is belief in many gods, and a polymath is a person who has learned many things, like Leonardo da Vinci. Multi- is the Latin version, more common in modern words. To multiply is to make many, multimedia mixes text, sound and video, and a multitude is a great crowd.

Two more mean "all." Omni- is Latin, and an omnivore eats all kinds of food. Omnipotent means all-powerful, and the word "omnibus," which means "for all," is where we get "bus." Pan- is the Greek partner. A panorama is a view of everything, a pandemic affects people over a wide area, and the Pantheon in Rome was a temple to all the gods. The pairs work in just the same way, and each language has its own.

# Big and small: macro-, micro-, mega-

These three prefixes are all about size, and they're all Greek. Macro- means "large" or "long," and it's used when you look at the big picture. Macroeconomics studies a whole economy, such as national employment and prices. Something macroscopic is big enough to see with the naked eye, and a macrocosm is the large world, in contrast to the small one inside it.

Micro- is its opposite, and it means "small." A microscope lets you see small things, a microbe is a small living thing, and a microchip is a tiny piece of electronics. Microeconomics looks at the choices of individual people and firms. In measurement, micro- stands for a millionth. Mega- comes from the Greek for "great." A megaphone makes your voice great, or loud, and a megabyte is a million bytes. A megalith is a great stone, like the ones at Stonehenge, and megalomania is an obsession with power.

# Over and under: hyper-, hypo-, super-, sub-

Two pairs of prefixes mean "over" and "under." In Greek, hyper- means "over" or "too much," and hypo- means "under" or "below." Someone hyperactive is too active. Hypertension is high blood pressure, and something hypersensitive reacts too strongly. Hypothermia is when the body temperature drops below normal, and a hypodermic needle goes under the skin. A hypothesis is something placed under an argument as its foundation.

Latin has its own pair. Super- means "above." To supervise is to look over someone's work, something superior is higher in quality, and the supernatural is above nature. Sub- means "under." A submarine travels under the sea, a subway runs under the street, and to subtract is to draw away from underneath. Sub- often changes its spelling before certain letters, as in "suppose" and "suffer." With these four, you can read the direction of many words.

# Between and beyond: inter-, intra-, extra-, ultra-

Four Latin prefixes tell you where something is happening. Inter- means "between" or "among." To interact is to act between each other, international means between nations, and an intermission is a break between two parts of a show. Even the word "internet" is a network between networks. Intra- means "within," and it's the one that's easiest to mix up with inter-. An intravenous drip goes into a vein, and an intranet is a network used only within an organization.

Extra- means "outside" or "beyond." Something extraordinary goes beyond the ordinary, an extraterrestrial comes from beyond Earth, and an extrovert is someone turned outward. Ultra- means "beyond" too. Ultraviolet light lies beyond violet in the spectrum, ultrasound is sound beyond human hearing, and ultramarine was a blue pigment from "beyond the sea." These days, ultra- has become a general word for "extremely." A good way to keep inter- and intra- apart is that "inter" is between, as in "international," and "intra" is inside.

# Across and around: trans-, circum-, peri-, para-

Trans- is Latin for "across" or "through." To transport is to carry across, to translate is to carry a meaning across from one language to another, and to transform is to change shape. Something transparent lets light shine through. The prefix is all about crossing from one side to the other, or from one state to another.

Circum- is Latin for "around." The circumference is the distance around a circle, and to circumnavigate is to sail around something, such as the whole world. A circumstance is something that stands around you, and to be circumspect is to look around carefully before acting. Peri- is its Greek partner, with the same meaning. A perimeter is a measure around an area, and a periscope lets you look around obstacles. Para- is Greek for "beside." Parallel lines run alongside each other, and a paramedic works beside the doctor.

# Before, after and again: pre-, ante-, post-, re-, retro-

Time has its own prefixes. Pre- means "before," and it's one of the most useful. To predict is to say something before it happens, to prevent is to come before an event and stop it, and prehistoric describes the time before written records. Ante- also means "before," in time or place. An anteroom is a small room before the main room, and antebellum means "before the war." Don't confuse ante- with anti-. Post- means "after." To postpone is to put something off until later, and a postscript, or P.S., is written after the main letter.

Re- means "again" or "back," and you can add it to almost any verb. To return is to turn back, to review is to see again, and to rebuild is to build again. Retro- is Latin for "backward." Retrospect is looking back, and a retroactive law applies to things that happened before it was passed. To retrofit is to add new equipment to something old, and "retro" on its own now means in the style of the past.

# For and against: anti-, contra-, pro-

Prefixes can take sides. Anti- is Greek for "against" or "opposite." An antibody is a protein your body makes to fight an invader, an antidote works against a poison, and an antonym is a word that means the opposite of another. Contra- is the Latin version of "against." To contradict is to speak against what someone says, and to contrast is to stand things against each other to show the difference. Contraband is goods brought in against the ban, in other words smuggled.

Pro- has several meanings, from both Latin and Greek: "forward," "in front of" and "in favor of." Progress is a step forward, to promote is to move something forward, and to be proactive is to act ahead of events. A proponent speaks in favor of something. The "in favor of" sense is the opposite of con, as in the old phrase "pros and cons." So the next time you see a word beginning with one of these, ask which side it's on.

# Together and both: con-, syn-, ambi-

A few prefixes are about togetherness. Con-, or com- before some letters, is Latin for "with" or "together." To connect is to join together, a community is a group of people together, and a conference brings people together. Its Greek counterpart is syn-, which becomes sym- before certain letters. Synonyms are words that have the same meaning, from "same name." To synchronize is to make things happen at the same time, a symphony is a sounding together, and sympathy is feeling together with another person.

Ambi- is Latin for "both." An ambidextrous person is equally skilled with both hands, though the word literally means "right-handed on both sides." To be ambivalent is to have both feelings at once. Something ambiguous leads in both directions, and so it has more than one meaning. Ambient means "going around," which is why we speak of ambient light or sound, the kind that surrounds you.

# Apart, out and toward: dis-, ex-, de-, ab-, ad-

Some Latin prefixes describe the direction of movement. Dis- means "apart," "away" or "not." To disagree is to not agree, to disconnect is to unjoin, and to disperse is to scatter. Ex- means "out" or "from." To exit is to go out, to extract is to draw out, and to exclude is to shut out. With a hyphen, ex- also means "former," as in "ex-president." De- means "down," "away" or "reversal." To descend is to climb down, and to decode is to undo a code.

Ab- means "away from." Something abnormal is away from the norm, to be absent is to be away from a place, and to abduct is to lead someone away by force. Its opposite is ad-, which means "to" or "toward." To adhere is to stick to something, to advance is to move toward, and adjacent means lying next to. Ad- changes to match the next letter, as in "attract," "assist" and "arrive." Together they describe the paths of coming and going.

# Good, bad and not: bene-, mal-, dys-, a-, an-

A handful of prefixes carry a verdict. Bene- is Latin for "well" or "good." A benefit is a good result, someone benevolent wishes others well, and a benediction is a good word, a blessing. Its opposite is mal-, which means "bad" or "badly." A malfunction is a bad function, malnutrition is bad nutrition, and malaria comes from Italian words meaning "bad air," from the old belief that the disease came from foul air in swamps, before it was found to be spread by mosquitoes.

Dys- is the Greek counterpart of mal-. It means "bad," "difficult" or "disordered." Dyslexia is difficulty with reading, and a dystopia is a bad place, the opposite of a utopia, a word invented by Thomas More. The Greek prefix a-, or an- before a vowel, means "without" or "not." An atheist is without belief in a god, anonymous means without a name, and anemia literally means "without blood."

# Self, half and new: auto-, semi-, neo-

Three small prefixes are easy to use. Auto- comes from the Greek for "self," and it shows up whenever something acts by itself. An automatic machine works on its own, an autobiography is a life written by the person who lived it, and autonomy means governing yourself. An automobile is a vehicle that moves by itself, without a horse. Semi- comes from Latin and means "half." A semicircle is half a circle, a semifinal is the round before the final, and a semiconductor conducts electricity only partly, which is the basis of computer chips.

Neo- comes from the Greek for "new." Neonatal relates to newborn babies, and a neologism is a new word. Even the gas neon takes its name from the same root, since its discoverers called it "the new one" in 1898. If a prefix means "half," "new" or "self," you can usually guess the rest of the word. It's a good habit to try, since the guess is nearly always right.

# Endings in medicine: -ectomy, -itis, -scope, -phobia

Doctors use word endings the way mechanics use tools, and four of them are especially useful. The Greek ending -ectomy means "cutting out." An appendectomy is the surgical removal of the appendix, and a tonsillectomy takes out the tonsils. When you see -ectomy, a surgeon is taking something out. The ending -itis means "inflammation." Arthritis is inflammation of the joints, tonsillitis is inflammation of the tonsils, and bronchitis is inflammation of the airways. It has also become a joke ending, as in "senioritis," a student's lack of motivation near graduation.

The ending -scope comes from a Greek word for "to look at." A microscope is for seeing small things, a telescope for seeing far, and a stethoscope for looking at the chest. A kaleidoscope and even a horoscope, "a look at the hour," follow the pattern. The ending -phobia means "fear." Claustrophobia is the fear of closed spaces, xenophobia is the fear of strangers, and acrophobia is the fear of heights. Phobos was the Greek god of fear.

# Word endings: -ology, -cracy, -less, -ous, -ify, -ize

Some endings build words for you. The Greek ending -ology means "the study of," from "logos," meaning "word" or "reason." Biology is the study of life, geology is the study of the earth, and psychology is the study of the mind. The ending -cracy comes from the Greek "kratos," meaning "rule" or "power." Democracy is rule by the people, aristocracy is rule by the nobles, and bureaucracy is rule by desks, from the French word for a desk.

The English ending -less means "without." To be careless is to be without care, and something endless goes on without end. The Latin ending -ous means "full of": famous, dangerous and glorious are full of fame, danger and glory. Finally, -ify and -ize both turn words into verbs. To purify is to make pure, to simplify is to make simple, to modernize is to make modern, and to realize is to make real. In the US, -ize is the standard spelling, while some other countries use -ise.
