# A misreading that became a fabric
@conf mixed
@src Heriot-Watt University textile collection; Brewer's Dictionary

Tweed is a rough woolen cloth from Scotland. The usual story is that the name began as a mistake. Weavers around Hawick made a twilled cloth, which in Scots was called tweel. In the early 1830s a clerk for a London merchant, James Locke, read a letter from a Hawick firm about its tweels, and took the word to be tweed. He thought it was a trade name, from the River Tweed, which flows through the region.

The name caught on, and by about 1850 it was firmly established. Historians are not sure that it happened exactly like that. Different accounts give different dates, and name a London tailor instead of a merchant, and some say the river gave the name directly. It is a nice story, and it may be true in outline, but it is a traditional account. Words often get their forms from a slip of the pen.

# Did Dr. Seuss invent the nerd?
@conf mixed
@src Dr. Seuss, If I Ran the Zoo, 1950; Newsweek, 1951

The word nerd first appears in print, as far as we know, in a children's book. In If I Ran the Zoo, published in 1950, Dr. Seuss describes a boy's imagined zoo, in which he would have a Nerkle, a Nerd and a Seersucker too. The Nerd in the picture is a small, grumpy creature, with a messy tuft of hair. It has no connection with the later meaning.

In 1951 Newsweek reported on the slang of teenagers in Detroit, and said that someone who was once called a drip or a square was now a nerd. It may be that the word was already in speech before Seuss used it, and that he picked it up. The Oxford English Dictionary says that its origin is uncertain and disputed. Other stories, such as one about a ventriloquist's dummy, have been rejected. We know when the word was first written down, but not who said it first.

# The hospital that gave us a word for chaos
@src Bethlem Royal Hospital, founded 1247

In 1247 a priory was founded in London, outside the city wall, in the name of St. Mary of Bethlehem. Over time it began to take in people who were mentally ill, and by the fourteenth century it was a hospital. Londoners had trouble with the name, and called it Bedleem, and then Bedlam. The hospital, now called Bethlem Royal Hospital, still exists, and it is still a place for mental health care.

In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the conditions were bad, and visitors came to watch the patients, which was a form of entertainment. The word bedlam came to mean any madhouse, and then, by the early 1600s, any place of uproar and confusion. A word that was once the name of a place has gone through a change of meaning. Its history is a reminder of how mentally ill people were once treated.

# The elephant that made everything large
@conf mixed
@src London Museum; Canada's History

In 1865 an African elephant called Jumbo arrived at the London Zoo. He grew to be one of the largest elephants in captivity, about eleven feet at the shoulder, and children rode on his back. In 1882 the zoo sold him to the American showman P. T. Barnum, for about two thousand pounds. The sale caused a public outcry in Britain, and many people wrote to the Queen to protest. Jumbo crossed the Atlantic, and Barnum's circus made him a star.

Jumbo died on September 15, 1885, in St. Thomas, Ontario, when a freight train struck him as he was being led back to the circus train after a show. The name was already used in English before the elephant, and some people think it came from an African word. But it was Jumbo who made it mean something huge. Today jumbo is used for jets, shrimp, and bags of potato chips.

# The shoe that was never thrown
@src Etymonline; Merriam-Webster; Grammarphobia

It is often said that sabotage comes from the sabot, the wooden shoe worn by French workers, and that angry workers threw their shoes into machines to break them. It is a good story, and there is no evidence for it. The word comes from the French verb saboter, which meant to walk noisily, clattering in wooden shoes, and then to do a job badly, or to botch it.

By the 1890s French labor movements had begun to use the word for the deliberate slowing of work, as a tactic in labor disputes. It came into English in the early 1900s, notably in reports on a railway strike in France in 1910. Later the word was applied to the destruction of machines and to acts of war, as in sabotage behind enemy lines. The word arrived from a sound, and the shoe story was invented afterward.

# Where the word lynch came from
@conf mixed
@src Etymonline; Oxford English Dictionary

The word lynch refers to a killing by a mob, without a trial. It is almost certainly from the surname Lynch, but there are two candidates, both of them from Virginia in the 1780s. Colonel Charles Lynch, a justice of the peace, held informal trials of Loyalists in his home county, and he wrote in 1782 about Lynch's Law. The earlier documented use is his.

William Lynch, from another county in Virginia, was also named, since in 1811 he claimed that the phrase came from a compact that he had signed in 1780. Scholars doubt that the document is genuine. A third story, about a mayor of Galway who is supposed to have hanged his own son in 1493, has been rejected. The phrase Lynch law appears in print by 1811, and lynching in 1835. The word has a dark history, and the exact origin is uncertain.

# Blackmail was once a payment for protection
@conf mixed
@src Merriam-Webster; Dictionary of the Scots Language

In the sixteenth century, on the border between Scotland and England, bands of armed men raided farms and stole cattle. Landowners and farmers found that they could avoid raids by paying a local chief, who promised protection from his own men and from others. The payment was called black mail. Mail was an old Scottish word for rent or tribute, probably from Old Norse. Black may have marked it as illegal, as opposed to the lawful rent, but the reason is not certain.

The term was in use by the 1550s. It had nothing to do with the postal mail, and the idea that it meant extortion by letter is false. By the nineteenth century the word was used for any demand for money under threat, such as the threat of exposing a secret. It is among the many words in English which came from the long, violent history of the border.

# The word that gave away who you were
@src Book of Judges, chapter 12

In the Book of Judges, in the Hebrew Bible, a war broke out between the people of Gilead, led by Jephthah, and the tribe of Ephraim. After the battle, the Gileadites seized the crossing places of the Jordan River, to catch the Ephraimites who were trying to flee. Anyone who came to the ford and said that he was not an Ephraimite was asked to say the word shibboleth.

The Ephraimites said sibboleth, because their dialect lacked the sh sound, and so they gave themselves away. The story says that forty-two thousand were killed. The word shibboleth meant ear of grain, or a flowing stream, and what mattered was the pronunciation, and not the meaning. In English it came to mean a test word, a custom, or a phrase that identifies who belongs to a group. The story shows how a small difference of speech can mark someone as an outsider.

# A victory that was too costly
@src Plutarch, Life of Pyrrhus

In 279 BC King Pyrrhus of Epirus, who had come to Italy to help a Greek city against Rome, fought the Romans at Asculum. He had war elephants and many trained men, and he won the field. But he lost a very large number of his best officers and soldiers, and he could not replace them as the Romans could. According to the Greek writer Plutarch, a companion congratulated him, and Pyrrhus answered that one more victory like that would ruin him.

Historians disagree on whether he really won, and some say that the Romans did. The phrase Pyrrhic victory entered English in the early nineteenth century, to mean a win that costs so much that it is almost like a defeat. It applies to wars, to lawsuits and to political fights. The question to ask is not just whether you won, but what you paid.

# The man whose name means to censor
@series people
@src Smithsonian Magazine; Oxford English Dictionary

In 1807 a book appeared in London called The Family Shakespeare. It offered the plays of Shakespeare with the parts that were thought unsuitable for reading aloud in a family left out. The first edition came out without a name, and it was mostly the work of Henrietta Maria Bowdler, who was known as Harriet, the sister of a physician, Thomas Bowdler. She was a writer on religion, and she had done the cutting.

In 1818 Thomas published a second edition, in ten volumes, with more plays, under his own name. It was a success, and was reprinted many times. In 1836 the verb bowdlerize appeared in print, and it was used in mockery, for the removal of anything improper from a book. The word is now used for any censoring that is excessive. A woman did most of the work, and a man gave the word his name.

# Written in blood
@src Plutarch, Life of Solon

In about 621 BC the Athenians asked a man called Draco to write down their laws. Until then they had been remembered and applied by the nobles, who could interpret them as they pleased. Draco's code was the first in Athens to be written down for all to see, which was a step toward fairness. But it was famous for its severity. Even small offenses, such as stealing a cabbage, were said to carry the penalty of death.

An old saying, which Plutarch repeated, was that Draco's laws were written not in ink but in blood. A later lawmaker, Solon, repealed most of them, but kept the law on murder. From Draco we have the word draconian, for a law or measure that is harsh. He was in fact a reformer, in that he put the law in writing, and the word remembers only his severity.

# A reply of one word
@src Plutarch; Laconia, ancient Sparta

Sparta was in a region of Greece called Laconia, and its people were known for speaking very briefly. We still use the word laconic for a short, blunt way of speaking. A story that has come down from ancient writers, including Plutarch, illustrates it. Philip II of Macedon, who had conquered much of Greece, sent the Spartans a message. If I enter Laconia, he wrote, I will raze Sparta to the ground.

The Spartans sent back a single word, which was If. Whether it really happened is uncertain, since the anecdote has been told for centuries, and some scholars suspect that it grew with the telling. But it has served for a model of how much can be said in a few words. The Spartans trained their children to answer quickly and with wit. The word reminds us that short speech can have force.

# Water of life
@src Etymonline; Oxford English Dictionary

Many words for strong drink began as words for water or fire. Whiskey comes from the Gaelic uisce beatha, which means water of life. It was a translation of the Latin aqua vitae, which medieval alchemists and monks used for distilled spirits. Over time uisce beatha was worn down by speakers, to usquebaugh, and then to whisky.

Vodka is from the Russian voda, water, with an ending that makes it little, so it means little water. Brandy comes from the Dutch brandewijn, which means burned wine, because it is made by heating wine, to distill it. Each name tells something of the way that the drink is made, or of the way that people felt about it. They also show how much of our vocabulary for drink came from languages across Europe.

# The left-hand word
@src Etymonline

In Latin the word for left was sinister. In English it came to mean evil, or threatening, as in a sinister plot. How the meaning changed is not certain, but in many cultures the left side and the left hand have been thought unlucky. In Latin the word for right was dexter. We get dexterity from it, meaning skill, and a person who is dexterous is good with the hands.

The French word for left is gauche, which English has borrowed to mean clumsy or awkward. English has a long record of bias against the left hand. Left-handed children were made to write with the right hand in some schools until the twentieth century. Our words keep the old prejudice, long after most people have stopped sharing it.

# The crane's foot
@src Oxford English Dictionary

A pedigree is a record of the ancestors of a person or an animal, and it has come to mean a distinguished background. The word began in medieval French as pié de grue, which means the foot of a crane. In old family trees, the lines that branched from one generation to the next were drawn with three short strokes, like the footprint of a crane, and the mark was used to show the line of descent.

The French words were borrowed into English in the fifteenth century, and were worn down in speech to pedegru and then pedigree. The mark was used to show the descent of nobles, and later of horses and dogs. A family tree is still a diagram of branches. It is a word that records, in its shape, a drawing that has been lost.

# A divided game
@src Oxford English Dictionary

Jeopardy now means danger. It comes from the Old French jeu parti, which means a divided game, or an even game. It was a term in chess and other games for a position in which the chances were equal, so that the outcome was uncertain. A player in such a position could win or lose.

In English it was first used in the fourteenth century for a risk or a danger, and the sense of a game faded. The word now is used for a legal term, in which a person who has been tried is not in double jeopardy, and for the name of a television quiz. It is a good example of a word that began in a game, and then took on a serious sense. A divided game is a risk, since you do not know who will win.

# The house of making
@src Oxford English Dictionary; Venice

An arsenal is a place where weapons are made and stored. The word came through Italian from Arabic, where dar al-sina'a means house of manufacture, or workshop. Italian traders, who had dealings with the Muslim world, borrowed it, and Venetians turned it into arzanà. The famous Arsenale of Venice was the great shipyard of the city.

It was founded in the early twelfth century, and at its height it could build and equip a warship in a matter of days, with workers who assembled pieces along a line, in a way that anticipated the factory. The word was borrowed into English in the sixteenth century. In the end it was applied to any large store of arms, and also to a football club. The word carries in it a record of the trade between Venice and the Arab world.

# Cloth named for cities
@src Oxford English Dictionary

Many fabrics are named for the cities that were known for making or selling them. Muslin, a thin cotton cloth, comes from Mosul, a city in what is now Iraq, where Europeans first saw it. Damask, a woven fabric with a pattern, takes its name from Damascus. Calico, a plain cotton cloth, came from Calicut, a port on the coast of India, which is now called Kozhikode.

Similar names are found in jeans, which are from Genoa, whose sailors wore a strong cotton cloth, and in denim, from the French serge de Nîmes. The place names are a trace of old trade routes, along which cloth moved to Europe. The words survive long after the cities stopped being the main source. In your clothes there may be a map of the medieval world.
