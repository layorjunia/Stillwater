# Paying people to quit smoking
@src Volpp et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2009

Smoking is hard to give up, and many programs have low success rates. In 2009 Kevin Volpp and his colleagues at the University of Pennsylvania tried a simple idea, to pay people. They recruited about nine hundred employees of a large company who smoked. All were offered information on quitting, and access to counseling and medicine. Half of them were also offered financial rewards for staying off cigarettes.

Those in the reward group could earn up to seven hundred and fifty dollars, for completing a cessation program, and for being confirmed as non-smokers by a test at six months, and again at nine to twelve months. Six months after the payments ended, about ten percent of them were still not smoking, compared with about four percent of the others. The rate was still low, but more than double. It's often called contingency management, and it's among the most effective ways of helping people to stop.

# Cash for a vaccine
@src Campos-Mercade, Meier, Schneider, Meier, Pope & Wengström, Science, 2021

In 2021 a team of economists in Sweden tested whether a small payment could persuade more people to be vaccinated against COVID-19. They randomly assigned about eight thousand people, who had not yet been vaccinated, to groups. One group was offered about twenty-four dollars, and the others were offered nothing. The payment was offered by letter, and the vaccination was recorded in the national register.

The payment of two hundred Swedish kronor raised the rate of vaccination by about four percentage points. It was a small effect, but cheap, and it worked at a time when others were pushing hard. The researchers argued that incentives could be one tool among others. The study also found that the money did not make people think badly of vaccination, as some had feared. It's a good example of the use of a randomized trial to answer a policy question.

# Did vaccine lotteries work?
@src Walkey et al., JAMA, 2021; Sehgal, American Journal of Medicine, 2021
@conf mixed

In May 2021 the state of Ohio launched a lottery for people who had been vaccinated against COVID-19. It was called Vax-a-Million, and offered five prizes of a million dollars. Other states copied it, with prizes of cash, scholarships and even a hunting rifle. The idea was that a chance to win a big prize would motivate people who were hesitant.

Researchers tried to find out if it worked. One study, by Allan Walkey and his colleagues, compared Ohio with other states, and found no increase in vaccinations. Another, by Neil Sehgal, found a rise in Ohio. Later work on many states was also mixed, with some showing small effects and some none. Part of the problem is that it's hard to separate the lottery from other trends. The evidence suggests that lotteries are not as effective as hoped.

# Pictures on cigarette packs
@src Noar et al., Tobacco Control, 2016

Cigarette packs in many countries carry warnings. Some use only words, and some also have a photograph, such as of a diseased lung or a rotting tooth. Canada was the first country to require picture warnings, in 2000. Does the picture make a difference? In 2016 Seth Noar and his colleagues combined the results of dozens of studies from many countries.

They found that pictorial warnings were more effective than text-only ones, at attracting attention, at making people think about the risks, and at making smokers say they intended to quit. The strongest warnings were those that were graphic and aroused strong feelings, such as fear. There is less evidence on the effect on actual smoking, but studies from countries that adopted them show small declines. Emotion draws attention in a way that statistics do not.

# A tax on sugary drinks
@src Colchero, Popkin, Rivera & Ng, BMJ, 2016

In January 2014 Mexico introduced a tax of one peso per liter on sugary drinks, about ten percent of the price. Mexico had one of the highest rates of obesity and diabetes in the world, and it was also one of the biggest consumers of soda per person. In 2016 Arantxa Colchero and her colleagues analyzed the purchases of thousands of households before and after the tax.

In the first year, the purchases of taxed drinks fell by about six percent, on average. The decline was greater for poorer households, about nine percent. Purchases of untaxed drinks, such as water, rose slightly. Later studies found that the effect continued. Similar taxes have since been introduced in many places, including cities in the United States and the United Kingdom. They reduce the amount of sugar consumed, and raise money for health programs.

# Telling people about their neighbors' taxes
@src Hallsworth, List, Metcalfe & Vlaev, Journal of Public Economics, 2017

Governments lose a lot of money to people who do not pay their taxes on time. In 2011 the British government's Behavioral Insights Team, sometimes called the Nudge Unit, ran an experiment with the tax authority. People who were late in paying their tax received a letter. Some of the letters were the normal reminder. Others added a sentence that was based on the facts, such as that nine out of ten people in the country pay their tax on time.

The letters that stressed the social norm increased the share of people who paid in the following weeks, by a few percentage points. It was most effective when the norm was local, such as saying that most people in your town have paid. The effect was small per person, but with millions of letters, it was worth millions of pounds. Telling people what others do is a cheap and powerful tool, as long as it's true.
