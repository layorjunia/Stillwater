# Brand names with made-up meanings
@series words
@src Company histories of Adidas and ArmaLite; General Motors and the Nova

The name Adidas is said to stand for All day I dream about sports. It does not. The company was named for its founder, Adolf Dassler, who was known as Adi, and the name was made from his nickname and the first letters of his surname. It was registered in 1949. The earliest published appearance of the dream backronym that has been found is from 1978, and it was a joke.

The letters AR in AR-15 are often thought to stand for assault rifle. They stand for ArmaLite Rifle. ArmaLite was the company that designed the weapon in the 1950s, before it sold the rights to Colt.

Another favorite is that the Chevrolet Nova sold badly in Spanish-speaking countries, because no va in Spanish means it doesn't go. The car sold well in Latin America, and General Motors did not rename it. People easily understood nova as meaning new. All three stories give a name a hidden joke or hidden fault, and in each case the truth is more ordinary. A backronym is a meaning given to a name after the fact.

# The business legends that are too good
@series sell
@src Netflix founders' accounts; Coca-Cola advertising history; PepsiCo barter deals, 1989

It is said that Netflix began when its co-founder, Reed Hastings, was charged a forty dollar late fee by Blockbuster, for returning a copy of the film Apollo 13 late. The story made the company seem to have been born from a customer's annoyance. Hastings has said that he made it up, as a short way of explaining what Netflix offered. The founders were actually inspired by Amazon, and by the idea of selling things by mail.

Another legend is that the Coca-Cola Company invented the modern image of Santa Claus, as a jolly man in red. It did not. By the late nineteenth century, Santa had already taken that form in American popular culture, long before Coca-Cola began to use him in its advertising in the 1930s. The company did help to make the picture famous, but it did not create it. Nor was the Coca-Cola contour bottle designed by the industrial designer Raymond Loewy.

A third is that Pepsi owned the sixth most powerful navy in the world. In 1989 Pepsi traded with the Soviet Union, taking some decommissioned warships in a barter deal for its drink. The oil tankers were leased out or sold, and the other ships were sold for scrap. A follow-on deal involved ten more ships. Pepsi never had a navy, but the joke was too good to resist.

# The white statues that were once bright
@series history
@src Studies of ancient polychromy; Vatican and museum research

When we picture Greek and Roman sculpture, we see white marble. The statues were not made that way. They were painted in bright colors. The paint has worn away over the centuries, and what we see now is the bare stone. Some of the best-preserved statues still bear traces of their original colors.

Researchers have found the traces with ultraviolet light and with other methods, and have made reconstructions that show the statues as they might have looked, with bright clothing, painted eyes, and skin tones. The reconstructions can look garish to modern eyes. They reveal that the white ideal that came to dominate Western taste was a result of ruins and not of the original design.

The mistake had a long influence. When Renaissance and later artists found ancient statues, they were already stripped of paint, and they took the white stone as the ancients' ideal. Neoclassical art, with its pale marble figures, followed. A related myth concerns Michelangelo, who is said to have painted the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel lying on his back. He painted it standing up, on a scaffold, with his head tilted back.

# The laughing Buddha is not the Buddha
@series history
@src Chinese Buddhist folk tradition; Budai

In many Chinese restaurants and shops, there is a statue of a bald, fat, smiling man, often with a sack and a big belly. He is known in the West as the laughing Buddha or the fat Buddha. It is often thought to be a picture of the Buddha, Siddhartha Gautama, who founded Buddhism. It is not. It is a figure called Budai.

Budai is a folk hero of Chinese Buddhism, who is said to have been a monk who lived about a thousand years ago. He is a symbol of contentment and good luck, and he is said to carry a sack of gifts for children. His name means cloth sack. His statue is believed to bring prosperity.

The word Buddha is a title, and means one who is awakened. In Buddhism it can be applied to more than one person, so both Budai and Siddhartha may be called by it. But they are two separate figures. Siddhartha is most often shown as a slim man, sitting in meditation, with a calm face. The cheerful monk belongs to a different tradition of Chinese popular religion.

# What the Christmas story does not say
@series history
@src The Gospels of Matthew and Luke; historians of the Roman Empire

Jesus was most likely not born on December 25. The date was chosen for the celebration of Christmas later, by the Church. It is more probable that his birth was in the spring or perhaps summer. The calendar that we use is supposed to count years from his birth, but he was probably not born in the year AD 1, or 1 BC. Modern historians put his birth between 6 and 4 BC, since King Herod, who is named in the story, died in 4 BC.

The Bible does not say that three wise men came to visit the baby. It says that magi came, which means that there was more than one, and three are inferred from the three gifts. It does not say they were kings, or that they rode camels, or that they were named Caspar, Melchior and Balthazar. Those details were added in later centuries.

Another mistaken idea is that Mary Magdalene was a prostitute before she met Jesus. It is not in the Bible or in any of the earliest Christian writings. The idea comes from a later tradition, in which she was identified with other women in the Gospels, and in the sixth century Pope Gregory I helped to spread it. Whether those women were the same person has been disputed in several traditions.

# Five things about early Christianity that are not so
@series history
@src Acts 13:9; Council of Nicaea, 325; Edict of Thessalonica, 380; Gregory I

Saint Paul is often said to have changed his name from Saul when he became a Christian. He did not. He was born a Jew who had inherited Roman citizenship from his father, and he had both a Hebrew name and a Greco-Roman name from birth. The Acts of the Apostles says of Saul, who also is called Paul.

The Council of Nicaea, which met in 325, did not decide which books belong in the Bible. The Old Testament had probably been settled by Jewish scribes before the time of Christ. The New Testament canon was largely formed in the third century, before the council, and it was finalized, along with the deuterocanon, at the Council of Rome in 382.

The emperor Constantine did not make Christianity the official religion of the Roman Empire. He was the first Christian emperor, and he promoted religious tolerance by the Edict of Milan in 313. But Christianity became the official religion only in 380, some 43 years after his death.

The Seven Deadly Sins are also not in the Bible. The list was developed by early Christian monks and theologians, and Pope Gregory I gave it in the form that is now common. The tradition is rich, but it is later than the texts that people assume it comes from.

# Three doctrines that are often mixed up
@series history
@src First Vatican Council, 1870; Catholic teaching on Mary; history of the LDS Church

The Immaculate Conception is often taken to mean that Mary conceived Jesus as a virgin. It does not. That is the virgin birth, a separate Christian doctrine. The Immaculate Conception is the Roman Catholic belief that Mary herself was free from original sin from the moment she was conceived by her own parents, whom tradition names as Joachim and Anne.

Another common idea among non-Catholics is that the pope is sinless, or that everything he says is infallible. Catholic dogma does not say so. Since 1870, it has held that when the pope speaks ex cathedra, that is, officially and from the chair of Peter, on a matter of faith or morals, he is protected from error. It does not say he is free from error in other statements, or from sin.

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints no longer practices polygamy. The church ended the practice more than a century ago, and now excommunicates members who take part in it. Some fundamentalist groups that broke away from the church do practice polygamy, and are often confused with it. Careful distinctions are needed where beliefs are strongly held.

# Four words from Islam that are often misunderstood
@series words
@src Islamic law and Arabic usage; Quranic scholarship

The burqa, the niqab and the hijab are often mixed up. A burqa covers the whole body, the head and the face, with a mesh panel to see through. A niqab covers the hair and the face, except for the eyes. A hijab covers the hair and chest, but not the face. Not all Muslim women wear a face or head covering.

A fatwa is often thought to be a death sentence. It is a legal opinion, which is given by an Islamic scholar, and it is generally not binding. Different scholars often give different opinions. The idea that it means a death sentence comes from the decree in 1989 of the Iranian leader, Ayatollah Khomeini, against the author Salman Rushdie. It is debated whether that was a fatwa in the strict sense.

The word jihad does not always mean holy war. Its literal meaning in Arabic is struggle. It includes the idea of fighting, but it can also mean any spiritual or moral effort, such as seeking knowledge or putting others before oneself. And the Quran does not promise martyrs 72 virgins in paradise. It speaks of companions called houri, who are promised to all people in heaven, not only to martyrs.

# The fruit that was not an apple
@series history
@src Book of Genesis; Jewish burial customs

In Western art, the forbidden fruit in the Garden of Eden is nearly always an apple. The Book of Genesis does not say so. The original Hebrew text mentions only a fruit. The tradition of the apple arose later, in art and in popular retelling, and it is thought to have been helped by a pun in Latin, in which the word for apple, malum, is also close to the word for evil.

Many Jewish people hold a belief about tattoos. The Book of Leviticus forbids marking the body, and some American Jews believe that a person with a tattoo cannot be buried in a Jewish cemetery. This is not so. Jews with tattoos are not barred, just as people who have broken other prohibitions are not barred from burial in a Jewish cemetery.

Both are examples of religious ideas that gather extra detail over time. The apple was supplied by artists. The tattoo rule was made up by popular belief, and then taken as the law. Most religions have such layers, in which the original text says one thing and the practice and the pictures add more.

# Five stories from the world of sports
@series history
@src Histories of golf, baseball, judo and the 1950 World Cup

The name of the game of golf is said to be an acronym for Gentlemen Only, Ladies Forbidden. It is not. It may come from the Dutch word kolf, which means a club, or from a Scottish word, gowf, which means to strike. Baseball was not invented by Abner Doubleday in Cooperstown, New York, as the story goes. It had no single inventor. It evolved from bat and ball games such as rounders, and took its modern form in New York City.

The black belt in martial arts does not necessarily mean an expert. It was introduced for judo in the 1880s to show that a student had learned all the basic techniques. What it takes to go beyond the first black belt rank, called 1st dan, differs from one martial art to another.

Artificial turf is not free of maintenance. It must be raked and patched to keep it safe. India did not withdraw from the 1950 World Cup because its players wanted to play barefoot. The reason was that the national football association was not ready to send a team. The shoeless story is much more fun, and has been told for decades.

# Rumors about video games
@series tech
@src Sid Meier on Civilization; Dragon Quest developers; Space Invaders, 1978

It is a favorite story among players that in the first Civilization game, the peaceful leader Mahatma Gandhi became wildly aggressive, and started dropping nuclear weapons, because of a bug in which his aggression score went below zero and wrapped around to the maximum. It is called Nuclear Gandhi. But the glitch never existed, in the original game or in Civilization II. The designer, Sid Meier, said that the rumor seems to have come from internet pages, and was spread by a website and an article. Gandhi was made unusually aggressive as a joke in Civilization V in 2010, and as a reference to the legend in Civilization VI in 2016.

Another is that the Japanese government banned the Dragon Quest games from being released on weekdays, because too many schoolchildren skipped class to buy them. No law ever did this. The rule is one that the makers imposed on themselves.

A third is that when Space Invaders came out in 1978, it caused a shortage of 100 yen coins in Japan. It did not. The claim came from an advertising campaign by the maker, Taito, and from an error in an article in the magazine New Scientist in 1980.

# Do violent video games make people violent?
@series mind
@conf mixed
@src Research reviews on video games and aggression

It has become common for people to say that violent video games make players violent, and the idea has often been raised after violent crimes. There is no definitive proof of it. Some studies have found a small short-term rise in aggressive thoughts or feelings after playing. Others have found no link between games and aggression.

Over the same years that games grew in popularity, youth violence went down in many countries. The two trends may not be connected, and no one has shown that one caused the other. But the decline does not support the claim that games make society more violent. Those who study the question disagree about how to interpret the research, and about how to measure aggression.

Panics about new forms of entertainment are old. In earlier times, books, comic books, films and rock music were all blamed for harming the young. The concern over video games, from the 1980s to the present, has led to studies, laws in many countries, and a lot of public debate. It is a reminder that a belief that seems obvious may be hard to test, and that a question that has been asked many times may still not be settled.
