# A schedule for remembering
@conf mixed
@src Pimsleur, A Memory Schedule, Modern Language Journal, 1967

In 1967 the language teacher Paul Pimsleur published a short paper called A Memory Schedule. He argued that a fact is best kept in memory by recalling it at gaps that grow longer each time, and he named the idea graduated interval recall. His example started with a gap of five seconds, then 25 seconds, then 125 seconds, so that each gap was about five times the last.

Sources list the full schedule as eleven steps: 5 seconds, 25 seconds, 2 minutes, 10 minutes, 1 hour, 5 hours, 1 day, 5 days, 25 days, 4 months and 2 years. His company still builds its audio courses on the same idea, so that a new word comes back just as it is about to be forgotten.

The principle of spacing is well supported. The exact schedule is less certain. A later study at the University of Victoria found that, for long-term retention, a uniform delay did better than a graduated one. The safe lesson is that reviewing at widening gaps works, and that the precise gaps matter less than doing it.

# Six verbs for learning
@src Bloom et al., 1956; Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001

In 1956 the educator Benjamin Bloom and a group of colleagues published a classification of the thinking that schools ask for. It had six levels, from simple to complex: knowledge, comprehension, application, analysis, synthesis and evaluation. The idea was to help teachers write goals and tests that asked for more than recall.

In 2001 Lorin Anderson and David Krathwohl, who had been one of Bloom's collaborators, published a revision. It changed the names from nouns to verbs: remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate and create. It also moved creating to the top, above evaluating. The revision added a second scale for the kind of knowledge involved, which can be factual, conceptual, procedural or about thinking itself.

The taxonomy is used in schools around the world. Not everyone agrees that the levels form a strict ladder, and some argue that a person may need to create before fully understanding. As a practical tool, it asks a good question of any lesson. Are the students only remembering, or are they using what they know?

# Learning from solved problems
@src Sweller & Cooper, Cognition and Instruction, 1985

Intuition says that the best way to learn algebra is to solve many problems. In 1985 John Sweller and Graham Cooper found that this is not always so. They compared students who solved problems with students who studied worked examples, which show each step of a solution. The students who studied examples did better on later problems.

In their design, the examples were alternated with similar problems, so that a student would study a solution and then try one. Sweller explained the result by the limits of working memory. A beginner who tries to solve a problem has to search for a method, and that search uses up the mental space that would be needed to learn the pattern.

The finding became a starting point for cognitive load theory. Later work found that it fades as learners become more expert, an effect called expertise reversal, and for them it may be better to solve problems. For a beginner the lesson is plain. Study how a solution works before trying to make your own.

# The poet who named the dead
@src Cicero, De Oratore, 55 BC; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy

According to the Roman writer Cicero, the first memory trick was found by accident. The poet Simonides of Ceos was paid to recite a poem at a banquet given by a nobleman, Scopas, in Crannon in Thessaly. The poem praised the twin gods Castor and Pollux, and Scopas, annoyed, said that he would pay only half, and that the twins could pay the rest.

A message came that two young men were waiting outside for Simonides. He went out, and no one was there. Behind him the roof fell in and killed Scopas and his guests, and the bodies were crushed past recognition. Simonides could tell the families who had been who, because he remembered where each guest had been lying at the table.

Cicero drew the lesson that order is the key to memory. One should choose places, picture the things to be remembered, and set the pictures in the places. This is the method of loci, or memory palace, and it was written up in a Latin handbook, the Ad Herennium, about 86 BC. The story is a legend, and nothing outside Cicero's telling confirms it. The method itself still works.

# Sleep and a skill
@conf mixed
@src Karni, Tanne, Rubenstein, Askenasy & Sagi, Science, 1994

People who practice a skill often do better the next morning than they did when they stopped. In 1994 Avi Karni, Dorit Tanne and their colleagues asked whether sleep is the reason. They used a visual task in which people had to pick out a small pattern against a textured background, and they tested people before and after a night.

After a normal night of sleep, performance improved. When the researchers woke people each time they entered REM sleep, the stage with vivid dreams, the improvement did not appear. Waking people from deep slow-wave sleep did not have this effect. Losing REM sleep did not harm a similar task that had been learned earlier. The authors concluded that this kind of memory consolidation depends strongly on REM sleep.

Later work has made the picture more complicated. A study of naps found that a nap of 60 to 90 minutes can produce the same kind of learning, when it contains both slow-wave and REM sleep. The exact role of each stage is still argued. The finding that sleep is part of learning, and not just rest from it, has held up well.

# A walk before the test
@src Hillman et al., Neuroscience, 2009

Does exercise help children think? In 2009 Charles Hillman and his colleagues tested children of about nine and ten years old on two days. On one day they walked on a treadmill for 20 minutes at a moderate pace, at 60 percent of an estimate of their maximum heart rate. On the other they sat quietly for 20 minutes. After each, they did tests of attention and of school subjects.

After the walk, the children were more accurate on a task that required them to ignore distracting information, especially on the harder trials. They also did better on a test of academic achievement. The authors saw this as a link between physical activity, attention and school performance, after only a single session.

Hillman himself noted a limit. Children do not normally walk on treadmills, so the design is not like real life, and one session says little about long-term effects. But the finding fits with other work suggesting that a short bout of exercise can make it easier to concentrate for a while afterward. It is a reason to take a walk before an hour of study.

# A cousin, a notepad and a million lessons
@series history
@src Khan Academy history page; Time and Stanford GSB accounts

In August 2004 Salman Khan was working at a hedge fund in Boston when his young cousin Nadia, in New Orleans, was struggling with converting units in math. He began tutoring her over the phone, using Yahoo Doodle, a shared drawing page, to show his work. Within a couple of years he had added more than a dozen relatives and family friends to his lessons.

A friend suggested that he put the sessions on YouTube so that the cousins could watch them when they pleased. In 2006 he began to upload videos. They were simple, a voice and a hand writing on a screen, and strangers began to watch them. Khan Academy was set up as a not-for-profit organization in 2008, and in 2009, he left his job to work on it full time.

The reasons for its success are debated, and so is its effect on learning. Part of the appeal was that a student could pause, replay and go at his or her own pace, which a classroom lecture does not allow. It started with a single tutor who wanted to help one child.

# Why most people never finish the online course
@src Jordan, IRRODL, 2014 and 2015; Times Higher Education, 2013

Around 2012 free online university courses, called massive open online courses or MOOCs, drew enormous crowds. Many predicted that they would remake higher education. Katy Jordan, a researcher, collected the figures that were available on how many people who enrolled finished.

Her analysis, reported in 2013, found that the average completion rate across 29 courses was 6.8 percent. In a later paper, in 2015, with a dataset of 221 courses, completion rates ranged from 0.7 percent to 52.1 percent, and the median was 12.6 percent. Completion was defined as the share of enrolled students who completed the course.

The numbers need careful reading. Since signing up is free, many who enroll never meant to finish, and some just wanted to watch a few lectures. But the pattern shows how hard it is to keep an adult learner going without deadlines, a teacher or a cost. A course that is easy to start is also easy to leave.

# A laptop for every child
@series tech
@src Cristia, Ibarraran, Cueto, Santiago & Severin, Inter-American Development Bank, 2012

The One Laptop per Child program aimed to give every child in a poor country a cheap, sturdy laptop. Peru bought them in large numbers. In 2012 a team led by Julian Cristia, working for the Inter-American Development Bank, studied what happened in a randomized trial. Of about 320 rural primary schools, 210 were picked by lot to receive the laptops, and the rest did not.

After 15 months the researchers found no effect on test scores in math or language, and no evidence of an effect on enrollment. There were some small gains in general thinking skills, measured by a test of reasoning, a test of verbal fluency and a coding test. A separate experiment with about a thousand children in Lima also found no significant difference between children with laptops and those without.

The bank's own comment on the work was titled One Laptop per Child is not enough. A device brings the chance for learning, but it does not make the lessons. The missing parts may be the training of teachers and suitable software, though the trial did not test that.

# Letters first, then words
@conf mixed
@src Johnston & Watson, Scottish Executive, 2005

How should children be taught to read? In synthetic phonics, they learn the sounds of letters first and then blend them to make words. In the late 1990s the schools of Clackmannanshire, in Scotland, took part in a study of this. Rhona Johnston and Joyce Watson compared three groups of about 300 pupils in all. One group learned synthetic phonics, one an analytic form of phonics, and one practiced sounds without print.

At the end of primary school, seven years later, the synthetic phonics group was about three and a half years ahead of its age in word reading. It was about one year and eight months ahead in spelling, but only a few months ahead in reading comprehension. Boys were nearly a year ahead of girls in word reading, which the researchers had not expected. The synthetic group did better than the analytic one.

There are cautions. The children were not assigned by lot, so other differences may have played a part. How much the study should have shaped policy has also been debated. Still, it was influential, and it is often cited. Its results are strongest for decoding words, and weakest for understanding what they say.

# Voting and arguing in a lecture
@src Crouch & Mazur, American Journal of Physics, 2001

Eric Mazur taught introductory physics at Harvard. He found that many students could solve the equations in an exam, but could not answer simple questions about the ideas. He changed his lectures. After a short talk, he would put a conceptual question to the room, and students would vote on an answer. Then they would turn to a neighbor, argue for their choice, and vote again.

He called it peer instruction, and the questions ConcepTests. In 2001 Catherine Crouch and Mazur reported on ten years of its use in Harvard's courses for nonmajors. They measured understanding with the Force Concept Inventory and the Mechanics Baseline Test, two standard tests of the ideas of mechanics. They found larger gains than those found with traditional lecturing.

A later paper by Fagen, Crouch and Mazur, in 2002, reported that courses at many other schools that used interactive methods also showed gains above those of traditional teaching. Students who explain an idea to a classmate have to put it in words, and find where their thinking fails. It is a way of making a large room into a place where people talk.

# The classroom where everyone held a piece
@series history
@src Aronson, Austin, Texas, 1971; jigsaw.org history

In 1971 the public schools of Austin, Texas, had recently been desegregated. White, Black and Hispanic children, who had always been in separate schools, were now in the same classrooms, and there were fights and tension. A former student of the psychologist Elliot Aronson, who worked in the school district, asked him for help.

Aronson and his team decided that the competitive classroom was making things worse. Children raced to answer the teacher, and put down others who were slow. He designed the jigsaw method. Students were placed in groups of about six, and each was given one part of the lesson. Each student also met with others who had the same part, and learned it, then went back to teach the group. No one could succeed without the others.

A 1977 study of ten fifth-grade classes in seven schools reported that children in jigsaw classes were absent less often than others, and showed greater improvement in their schoolwork. The method rests on an idea that Gordon Allport suggested earlier: groups get along better when they share a goal and need each other. It is still used in classrooms today.

# Teaching a student to ask the questions
@src Palincsar & Brown, Cognition and Instruction, 1984

Some children can read every word of a passage and still not understand it. In 1984 Annemarie Palincsar and Ann Brown worked with seventh graders who were good at decoding but poor at understanding. They taught them four activities that good readers use without thinking: summarizing, asking questions, clarifying what is confusing, and predicting what comes next.

The method was called reciprocal teaching. An adult first led a discussion of a short text, using the four activities. Then the students took turns being the teacher, with the adult helping. In the first study, the experimenters ran it. It produced larger gains than a comparison method that was like normal classroom practice, and the gains lasted longer. They carried over to new tasks too.

In the second study, volunteer teachers used it with their own reading groups, and many of the results were repeated. The quality of the students' summaries and questions improved. The approach shows that comprehension can be taught, by making the hidden habits of a good reader into things that can be said and practiced aloud.

# The test that was meant to find who needed help
@series history
@src Binet & Simon, 1905 and 1908; Classics in the History of Psychology

In 1904 the French government set up a commission to look at how to teach children who were failing in school. It needed a way to find the children who would do better in special classes, before teachers' guesses and prejudices decided it. The psychologist Alfred Binet, with a doctor, Theodore Simon, was asked to find one.

Their scale of 1905 had 30 tasks, arranged from easy to hard, for children from infancy to about eleven. A revision in 1908 had 58 items, for ages three to thirteen. Binet described a child's result as a level of development, and he wrote that it classified children, and did not measure a fixed quantity. His main goal was to separate children who needed extra help from the rest of the school population.

Binet warned against taking the scores as a permanent label, and he believed that teaching could raise them. When the test was taken up in other countries, and revised, it came to be used in ways he had not intended, including the ranking of people by a single number. The origin was a practical question about how to help children who were struggling.

# The man whose memory had no limit
@series mind
@conf mixed
@src Luria, The Mind of a Mnemonist, 1968; Wikipedia summary

In the 1920s a Moscow newspaper reporter named Solomon Shereshevsky was sent by his editor to see the psychologist Alexander Luria, because he never took notes. Luria tested him, and found that he could remember long lists of numbers and words, apparently without any limit. Luria studied him for about thirty years and described him as S. in a 1968 book, The Mind of a Mnemonist.

Luria linked the memory to synesthesia, the mixing of senses. Sounds, words and numbers produced colors, tastes and images for S. The pictures were the basis of his recall, and he could place them along streets, as in the method of loci. Luria wrote that the capacity of his memory had no distinct limits.

The account has been questioned. Luria did not clearly separate S.'s natural gifts from the memory techniques he used, and a diary that was found later suggests that he did not in fact have perfect recall. The case is among the most famous in psychology, and perhaps shows how far both talent and method can go.

# The reporter who won a memory contest
@src Foer, Moonwalking with Einstein, 2011; NPR and PBS reports

In 2005 the science writer Joshua Foer covered the USA Memory Championship for Slate, and was struck that the contestants said they had ordinary memories. A man there offered to train him. Foer spent about a year learning the methods under a British memory grandmaster, Ed Cooke, and entered the 2006 championship himself. To the surprise of many, including himself, he won.

The core of the method was the old memory palace. He would picture a familiar place, and put vivid, odd images of the things to be remembered in different spots. He set a United States record in the speed cards event that year, memorizing a deck of 52 cards in 1 minute and 40 seconds. In his 2011 book, Moonwalking with Einstein, he described what he had learned.

His story is evidence that a lot of what looks like a gift is trained. Contest memorizers practice for hours, and the skill applies to lists and cards and not to everything. It does not turn a person into someone who remembers everything. But it shows what a well-practiced technique can do for a person who thought that he was average.

# The seven ways memory goes wrong
@series mind
@src Schacter, The Seven Sins of Memory, 2001

In 2001 the Harvard psychologist Daniel Schacter published a book that sorted the everyday failures of memory into seven kinds, which he called sins. Three are errors of omission, in which something is not remembered. Transience is the fading of memories over time. Absent-mindedness is a lapse in attention, such as putting down keys while thinking about something else. Blocking is failing to bring up something that you know, as with a name on the tip of the tongue.

Four are errors of commission, in which memory is present but wrong or unwanted. Misattribution is remembering something correctly but linking it to the wrong source. Suggestibility is taking in a false detail from a question or a comment. Bias is the way that present beliefs color what we recall. Persistence is the return of memories we would rather forget, as with a painful event.

Schacter argued that these errors are the cost of a system that works well most of the time. Forgetting what is no longer useful, for example, helps us deal with the present. A memory that kept everything would be a different kind of problem. He updated the framework in 2021.
