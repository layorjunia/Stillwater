# The Chinese room
@src Searle, Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 1980

Imagine a man in a closed room who doesn't know any Chinese. He has a big book of rules, written in English, that tells him which Chinese symbols to send out in reply to the symbols that are passed in through a slot. People outside, who read Chinese, find that the answers make sense. They think that the person in the room understands Chinese. But he doesn't. He is only following rules.

The philosopher John Searle put this forward in 1980 as an argument against the idea that a computer running a program could understand anything. A program is about manipulating symbols according to rules, which is syntax. Understanding involves meaning, which is something else. Many replies have been offered, for example that the whole system, the room together with the man and the book, understands. The argument is still debated, and is raised again whenever a program holds a convincing conversation.

# Mary's room
@src Jackson, Philosophical Quarterly, 1982

Mary is a scientist who has spent her whole life in a black-and-white room. She has never seen color. But she knows everything that could be known about the physics and biology of color vision, including how light of different wavelengths affects the eye and brain. One day, she leaves the room and sees a red tomato. Does she learn something new?

The philosopher Frank Jackson argued in 1982 that she does. She learns what red looks like. If so, there are facts about experience that are not captured by the physical facts, and physicalism, the view that everything is physical, is incomplete. Others reply that she gains an ability, not a fact, or that she learns an old fact in a new way. Jackson himself later changed his mind. The thought experiment remains a standard way to bring out the puzzle of consciousness.

# What is it like to be a bat?
@src Nagel, Philosophical Review, 1974

Bats find their way in the dark by echolocation. They experience the world through sound, in a way that is very unlike ours. We could study their brains in the greatest detail. In 1974 the philosopher Thomas Nagel asked whether that would ever tell us what it is like to be a bat. He argued that a creature has conscious experience if there is something that it is like to be that creature, from its own point of view.

Imagining that we hang upside down and eat insects doesn't help. We would only be imagining what it would be like for us to behave like a bat. Nagel's point was that a theory of the mind that leaves out the subjective view is incomplete. The essay is one of the most cited in the philosophy of mind, and it set the agenda for debates about consciousness in animals and machines.

# Philosophical zombies
@src Chalmers, The Conscious Mind, 1996

Imagine a being that looks and acts just like you, with the same brain, the same behavior and the same words. It says that it is in pain when it's hurt, and that it loves the taste of chocolate. But inside, there is nothing. It has no experience at all. Philosophers call this a zombie, but not the kind in films.

David Chalmers used the idea in the 1990s to argue that consciousness is more than physical processes. If a zombie is even conceivable, he said, then the physical facts don't settle whether there is experience. He called the question of why there is any experience at all the hard problem. Critics reply that a zombie is not really conceivable, or that conceiving something doesn't show that it's possible. The problem is still unsettled.

# The experience machine
@src Nozick, Anarchy, State, and Utopia, 1974

Suppose that you could plug into a machine that gave you any experiences that you liked. You would think that you were writing a great novel, or making friends, or falling in love, and it would all seem completely real. Your body would float in a tank, with electrodes in your brain. Would you plug in for life?

Robert Nozick asked this in 1974, and he thought that most people would say no. He argued that we care about more than how things feel. We want to actually do things, and to be a certain kind of person, and to be in touch with reality. If this is right, then pleasure isn't the only thing of value. Studies of what people say when asked suggest that many decline the machine, though the answers change if the question is worded differently.

# The brain in a vat
@src Putnam, Reason, Truth and History, 1981

Suppose that you are a brain in a vat of nutrients, connected to a computer that gives you the experience of a normal life. How could you know that you're not? The idea is a modern version of Descartes's demon, and it's the basis of films such as The Matrix. The philosopher Hilary Putnam gave it a twist in 1981.

He argued that a brain in a vat could not even think the thought that I am a brain in a vat. Its words would refer to things in the computer's simulation, not to real vats. So if you are saying the sentence, and it is true that you are in a vat, then the sentence is false. His argument is clever, and it has been much debated. Some think that it only shows something about language, and does not remove the doubt.

# The teletransporter
@src Parfit, Reasons and Persons, 1984

You step into a machine on Earth. It scans every atom of your body, destroying it in the process, and sends the information to Mars. There a machine builds an exact copy of you from new atoms. The copy remembers your life and has your character. Have you survived the trip, or have you died, and been replaced by a copy?

The philosopher Derek Parfit used this puzzle in 1984 to question our ideas about identity. He suggested that what matters is not whether the same person continues, but the connections of memory and character. If the copy has these, he thought, it is about as good as survival. Many people find this hard to accept. Parfit also said that it freed him from a fear of death, since his own life was just a part of a stream of experiences.

# The drowning child
@src Singer, Philosophy & Public Affairs, 1972

You are walking past a shallow pond and see a child drowning. You can wade in and save the child, but your clothes will be ruined. Should you do it? Almost everyone says yes. The cost is small, and the life is at stake. In 1972 the philosopher Peter Singer used this case to argue for a very demanding conclusion.

He said that distance doesn't change the moral situation. A child dying of poverty far away is as much in need as the one in the pond. If we can prevent something very bad, at little cost to ourselves, we ought to do so. Most of us could give much more than we do to charity. The argument has been highly influential, and gave rise to the effective altruism movement. It has also been criticized as too demanding.

# Bentham, Mill and the greatest happiness
@src Bentham, 1789; Mill, Utilitarianism, 1861

Jeremy Bentham, an English philosopher who lived from 1748 to 1832, argued that the right action is the one that produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. He proposed to count pleasure and pain as the measure of value. For him, a game of pushpin was as good as poetry, if it gave as much pleasure.

His follower John Stuart Mill refined the idea in 1861. He said that some pleasures are higher than others, and that it is better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. This view is called utilitarianism. It has been very influential in law, economics and public policy. The main objection is that it may allow terrible acts if they make the total happiness larger, which has led to many arguments over rights and fairness.

# Paradigm shifts
@src Kuhn, The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, 1962

How does science change? The usual story was that it grows steadily, as facts accumulate. In 1962 the physicist and historian Thomas Kuhn offered a different picture. In normal science, he said, researchers work within a framework of shared assumptions, which he called a paradigm. They solve puzzles that the framework defines.

Over time, anomalies pile up, results that the paradigm cannot explain. At first, they are ignored. Eventually there is a crisis, and a new paradigm arrives, such as the move from Newton's physics to Einstein's. Scientists then see the world in a new way. Kuhn argued that the two paradigms can't be compared easily, since they use different standards. The phrase paradigm shift, which he made popular, is now used well outside of science.

# What is knowledge?
@src Gettier, Analysis, 1963

For centuries, philosophers said that knowledge is justified true belief. To know something, you have to believe it, it has to be true, and you must have good reasons. In 1963 Edmund Gettier published a three-page paper that raised a problem with this.

Here is a case in the spirit of his. You look at a clock that says two o'clock, and you believe that it is two. You have good reason, because the clock has always been reliable. And it is two. But the clock stopped exactly twelve hours ago. Your belief is justified and true, but you were right by luck. Do you know the time? Most people say no. The paper set off decades of attempts to repair the definition, and no fix has been fully accepted.

# The heap
@src Eubulides of Miletus, 4th century BC

A single grain of sand is not a heap. Two grains are not a heap either. Adding one grain to something that is not a heap never makes a heap. Yet if you keep adding grains one at a time, you will end up with a heap. Where did it start? This puzzle is attributed to a Greek philosopher, Eubulides, in the fourth century BC. It's called the sorites paradox, from the Greek word for heap.

The puzzle applies to any vague word. When does a man become bald as hairs fall out? When does a color shift from red to orange? There is no sharp line, and yet the ends are clearly different. Philosophers have proposed several solutions, including a hidden sharp boundary that we can't know, and logics where truth comes in degrees. The problem matters for the law, where vague terms such as reasonable must be applied to cases.

# This sentence is false
@src Tarski, 1933; Eubulides, 4th century BC

Consider the sentence: this sentence is false. If it's true, then what it says is correct, so it is false. If it's false, then what it says is wrong, so it is true. It seems to be both. This is the liar paradox, which has been known since ancient Greece. It has often been attributed to Eubulides, and also to Epimenides, a Cretan who is said to have remarked that all Cretans are liars.

In the 1930s the logician Alfred Tarski showed that the paradox causes trouble for any language that can talk about its own truth. His solution was to separate levels. A statement about the truth of sentences in a language must be made in a different, higher language. The paradox is close to the ones that Kurt Gödel used in 1931 to prove his incompleteness theorems, in which a sentence says that it cannot be proved.

# Russell's paradox
@src Russell, letter to Frege, 1902

In 1902 the British philosopher Bertrand Russell wrote to the German logician Gottlob Frege, who had just finished the second volume of his life's work, an attempt to base arithmetic on logic. Russell pointed out a flaw. Consider the set of all sets that do not contain themselves. Does this set contain itself? If it does, then it doesn't. If it doesn't, then it does.

Frege replied that arithmetic totters. The paradox showed that the natural idea that any property defines a set leads to a contradiction. Russell and others spent years building new foundations. The standard solution is a set theory with axioms, devised by Ernst Zermelo and others, that restricts how sets can be formed. A popular version of the paradox is the barber who shaves all those, and only those, who do not shave themselves.

# Newcomb's problem
@src Nozick, 1969

A superintelligent being has put two boxes in front of you. Box A is clear and holds a thousand dollars. Box B is opaque, and holds either a million dollars or nothing. You may take both boxes, or only box B. Here is the catch. The being has predicted what you will do. If it predicted that you would take only box B, it put a million dollars in it. If it predicted that you would take both, it left it empty. It has been right many times before.

One argument says to take only B, since people who do so seem to leave with a million. Another says to take both, since the contents are already fixed, and you can't change them by choosing, so you may as well take the extra thousand. William Newcomb devised the problem, and Robert Nozick described it in 1969. Philosophers are split almost evenly on the right answer.

# Moral luck
@src Williams, 1976; Nagel, 1976

Two drivers are each a little drunk, and both drive home. One of them gets there safely. The other, by bad luck, hits a child who runs into the road. Is the second driver more blameworthy than the first? Most of us feel that he is. And yet, what separates them is chance, which neither of them controlled.

In 1976 the philosophers Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel raised this puzzle, which they called moral luck. We usually say that people should be judged only by what is in their control. But in practice, our judgments depend on outcomes, on how people were brought up, and on the circumstances they happened to face. The problem suggests that our idea of responsibility may be less coherent than we think. It's relevant to law, where the same act is punished differently depending on the result.

# The repugnant conclusion
@src Parfit, Reasons and Persons, 1984

Suppose that you want to maximize the total amount of happiness in the world. Consider a world with ten billion people, each with a very good life. Compare it with a world with many more people, whose lives are barely worth living. If the numbers are big enough, the total in the second world can be larger, because many small pieces of happiness add up. So the second might seem better.

Derek Parfit called this the repugnant conclusion, and he found it impossible to accept. Yet it follows from some attractive ideas about how to add up well-being. He spent years trying to find a way out, and never fully succeeded. The problem is central to the study of population ethics, which asks how we should weigh the interests of future people, including questions about the size of the population.

# The Euthyphro dilemma
@src Plato, Euthyphro

In a short dialogue by Plato, Socrates meets a man named Euthyphro near a court in Athens. Euthyphro is going to prosecute his own father for a killing, and is confident that it is the pious thing to do. Socrates asks him what piety is. Euthyphro says that it's what all the gods love. Then Socrates asks: is something pious because the gods love it, or do the gods love it because it is pious?

The question applies to morality in general. If something is good only because a god commands it, then goodness seems arbitrary, as the god could have commanded the opposite. If the gods love it because it is good, then goodness exists independently of the gods, and they are not its source. Philosophers and theologians have argued over this for over two thousand years. The dialogue ends without an answer, as many of Plato's early ones do.

# The paradox of tolerance
@src Popper, The Open Society and Its Enemies, 1945

Should a tolerant society tolerate intolerance? In 1945 the philosopher Karl Popper wrote a note on the question, which he called the paradox of tolerance. He argued that unlimited tolerance leads to the disappearance of tolerance. If a society is tolerant without limit toward those who are intolerant, and who would destroy it, the tolerant will be destroyed.

His conclusion was that a society has a right to protect itself. He said that we should claim the right to suppress intolerant philosophies if they won't engage in rational argument, and respond with force. But he added that, as long as such views can be countered by argument, they should be allowed. People still disagree about where to draw the line. It was written just after the Second World War, and shaped by the rise of Nazism.

# Meaning is use
@src Wittgenstein, Philosophical Investigations, 1953

Ludwig Wittgenstein was an Austrian philosopher who lived from 1889 to 1951. He thought that he had solved the main problems of philosophy in his first book. Later, he changed his mind. His second book, the Philosophical Investigations, was published after his death in 1953, and it argued against his own early view.

He proposed that the meaning of a word is its use in a language, and not an object that it stands for. Think of the word game. There is no one feature shared by all games, only family resemblances, as in a family where no one trait is common to all. Language is a set of language games, tied to activities. He also wrote that if a lion could speak, we could not understand him, because our forms of life would be so different.
