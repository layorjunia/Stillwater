# The what-the-hell effect
@src Herman & Mack, Journal of Personality, 1975

In 1975 Peter Herman and Deborah Mack ran an odd experiment on dieters. They told people that they were testing taste, and gave some of them a milkshake or two, and others none. Then everyone was asked to rate several flavors of ice cream, and could eat as much as they wanted. The ice cream was weighed afterward.

People who weren't dieting ate less ice cream after the milkshakes, as you'd expect from a full stomach. Dieters ate more. Having broken their diet, they seemed to decide that the day was spoiled, and let go. The effect has been called what-the-hell. It applies to habits of all kinds. One slip can turn into a binge, because the slip feels like failure. The cure is to treat a slip as one meal, not as the end of the plan.

# Optimism and a longer life
@src Lee et al., PNAS, 2019

In 2019 Lewina Lee and her colleagues looked at two large studies of older adults. One followed nearly 70,000 women, and the other about 1,400 men, for years. Everyone had answered a questionnaire about how optimistic they were. The researchers asked whether optimism went along with how long people lived.

The most optimistic people lived about eleven to fifteen percent longer, on average, and had much better odds of living to eighty-five. The result held after the researchers adjusted for things like age, education, health conditions and depression. It's an observational study, so it can't prove that optimism causes longer life. Optimists may exercise more or cope better with stress. But the link is consistent and fairly large, and it fits other work on mindset and health.

# A reason to get up
@src Hill & Turiano, Psychological Science, 2014

Having a purpose in life means feeling that your life has direction and that your activities matter. In 2014 Patrick Hill and Nicholas Turiano used data from a large American study that followed about six thousand adults for fourteen years. At the start, each person answered questions about their sense of purpose.

People with a stronger sense of purpose were less likely to die during the follow-up. The link held for young, middle-aged and older adults, and after the researchers took account of other things that matter for health, such as how positive people felt and the quality of their relationships. It's a correlation, and it's possible that illness reduces purpose as well as the other way round. But it suggests that a sense of meaning is more than a nice feeling.

# Chasing happiness
@src Mauss, Tamir, Anderson & Savino, Emotion, 2011
@conf mixed

It seems sensible to want to be happy, and to try. In 2011 Iris Mauss and her colleagues asked what happens when people place a very high value on happiness. In one study, people who valued happiness more reported less of it in their own lives, and more symptoms of depression. In another, people who were told to read a newspaper article extolling the importance of happiness felt worse after watching a happy film than people who read a neutral article.

The explanation they offered is that if you set a high standard for how happy you should be, you're likely to be disappointed, and the disappointment makes you less happy. The findings were not the same in every situation, and some later work gives a more mixed picture. But the idea is a useful one. It may be better to focus on doing things that matter, and let the feeling come.

# Keeping score
@src Harkin et al., Psychological Bulletin, 2016

If you want to reach a goal, does it help to track your progress? In 2016 Benjamin Harkin and his colleagues combined 138 experiments, with almost twenty thousand people in total. In each study, some people were prompted to monitor their progress toward a goal, such as eating better or saving money, and others were not.

Monitoring helped, with a modest but reliable benefit. It helped more when people wrote their progress down, rather than keeping it in their heads, and when they made it public, for example by reporting it to others. It matters, too, what you monitor. Tracking your behavior mainly changes your behavior. If you want to change an outcome, such as your weight, it's best to track that. It's a simple reason to keep a log.

# Kindness is good for the giver
@src Curry et al., Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 2018

We usually think of acts of kindness as good for the receiver. In 2018 Oliver Curry and his colleagues asked whether they are good for the giver as well. They combined the results of twenty-seven experiments, involving about four thousand people, in which some people were asked to do kind things and others were not, and well-being was measured afterward.

Those who performed acts of kindness reported a small to medium increase in well-being. The effect did not seem to depend on age, sex or the kind of kindness, and the researchers found no sign that unpublished studies were missing. It isn't large, and the benefit is probably a bit bigger when the act is of real help, and is done by choice. But it's cheap, and it's one of the more reliable ways of feeling a little better.

# Procrastination's bill arrives late
@src Tice & Baumeister, Psychological Science, 1997

Putting things off is a form of relief, at least at first. In 1997 Dianne Tice and Roy Baumeister followed college students through a semester. Early in the term, those who tended to procrastinate on a term paper reported less stress and fewer health problems than the others. They were enjoying their free time.

But by the end of the term, the picture had reversed. The procrastinators reported more stress and more illness, and they turned in worse work. Overall they came out behind. It's a clear pattern. The costs of delay are real, but they come later, and that's why delay is tempting. When you feel the urge to put off a task, it may help to remember that the relief is a loan.

# Meeting your future self
@src Hershfield et al., Journal of Marketing Research, 2011
@conf mixed

Why is it hard to save for old age? One idea is that we think of our future self as a stranger. In 2011 Hal Hershfield and his colleagues tested a way to change that. People used a computer to create a digital avatar of themselves, and some were shown a version aged to what they would look like at retirement. Then they were asked how they would spend a sum of money.

The people who had met their older selves put more of the money into a retirement account than those who saw only their present selves. The study was small and the effect was on a pretend choice. Later work has found that the effect is not always repeated, so it should be treated with care. But the idea is worth keeping. It's easier to take care of someone you can picture.

# Introverts and acting outgoing
@src Zelenski, Santoro & Whelan, Emotion, 2012; Zelenski et al., JPSP, 2013
@conf mixed

Most introverts would rather not act like extraverts. In 2012 John Zelenski and his colleagues asked people to take part in a discussion, and told some of them to act in an assertive, talkative way. Others were asked to act in a quiet, reserved way. Afterward, people rated how they felt.

Both introverts and extraverts felt more positive after acting extraverted, and the introverts had not expected it. They underestimated how good it would feel. There seem to be some costs, since acting against your nature can be tiring, and not all studies agree. But it suggests that when you hesitate to join in, your forecast of the experience may be too gloomy. A short try may be worth it.

# Dennis the dentist
@src Pelham, Mirenberg & Jones, JPSP, 2002; Simonsohn, 2011
@conf contested

In 2002 Brett Pelham and his colleagues reported that people are drawn to things that resemble their own names. Using public records, they found that there were more dentists named Dennis or Denise than would be expected by chance, and more people named Louis living in St. Louis. They called it implicit egotism. We like what looks like us.

The finding was striking and widely cited. In 2011 Uri Simonsohn looked again, and found that the patterns could come from other causes. Names tend to be common in certain families, ethnic groups and regions, which are also linked to jobs and places. The debate hasn't been closed. There may be a small preference for the familiar. But the dramatic version, that your name steers your life, has not held up well.

# Born in January
@src Barnsley & Thompson, 1988; Cobley et al., Sports Medicine, 2009

In the 1980s the psychologist Roger Barnsley and his colleagues noticed something in the hockey results. Among Canadian players in the top junior leagues, many more had been born in the first months of the year than in the last. Those born in January, February and March were the most common, and those born in October through December were the rarest.

The reason is the age cut-off for youth leagues. Children are grouped by calendar year, so a boy born in January is almost a year older than one born in December, and at ten that makes a big difference in size and skill. The older boys are picked for the better teams, get more coaching, and improve more. The relative age effect has since been seen in many sports, and in schools. A small head start, at the right time, can be amplified.

# Give them what they asked for
@src Flynn & Adams, JESP, 2009; Gino & Flynn, JESP, 2011
@series sell

Most of us would like to give a gift that surprises someone. In 2009 Francis Flynn and Gabrielle Adams found that gift givers tend to think that a more expensive gift will be more appreciated. Recipients didn't care about the price as much as the giver thought. The giver's expectation and the receiver's feelings didn't line up.

In 2011 Francesca Gino and Francis Flynn compared gifts that had been asked for with gifts chosen by the giver. People who got what they had requested said they liked them more and felt more appreciative than people who got unrequested gifts. The givers hadn't expected this. They had thought that asking for what you want is a bit cold. The lesson is that practical gifts are not a failure of imagination. Often the best gift is the one that is wanted.

# The happiness of looking forward
@src Nawijn et al., Applied Research in Quality of Life, 2010

Does a vacation make people happier? In 2010 Jeroen Nawijn and his colleagues asked Dutch adults about their happiness before, during and after trips. People who were about to go on a vacation were happier than people who weren't planning one, and the lift was seen before they left.

After they came back, the extra happiness was gone. Those who had a relaxing trip felt a bit better for a short while, but the gain didn't last. The researchers suggested that much of the pleasure is in the anticipation. You can use this. Planning something good, and giving yourself time to look forward to it, can add to your daily mood, even before it happens.
