# The fan that was said to kill sleepers
@series history
@src Fan death belief in South Korea

In South Korea there has long been a belief in fan death, the idea that if you sleep in a closed room with an electric fan running, you will die. The belief has been strongest among older people, and it has been reported in the Korean news for decades, when someone is found dead in a room with a fan on. Explanations given include that the fan uses up the oxygen, cools the body too much, or blows away the air that a sleeper needs.

None of these is true. A fan does not use up oxygen, and it does not make a room airless. Deaths of people found with a fan are explained by other causes, such as heat, a heart problem or another illness, with the fan simply being in the room.

The belief has been fading. By 2019 it was in decline. It is a good example of a folk idea that has an air of plausibility and is passed from one generation to the next. The lesson is to look for the evidence, and to ask how many people have slept happily with fans running without any harm.

# Three bits of first-aid folklore
@series sci
@src First-aid guidance; drowning research

Many people think that it is dangerous to wake a sleepwalker. It is not. A sleepwalker who is woken may be confused or disoriented for a short while, but waking does no harm. The risks of sleepwalking come from injury, such as falling down stairs, and from lost sleep. The advice is to guide a sleepwalker gently back to bed.

Another belief is that a person having a seizure can swallow their tongue. That cannot happen, and it is dangerous to try to put anything into the mouth of a person who is convulsing. The recommended action is to lay the person gently on one side, so that the risk of choking is lessened, and to wait until it passes.

The third concerns drowning. Films show a drowning person waving and yelling. In real life drowning is often quiet, and onlookers do not notice. The instinctive drowning response keeps the person from calling out or waving. The arms press on the water to keep the head up, and there is no spare breath for a shout. Most victims show no sign of distress that an onlooker would recognize, so the lesson is to watch children near water closely.

# What the movies get wrong about emergencies
@series sci
@src Resuscitation guidelines; the Armstrong limit; cold shock research

In films, a doctor shocks a patient whose heart monitor shows a flat line. It does not work. A flat line, known as asystole, is not a rhythm that responds to a defibrillator. The treatment is high-quality chest compressions and, in a hospital, a drug, epinephrine. Defibrillation is for certain disorders of the heartbeat, but not for a flat line.

Another scene is a person exposed to the vacuum of space, whose body explodes or whose blood boils. It does not happen. In all but the most extreme cases of sudden loss of pressure, the person loses consciousness after the oxygen in the blood is used up, and dies of oxygen starvation within minutes. At altitudes above a level called the Armstrong limit, fluids in the mouth and lungs do boil, but not the body as a whole.

A third is falling into icy water. People think that the cold kills by hypothermia, but it usually takes much longer. The danger in the first minutes is the cold shock response, which can cause a gasp, rapid breathing and a heart attack. The advice that follows is to stay calm and float until the first reaction passes.

# Why the veins look blue
@series sci
@src Optics of skin; medical illustration conventions

Blood in the veins is often said to be blue until it meets the air. It is not. Human blood is always red. The red comes from hemoglobin, a protein that carries oxygen. Blood that has given up its oxygen, in the veins, is a deep dark red. Blood that is rich in oxygen, in the arteries, is a bright cherry red.

The veins under your skin look blue or greenish because of the way light passes through skin. Red light is absorbed more deeply, and blue light is scattered back to the eye, and the brain's way of judging color adds to the effect. Skin changes what you see.

Another reason for the belief is the way that medical diagrams are drawn. By custom, veins are colored blue and arteries red, so that they can be told apart. The picture then comes to seem like the truth. When blood is drawn, the sample in the tube is red. If a person is cut, the blood is red. The idea of blue blood is an illusion of light and of our drawings.

# Three numbers about the body that were wrong
@series sci
@src Wunderlich, 1860s study; Sender, Fuchs & Milo, 2016; Krogh, 1929

The normal body temperature is 37 degrees Celsius, or 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit. That figure comes from a study in 1860. Modern research finds that the average is lower, about 36.4 degrees, or 97.5 degrees Fahrenheit, with small variations through the day and between people.

Another number is that your body has ten times more bacterial cells than human cells. The ratio was an estimate made in 1972. More recent estimates put the ratio at about three to one, and others at about 1.3 to one, so the two are close to equal. The microbes are very small, and they weigh only a small fraction of the body's weight.

A third is that if the capillaries of the body were laid out, they would be 100,000 kilometers long. This comes from a 1929 book by the Danish physiologist August Krogh. He used a model of a person who was larger than real, and a density of capillaries that was too high. The best estimate now is between 9,000 and 19,000 kilometers. Each of the three figures was repeated for decades, because they were in textbooks and nobody checked them.

# Heat, ribs and ears
@series sci
@src Thermal physiology; anatomy; otolaryngology guidance

The advice to wear a hat because you lose half your body heat through your head is not true. Heat is lost in proportion to the amount of skin that is exposed. The head is about seven to nine percent of the body's surface, so it loses about that proportion. Studies of people with the head in cold water showed that they lost only about ten percent more heat overall.

Another belief is that women have one more rib than men, or one fewer. Men and women both have 24 ribs, or 12 pairs. The idea probably comes from the story of Adam and Eve, in which Eve is made from a rib of Adam.

A third is about cleaning the ears. Cotton swabs, the kind sold as Q-tips, are widely used for it. But there is no medical benefit in putting them into the ear canal, and there are risks, such as pushing wax further in, scratching the canal and damaging the eardrum. The ear is self-cleaning, in that wax slowly moves outward. The three ideas are all easy to believe, and none stands up.

# The remains that are not ashes
@series sci
@src Cremation practice; the cremulator

When a person is cremated, the family receives what is called ashes. They are not ashes in the sense of what is left after wood burns. After the body has been through the fire, what remains are dry fragments of bone. They are swept out of the chamber where the cremation takes place, which is called a retort.

The fragments are then put into a machine, called a cremulator, which grinds them into a fine, pale powder. It is, in essence, a high-capacity, high-speed blender. The result is what is given back as cremated remains. The remains of an adult usually weigh several pounds.

The word ashes is used out of habit, and out of a wish to avoid harder words. What we call ashes is really the mineral part of the skeleton, which fire does not destroy, and which has been ground fine. The softer parts of the body are burned away. The fact is not secret, but few people hear it until they have to arrange a cremation, and they are often surprised.

# The made-up drug that shares a real chemical's name
@series history
@src Hunter S. Thompson, Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas, 1971; adrenochrome chemistry

Adrenochrome is a real chemical. It is made when adrenaline, the hormone of the fight or flight response, oxidizes, and it has been known to chemists for a long time. It has no use as a recreational drug, and it is not harvested from living people.

The rumors have a literary source. In his 1971 book, Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas, the writer Hunter S. Thompson described a fictional drug that he called adrenochrome, which was taken from the adrenal glands of a living person and gave an extreme high. He seemed not to know that a real substance had that name. The substance in the book was made up, and it has nothing to do with the real chemical.

In recent years the invented drug became a part of conspiracy theories that claim that powerful people harvest it from trafficked children. There is no evidence for such claims. It is a clear example of how a work of fiction can become a rumor, as people forget where an idea came from. What circulates is a novelist's invention, mixed up with a real compound.

# What does and does not help a cold
@series sci
@src Cochrane reviews of vitamin C and echinacea; infectious disease guidance

The common cold is caused by viruses, and not by being cold. Low temperatures do not make you ill by themselves. But cold may weaken the body's defenses a little, and a person who is infected but without symptoms may start to feel ill after being chilled. Colds are more common in winter, and the reasons include dry air, less ventilation in homes, more time spent indoors, and lower vitamin D levels.

Antibiotics do not cure a cold, because they work on bacteria and not on viruses. The color of what you blow from your nose, which may be clear, yellow or green, does not show whether the cause is a virus or a bacterium. It depends on immune cells fighting the infection.

Vitamin C does not prevent colds or treat them, although taken daily it may shorten them slightly. It has no effect if it is taken after the cold has started. A 2014 review of 24 randomized trials found that echinacea supplements do not prevent colds, and most trials showed no benefit over a placebo. Even the temperature of the water makes no difference when washing your hands. With soap, cold and warm water remove the same amount of germs.

# Warts, knuckles and a dry skin
@series sci
@src Dermatology and rheumatology guidance

Many children are told that touching a toad gives you warts. It does not. The bumps on a toad are not warts, and warts on human skin are caused by a virus, the human papillomavirus, which infects only humans. A person can catch it from another person, or from a surface, but not from an animal.

Another warning is that cracking your knuckles gives you arthritis. Studies have not found that it causes osteoarthritis. The popping sound comes from gas bubbles in the fluid of the joint. It may annoy others, but it does not seem to harm the joints.

A third idea is about people with eczema. It is thought that bathing dries out the skin and should be avoided. But bathing does not dry out the skin if a moisturizer is applied soon afterward. If it is not, the water that evaporates from the skin can leave it dry. The three examples show how old advice can be reversed by a closer look, and how it can be wrong in a way that is both harmless and widely repeated.

# Rumors about tabs, horns and warning labels
@series sci
@src National Kidney Foundation statement; traditional Chinese medicine; package inserts

Since at least the 1970s a rumor has gone around that collecting the pull tabs from cans of drink will help to pay for dialysis machines for people with kidney disease. It is not true. There has never been such a program in the United States. The National Kidney Foundation, which is usually named in the rumor, has denied it. Dialysis is mainly paid for through Medicare.

Another belief is that powdered rhinoceros horn is used in traditional Chinese medicine as an aphrodisiac. It is not. It has been prescribed for fevers and convulsions, a use that evidence-based medicine does not support. The trade in horn has driven the killing of rhinos, so the myth about its use has real consequences.

A third is about the lists of side effects on the papers in a medicine's box. The frequency stated is how often the effect occurs after taking the drug, and it is not a claim that the drug caused it. What is reported is a correlation, which may or may not be a cause. Some people who take a sugar pill also report headaches. Reading them correctly takes care.

# What leprosy is, and is not
@series history
@src Hansen's disease; Leviticus and tzaraath

Leprosy, now often called Hansen's disease, has a fearful reputation. It is widely thought to make fingers and toes rot and fall off on their own. That is not so. The disease causes rashes and may damage cartilage and nerves. Because nerves are damaged, a person may not feel pain, and so injuries and infections that go untreated can destroy tissue. The disease itself does not cause body parts to drop off.

It is also only mildly contagious. Of the people who are infected with the bacteria that cause it, about 95 percent never develop the disease. It is curable today with antibiotics.

Much of the terror of the word comes from the Bible. A disease of the skin called tzaraath, which makes a person unclean and sends him out of the community, is often identified with leprosy, and may be the source of many myths about it. The Hebrew term may have covered several conditions. For centuries people with leprosy were made outcasts. The reality of the illness is less terrible than the story, and the greater harm was often the stigma.

# Three medical legends: rust, rabies and the bubble boy
@series history
@src Tetanus microbiology; rabies post-exposure prophylaxis; SCID treatment; David Vetter

Rust does not cause tetanus. The bacterium that causes it lives in soil, and it thrives where there is little oxygen. A rusty nail has become a symbol, because rust is a place where oxygen has combined with iron. But any puncture wound can bring in the spores of the bacterium, and it is why a tetanus shot is advised after a deep wound from any object.

Another legend is about rabies shots. People dread a long needle in the stomach. Before the 1980s, the vaccine for rabies did have to be given in the abdomen, but modern vaccines are injected into the muscle of the upper arm, as with other shots.

The third is the bubble boy. People with severe combined immunodeficiency, in which the immune system fails, have been shown in films as living in a sterile bubble. Quarantine was never the standard treatment. The standard is a bone marrow transplant in the first months of life. The case that inspired the idea was David Vetter, who spent most of his life in a sterile enclosure, and did not receive a transplant until the age of 12. He had been born at a time when treatment was less advanced.

# Eyes, light and the colors we miss
@series sci
@src Ophthalmology guidance; color vision research

Reading in dim light does not harm the eyes permanently. It is tiring, and it can cause eye strain, with sore eyes and headaches. But it does not damage the eye, and the strain goes away when you rest. Children who read under the covers with a flashlight need not worry about going blind.

A second belief is that glasses can cure color blindness. They cannot. Color blindness, which is most often inherited, results from a difference in the cone cells of the retina, and it cannot be significantly relieved by glasses or lenses. Some lenses are marketed to the colorblind, but professionals doubt that they work, and they do not give a person the colors they cannot see.

A third concerns food. There is a broad scientific consensus that the foods from genetically modified crops now on sale pose no greater risk to human health than ordinary foods. This is a matter on which many bodies have given their opinion. Whether other issues about such crops, such as their effects on farming and the environment, are of concern is a separate question, and still discussed.

# Natural is not the same as safe
@series sci
@src Toxicology; herbal medicine safety; air pollution research

Many people think that something natural must be harmless. Wood smoke, for example, is not a benign sort of pollution because it comes from trees. It is as harmful as other common forms of air pollution, such as diesel fumes. The fine particles in it can get deep into the lungs, and a wood stove in a neighborhood can add to the risk.

The same is true of herbal medicines. They are not necessarily safe or free of side effects. Plants contain powerful chemicals, and some can cause serious harm or react with other medicines. Because they are often sold as supplements, they may not have been tested as carefully as drugs.

And not only heavy metals are toxic. Others, such as beryllium and lithium, can be too. Lithium is used as a medicine, but at a high dose it is dangerous, and doctors check the blood levels of those who take it. Natural origin says nothing about safety. A chemical is what it is, whether it is made by a plant or in a factory, and the dose often makes the difference.

# Tin foil is not made of tin
@series words
@src History of packaging materials

Many people call the thin silver sheets used in kitchens tin foil. It is not made of tin. Aluminum foil replaced tin foil in almost all uses during the twentieth century, though the old name stuck. Tin was expensive, and aluminum is lighter, cheaper and does not impart a taste to food.

The word tin can is also a leftover. Tin cans are not mainly tin now. They are made mostly of steel, with a thin coating, or of aluminum. Tin was used as a protective coat on steel, because it resists rust and is safe with food, and that gave them the name. The tin is now a small fraction.

The same thing happened to the word lead in a pencil. Pencils have never contained the element lead. The core is graphite, which in the past was called black lead. Words keep old meanings after the things have changed. When we talk about tin foil, tin cans and pencil lead, we use names that record how things used to be made.

# The deep web is not the dark web
@series tech
@src Terminology of the internet

People sometimes use the terms deep web and dark web as if they mean the same thing. They do not. The deep web is simply everything on the internet that ordinary search engines do not index. It includes your private email, your messages, your bank account pages, online databases, and academic libraries. Most of it is quite ordinary.

The dark web is a small part of the deep web. It is made up of sites that can only be reached with special software, and that are built to hide the identity of users and operators. Some of those sites do host illegal markets, but others serve people who want privacy, such as journalists and people living under repressive governments.

So the idea that the deep web is mainly pornography, illegal drugs and stolen bank details is not true. That kind of material is found in a small portion of it. The common picture of an iceberg, with the web above the water and the dark depths below, gives the wrong idea of proportion. Most of what lies beneath the surface is no more sinister than a locked filing cabinet.

# Three things in the sky that are not what they seem
@series sci
@src Meteorology; power plant engineering; atmospheric science

The large clouds that rise from the tall, curved towers of power stations are often thought to be smoke or poison. Those towers are cooling towers, and the clouds are water vapor. They give off no smoke or harmful fumes, and no radiation. The vapor does not add to climate change. The tall towers that stand beside nuclear plants are the same.

On a warm summer night, you may see flashes in the sky with no thunder. People call this heat lightning, as if the heat made it. There is no such thing as a separate kind of lightning. What people are seeing is ordinary lightning from storms that are too far away for the thunder to be heard. The flash can be seen across a much greater distance than the rumble can be heard.

The white lines that stay behind airplanes are called contrails. They are made of water vapor and engine exhaust that has frozen into ice crystals in the cold upper air. They are not chemtrails, a name that is given by those who believe they are part of a plot to control the weather or the people below. They are plain condensed water, and whether they last depends on the humidity of the air.

# Two things in the atlas that people get wrong
@series sci
@src Geography; hydrology

Ask people where the southern tip of Africa is, and many will say the Cape of Good Hope. It is not. The most southerly point of the continent is Cape Agulhas, which lies about 150 kilometers to the east-southeast. The Cape of Good Hope is more famous because of the history of sailing, when ships on the way to Asia had to go around it. The two capes are often mixed up.

Another idea is that rivers flow from north to south, because maps have north at the top and water goes down. Water flows downhill, and the direction depends on the land. Many of the great rivers flow to the north. They include the Nile, the Yenisey, the Ob, the Rhine, the Lena and the Orinoco.

The feeling that north is up and that up means uphill is an effect of how maps are drawn. In fact a map could just as well have south at the top, and some have been made like that. The world has no natural up. It is a reminder that a convention can shape our sense of what is true, when it is a choice made by the mapmaker.

# Is there a fixed amount of work to be done?
@series think
@src Economics; the lump of labor fallacy

One idea about jobs is that there is a fixed amount of work to be done in the economy. If a machine does some of it, there is less left for people, and unemployment goes up. Another version is that if some people work shorter hours, there will be work for others. Economists call this the lump of labor fallacy.

The idea has a long history. It was behind the Luddites, English workers who in the early 1800s smashed the machines that they feared would take their jobs. It is still heard in debates about automation and about rules that limit working time. The error is in the assumption that the quantity of work is fixed. In fact the amount of labor needed to produce a given output can change, because of new uses of capital, better efficiency and learning.

When production gets cheaper, people may buy more, or buy new things, and that makes new work. This does not mean that technology never causes hardship. Particular workers and places can lose out, and the change can be painful. What it means is that the total is not a fixed lump, and that the effects of change cannot be read from a simple count of jobs that disappear.
