# The low-ball
@src Cialdini, Cacioppo, Bassett & Miller, JPSP, 1978

In 1978 Robert Cialdini and his colleagues asked students to join a psychology experiment. For some of them, the researcher first got a yes, and only then mentioned that the session started at seven in the morning. For others, the early start was stated in the first request. Roughly half of the students who were asked the first way agreed, compared with about a quarter to a third who were told up front.

Most of the people who had said yes before they knew the cost showed up, even though they were free to back out. The first yes created a commitment, and the later cost didn't undo it. Sellers sometimes advertise a very low price, then add fees. A later meta-analysis found that the technique works across many studies. A good defense is to ask for the full cost before you say yes.

# The odometer cliff
@src Lacetera, Pope & Sydnor, American Economic Review, 2012

Imagine two used cars. One has 79,999 miles on the odometer, and the other has 80,001. They are nearly the same car. In 2012 Nicola Lacetera, Devin Pope and Justin Sydnor looked at more than twenty million wholesale used-car sales and found that prices drop sharply at each ten thousand mile mark. The drop was around one or two hundred dollars, just from crossing the line.

The reason seems to be that people focus on the leftmost digit of a number and pay less attention to the rest. A mileage of 79,999 reads as seventy something, and 80,001 as eighty. Sellers seemed to know it, because many more cars were brought to auction just before crossing a threshold. It's a case of a sensible-looking market being moved by a quirk in how we read numbers.

# A precise price looks firm
@src Janiszewski & Uy, Psychological Science, 2008

Suppose a seller asks five thousand dollars for a car. Another asks $4,985. Which is more likely to be negotiable? In 2008 Chris Janiszewski and Dan Uy found that the precision of an opening number changes how far people move from it. When the anchor was precise, people adjusted their own estimate less than when it was round.

A round number seems like a rough estimate that can be bargained down. A precise number seems to come from careful calculation, so it feels firmer. Later studies of negotiation found that a precise first offer made people think that the other side knew more, and they made smaller counteroffers. If you want to hold your price, a precise number can help. If you're on the other side of the table, it helps to know that the number is not necessarily more reliable.

# The membership you don't use
@src DellaVigna & Malmendier, American Economic Review, 2006

In 2006 Stefano DellaVigna and Ulrike Malmendier studied the records of several health clubs. Many members chose a monthly contract of over seventy dollars. These members went to the club about four times a month, on average. When the researchers worked out what they paid for each visit, it came to about seventeen dollars, though a ten dollar pay-per-visit option was available.

Members seemed to be too optimistic about how often they would go. The monthly plan felt like a commitment to exercise, but the plan paid off for the club and not for them. Many also stayed on the plan for months after they had stopped going. The study applies to any subscription. We tend to buy for the person we hope to be, and not for the person we are.

# Why we choose flat rates
@src Lambrecht & Skiera, Journal of Marketing Research, 2006

In 2006 Anja Lambrecht and Bernd Skiera analyzed the choices of customers of internet access providers. Many customers chose a flat rate, though they would have paid less on a plan that charged for what they used. The researchers called this the flat-rate bias. They found that it is common in many kinds of services.

They suggested several reasons. People like to know their bills in advance. They are afraid of running up charges, and they overestimate how much they'll use. A flat rate also takes away the feeling of paying each time, which can spoil the enjoyment of a service. It's a sensible choice for some people, since the peace of mind can be worth a few extra dollars. It helps to know what you are paying for, though.

# The labor illusion
@src Buell & Norton, Management Science, 2011

When you search for a flight, would you rather get results instantly or wait a bit? In 2011 Ryan Buell and Michael Norton tested this with simulated travel sites. Some showed only a progress bar. Others showed the site working, with a running list of the airlines it was checking. Results were the same in each case.

People valued the search more, and rated the site higher, when it showed its work, even when it made them wait longer. The researchers called it the labor illusion. We tend to value a service more when we can see the effort behind it. This is why a plumber who explains what they're doing can seem more valuable, and why a restaurant with an open kitchen can feel special. Showing the work can be worth the wait.

# The complaint that wins a customer
@src McCollough & Bharadwaj, 1992; de Matos et al., 2007
@conf mixed

Imagine that a company makes a mistake, and then fixes it very well. Could you end up liking them more than if nothing had gone wrong? In the 1990s researchers who study customer service called this the service recovery paradox, and early studies suggested that it could happen. Customers who were treated generously after a failure sometimes gave higher ratings than those who had no problem at all.

Later reviews found that the effect is smaller and less reliable than it first seemed. It tends to show up only when the failure is small, when it's the first one, and when the fix is fast and goes beyond what was needed. A big failure or a repeated one can wipe it out. The safe conclusion is that a good fix is worth making, but it's no reason to plan for failures.

# The middle one
@src Simonson, Journal of Consumer Research, 1989

Suppose that you're choosing a camera, and you see two models, one cheap and basic and one expensive and excellent. Now a third, in the middle, is added. In 1989 Itamar Simonson showed that adding a middle option can change what people pick, with more people choosing the one in the middle than before. He called it the compromise effect.

People are often unsure how to compare features, so they use a simple rule. The extreme options feel risky, and the middle option feels safe and defensible. It's a reason why menus and price lists often have a high-priced item that few people buy. Its job is to make the next one down look moderate. When you notice yourself picking the middle, ask whether it is the best for you, or just the one that is easiest to justify.

# The money illusion
@src Shafir, Diamond & Tversky, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 1997

Which is better, a two percent raise when prices are rising by four percent, or a two percent pay cut when prices are not rising at all? In real terms, the raise leaves you worse off. In 1997 Eldar Shafir, Peter Diamond and Amos Tversky found that many people said that the raise was better, and they felt better about it.

We tend to think about money in the numbers on the paper, and not in what it can buy. The researchers called it the money illusion. It has real effects. Employers find it easier to hold wages steady in real terms by giving small raises than to cut pay, and people feel richer when their wages go up in a time of high inflation. It's a reminder to ask what a number will buy.

# The beer on the beach
@src Thaler, Marketing Science, 1985

In 1985 Richard Thaler asked people to imagine lying on a beach on a hot day, wishing for a cold bottle of beer. A friend offers to bring one from either a fancy resort hotel or a run-down grocery store. They were asked how much they would pay. The bottle was the same, and they would drink it on the beach either way.

People said that they would pay more when the beer came from the resort. The median answers were about two dollars sixty-five for the hotel and one dollar fifty for the store. Thaler argued that we get a second kind of pleasure from a deal. It's the sense that the price was fair compared with what we expected to pay. It explains why a price that seems unreasonable in one place can feel fine in another.

# The snow shovel
@src Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, American Economic Review, 1986

In 1986 Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch and Richard Thaler asked people in Canada a question. A hardware store has been selling snow shovels for fifteen dollars. The morning after a large snowstorm, the store raises the price to twenty. Is that fair? Most people, over eight in ten, said that it was unfair.

By the usual logic of supply and demand, the price should rise when demand rises. But people judge a price against a reference point, which is the price they were used to, and they see a rise as taking advantage of someone's need. This explains why companies avoid raising prices after disasters, and why surge pricing can anger customers. Fairness is a force in markets, and it is hard to explain by logic alone.

# Experiences versus things
@src Van Boven & Gilovich, JPSP, 2003

When you have money to spend, should you buy something to have, or something to do? In 2003 Leaf Van Boven and Thomas Gilovich asked people about recent purchases of each kind. Purchases made to gain an experience, such as a trip or a concert, tended to make people happier than purchases made to own something, such as a gadget or a piece of clothing.

They suggested several reasons. Experiences are more a part of who we are, they're often shared with other people, and they are harder to compare unfavorably with other options. Things lose their novelty as we get used to them. The finding comes with caveats, since the line between the two kinds is blurry, and a thing you use to do something can be an experience. But it's a handy guide when you're deciding where to spend.

# Scarcity takes up mental room
@src Mani, Mullainathan, Shafir & Zhao, Science, 2013
@conf mixed

In 2013 Anandi Mani and her colleagues studied sugarcane farmers in India. The farmers get most of their income at harvest, so before the harvest they are short of money, and after it they are better off. The same farmers did worse on tests of reasoning and attention before the harvest than after it.

In another experiment, shoppers at a New Jersey mall were asked what they would do if they faced a car repair bill. Lower income people did worse on a reasoning test after thinking about a large bill than after a small bill, while higher income people were not affected. The idea is that worry about money takes up mental space. Some later studies have found weaker effects, so the size of the effect is still under debate. But it suggests that being short of money can affect thinking, and not just the other way round.

# You are free to say no
@src Guéguen & Pascual, 2000; Carpenter, Communication Studies, 2013
@conf mixed

In 2000 Nicolas Guéguen and Alexandre Pascual asked people on the street for a small amount of money. Some were asked in the usual way. Others were asked, and then told "but you are free to accept or to refuse." People who heard the extra sentence were more likely to give. Reminding them that the choice was theirs seemed to take away the feeling of being pushed.

A 2013 review by Christopher Carpenter found that the technique tended to work across studies. But a later and more careful review found that when it limited itself to the best designed studies, the effect shrank a lot, and it may be weaker than it looked. The idea is plausible. People resist pressure, and when they are left free to choose, they are often more willing. Whatever the size of the effect, it's a kind and honest way to ask.

# Disrupt, then reframe
@src Davis & Knowles, JPSP, 1999
@conf mixed

In 1999 Barbara Davis and Eric Knowles went door to door selling packs of note cards for a charity. They varied how they stated the price. The usual pitch was "the cards are three dollars." In another version the seller said the price in an odd way, "three hundred pennies," and then added "that's a bargain."

More people bought when the price was said in the odd way and then reframed as a bargain. The researchers called it disrupt-then-reframe. The strange phrase interrupted the usual script that people use to say no, and the reframing pointed them to a reason to say yes. Later researchers found that the effect is not always reliable, and it works best in some conditions. It's an example of how a small surprise can briefly stop an automatic refusal.

# Pay what you want
@src Gneezy, Gneezy, Nelson & Brown, Science, 2010
@conf mixed

What would happen if you let customers set their own price? In 2010 Ayelet Gneezy and her colleagues tested this at an amusement park, where a photo of visitors on a ride was normally sold at a fixed price. They tried letting customers pay whatever they wanted. Far more people bought, but the average price was under a dollar, which was less than the photo cost to make.

When the researchers added a promise that half of the price would go to charity, the results changed. People paid much more than under plain pay what you want, and the park's total revenue ended up greater than under the fixed price. Offering charity at a fixed price did not help. The idea was that the charity link gave people a reason to pay a fair amount, when they were free to choose. Later studies of pay what you want have been mixed. It seems to work best with small shared goals, and where customers feel a connection to the seller.

# Saying the other side first
@src O'Keefe, Communication Yearbook, 1999

When you try to persuade someone, is it better to present only your side or to mention the other? In 1999 Daniel O'Keefe combined the results of many experiments. The answer was that it depends on how you do it. A message that mentions the opposing view and then answers it was more persuasive than a one-sided message.

Mentioning the other side without answering it was the least persuasive of all. It seems to make people think of reasons against you, and leave them with nothing to put against them. The refutation is what counts. It shows that you've thought about the objections, and it makes you seem fair and honest. If you want to make a case, bring up the strongest objection yourself, and say why you still think you're right.

# Don't tell me what to do
@src Brehm, 1966; Bushman & Stack, JEP: Applied, 1996

In 1966 the psychologist Jack Brehm proposed that when people feel that their freedom to choose is threatened, they push back. He called it reactance. A pushy sales pitch, a strict order or a ban can make the forbidden thing more attractive. Parents of teenagers know the effect well.

In 1996 Brad Bushman and Angela Stack tested it with warning labels. People were shown descriptions of television programs, with or without a label saying that the show was violent. The labeled programs were more attractive to many of the viewers. The effect isn't universal, but it explains why a heavy-handed approach can backfire. Giving people reasons and choices tends to work better than giving them orders.

# Why a quiet message can stick
@src Hovland & Weiss, 1951; Kumkale & Albarracín, Psychological Bulletin, 2004
@conf mixed

In 1951 Carl Hovland and Walter Weiss gave people messages from sources that were trustworthy or not. At first, people were persuaded more by the trustworthy sources. But several weeks later, the difference had shrunk, because people had forgotten where the message came from while still remembering the message. They called it the sleeper effect.

It has been hard to reproduce. A review in 2004 by Tarcan Kumkale and Dolores Albarracín concluded that it happens under certain conditions. It is most likely if people only learn about the weakness of the source after they've heard the message, and if they have a chance to forget it. The finding is a reminder that a claim from a poor source can lose its label in your memory, and what is left is the claim.

# A small dose of the trick
@src McGuire, 1961; Roozenbeek et al., Science Advances, 2022

In the 1960s William McGuire proposed that people could be protected against persuasion in the same way as against disease. If you give a weak version of an argument, with a refutation, people build defenses. He called it inoculation. The idea sat for years, and in recent times it has been applied to misinformation.

In 2022 Jon Roozenbeek and his colleagues tested short videos that explained common manipulation tricks, such as scapegoating and false dilemmas, without being about any single topic. After the videos, people were better at spotting these techniques in new examples. The effects were modest, and they fade over time without a reminder. It's a promising idea for defending against persuasion. Learning how a trick works makes it harder to fool you.

# The story that made trash worth more
@src Walker & Glenn, Significant Objects, 2009
@conf mixed

In 2009 the writers Rob Walker and Joshua Glenn ran a project to test the power of a story. They bought about a hundred cheap objects, mostly at thrift shops, such as a plastic horse head or a toy mouse. They asked writers to invent a short story about each one, and then sold them online with the story in the listing.

The objects had cost about 129 dollars in total, and they sold for about 3,600 dollars, nearly thirty times as much. It wasn't a controlled experiment, and some buyers were motivated by the project as well as by the objects. But it fits with other studies about stories and value. We don't buy only what a thing is. We buy what it means, and a good story can supply the meaning.

# The note that was written by hand
@src Garner, Journal of Consumer Psychology, 2005

In 2005 Randy Garner mailed surveys to a large number of people. Some got just a cover letter and some a letter with a signature. Others got a small sticky note, written by hand, asking for help. The people with the handwritten note were much more likely to fill in and send back the survey.

Garner suggested that a personal note shows that someone has taken the time to ask. Since it's hard to fake, it triggers a feeling of wanting to return the favor. The effect has been seen in other studies of requests. It's a reminder that in an age of automated messages, a small sign of real effort can stand out. If you need something from someone, take a minute to write them a few personal words.
