# The pan that wouldn't stick
@src Plunkett, DuPont records, 1938

In April 1938 a young chemist at DuPont, Roy Plunkett, was working on refrigerants. He had a cylinder of a gas, tetrafluoroethylene, and when he opened the valve, nothing came out. The cylinder was not empty. It weighed as much as before. Puzzled, he cut the cylinder open, and found a white, waxy powder inside. The gas had polymerized on its own.

The powder was extremely slippery, and it was not harmed by acids, heat or solvents. It was named Teflon. Its first big use was in the Manhattan Project, where it lined pipes that carried corrosive gases. Only later, in the 1950s, was it used for frying pans, after a French engineer, Marc Grégoire, applied it to his fishing tackle, and his wife suggested using it on her pans. A failure in the lab gave us the nonstick pan.

# The candy bar and the microwave
@src Raytheon records; Spencer, 1945

Percy Spencer was an engineer at the American company Raytheon, working on magnetrons, the tubes that produce microwaves for radar. He had only a primary school education, but became one of the best engineers of his time. One day around 1945, as he stood near a magnetron, he noticed that a candy bar in his pocket had melted.

He was curious. He tried popcorn kernels, which popped, and then an egg, which exploded in the face of a colleague. Raytheon patented a microwave cooking device, and in 1947 sold the first Radarange. It was nearly six feet tall, weighed about seven hundred pounds, and cost thousands of dollars. Smaller ones, for homes, came out in the late 1960s. Today, more than ninety percent of American homes have one.

# Burrs on a dog
@src de Mestral, patent, 1955

In 1941 a Swiss engineer named George de Mestral went for a walk in the Alps with his dog. When he got home, he found that the dog's fur, and his own trousers, were covered with burrs, the seed pods of the burdock plant. He was curious about how they stuck so firmly, and looked at one under a microscope.

He saw that each burr was covered in tiny hooks, which caught on loops in the fabric. It took him almost fifteen years to develop a way of making a fastener that used the same principle, with one strip of tiny hooks, and one of tiny loops. He patented it in 1955, and called it Velcro, from the French velours, velvet, and crochet, hook. It was used in space suits, in shoes and in many other places. It's a famous example of nature giving an idea to engineers.

# A weak glue and a hymnbook
@src Fry, 3M records; Nayak & Ketteringham, Breakthroughs!, 1986

In 1968 Spencer Silver, a chemist at 3M, was trying to make a very strong adhesive. He failed. He made a glue that was weak. It stuck to things, but could be peeled off without leaving a mark, and be used again. He thought it was interesting, but no one could think of a use for it. He promoted it inside the company for years.

In 1974 another 3M employee, Art Fry, who sang in a church choir, was annoyed that the slips of paper that he used as bookmarks in his hymnbook kept falling out. He remembered Silver's glue, and put a little on the paper. The notes stayed put, and could be removed without damage. They were launched as Post-it Notes in 1980. Silver's glue had been a solution in search of a problem, until Fry found one.

# The broken flask
@src Bénédictus, 1903

In 1903 a French chemist, Édouard Bénédictus, was in his laboratory, when he knocked a glass flask off a shelf. It fell to the floor and cracked, but didn't shatter. It stayed in the shape of a flask, with the pieces held together. He asked his assistant what had been in it. It had held a solution of cellulose nitrate, a plastic, which had dried to form a thin coating on the inside.

Bénédictus thought of the injuries from broken windshields, and he realized that this could be used to make safety glass. He patented a laminated glass, with a layer of plastic between two sheets of glass, in 1909. It was first used in gas masks in the First World War. It was adopted in cars in the 1920s, and is now required in windshields. A clumsy moment helped to save many lives.

# The rubber that Goodyear cooked
@src Goodyear, Gum-Elastic and Its Varieties, 1853

Natural rubber was a disappointment. In heat, it became soft and sticky, and in cold, it grew brittle. Many people tried to make it more useful. Charles Goodyear, an American inventor who was repeatedly in debt, and went to jail more than once for unpaid bills, devoted years to experiments.

In 1839, according to his own account, he accidentally dropped a mixture of rubber and sulfur on a hot stove. It charred, like leather, but stayed flexible. He found the right temperature and time for heating, a process that is now called vulcanization, after Vulcan, the Roman god of fire. It made rubber durable and elastic. It led to tires, hoses, belts and shoe soles. Goodyear never became rich. He died deep in debt in 1860.

# The sweet taste on his hands
@src Fahlberg & Remsen, Berichte der deutschen chemischen Gesellschaft, 1879

In 1879 Constantin Fahlberg, a Russian chemist who worked in the laboratory of Ira Remsen at Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore, was studying coal tar compounds. One evening, he sat down to dinner and noticed that the bread tasted unusually sweet. He realized that it was his hands. He had not washed them properly after the day's work.

He went back to the laboratory, and tasted what was in his beakers, which was not wise, and found the sweet one. It was saccharin, the first artificial sweetener, about three hundred times sweeter than sugar. Fahlberg patented it, and made a fortune, without giving credit to Remsen, which angered him. It came into wide use during sugar shortages in the First World War. Questions about its safety were raised for years, and it's now regarded as safe.

# The spring that walked
@src James Industries; Fortune, 1945

In 1943 Richard James, a naval engineer in Philadelphia, was working on a spring for ships, to keep sensitive instruments steady in rough seas. One of the springs fell off a shelf, and rather than lying where it landed, it flipped over end after end, and walked down to the floor. He took it home to show his wife, Betty, who said that children would like it.

She found a name in the dictionary, Slinky, meaning sleek and graceful. They borrowed about five hundred dollars to make four hundred. In November 1945, at a department store in Philadelphia, Gimbels, the display was a slope with a Slinky walking down it. They sold all the Slinkies in ninety minutes. It has since sold hundreds of millions. It's a toy that was invented by chance, and with the price kept low for many years.

# The wallpaper cleaner
@src Rainwater, Kutol Products; Smithsonian

Play-Doh began as a product for cleaning coal soot from wallpaper. In the 1930s the Cincinnati company Kutol Products made a soft, putty-like compound that was rolled over the walls. It worked well when homes were heated by coal. But after the Second World War, when oil and gas replaced coal, sales fell.

In the early 1950s a nursery school teacher, who was the sister-in-law of the company's owner, found that children liked to model with the wallpaper cleaner. A nontoxic version was developed, with colors added. It was marketed in 1956, as Play-Doh. Within a few years, it was a huge success, and it's sold billions of cans. The product is a case of finding a new use for something that no longer worked in its old one.

# Pie tins in the air
@src Wham-O; Stancil, The Frisbee Book, 2011

In the late nineteenth century, a bakery in Bridgeport, Connecticut, the Frisbie Pie Company, sold pies in round metal tins, stamped with the name Frisbie. College students in New England had a habit of throwing the empty tins to each other, shouting frisbie as a warning. After the Second World War, a Californian, Walter Frederick Morrison, made a plastic version, based on a flying disc that he and his future wife had tossed on a beach.

He sold the disc under the name Pluto Platter, after the interest in flying saucers. In 1957 the toy company Wham-O bought the rights, and changed the name to Frisbee, which alluded to the tins. It has sold in the hundreds of millions, and gave rise to sports such as ultimate and disc golf. The name came from a pie.

# The first photograph
@src Niépce, 1826 or 1827; Daguerre, 1839

The earliest surviving photograph was taken by a French inventor, Nicéphore Niépce, in about 1826 or 1827, from an upstairs window of his estate at Le Gras. He used a pewter plate coated with bitumen, a type of asphalt, which hardens in light, and exposed it in a camera for many hours, perhaps eight or more. The image, called View from the Window at Le Gras, shows rooftops, in a blurry way.

Niépce teamed up with Louis Daguerre, a painter and showman. Niépce died in 1833, and Daguerre developed a faster, clearer process, using a silvered copper plate. He announced the daguerreotype in 1839, and the French government bought the rights, and gave them to the world. Exposure times fell to a few minutes. The first photo of a person, probably by accident, shows a man having his boots shined on a Paris street, in 1838.

# Telescopes and spectacles
@src Van Helden, The Invention of the Telescope, 1977; Rosen, 1956

Spectacles were probably invented in Italy around 1286. Nobody knows who the inventor was. A monk in Pisa wrote in 1306 that they had been invented about twenty years before, and that he'd met the man who invented them. They were convex lenses, to help old people to read, and they spread quickly across Europe.

The telescope came more than three hundred years later. In 1608 a Dutch spectacle maker, Hans Lipperhey, applied for a patent on a device that made distant objects seem near. The authorities refused, because other people claimed the same idea. Galileo heard of it in 1609, built his own, and turned it on the sky. He saw the mountains of the Moon, the moons of Jupiter, and the phases of Venus. A device meant for ships and soldiers changed astronomy.

# The shopping cart
@src Goldman, Oklahoma Historical Society; Smithsonian

Before 1937, shoppers carried baskets. In that year, Sylvan Goldman, who owned a chain of grocery stores in Oklahoma City, noticed that customers stopped buying when their baskets got heavy. He looked at a folding chair, and had an idea. He asked a mechanic to attach wheels to the legs and a wire basket to the seat. The first carts were made.

Shoppers didn't want to use them. Men thought them unmanly, and women thought them like a baby carriage. Goldman hired people, models, to push the carts around the store, to demonstrate that it was normal. It worked. Sales rose, since people bought more. Carts spread, and Goldman became a millionaire. In the 1940s, a design that nested was introduced, so that carts could be stored in a smaller space.

# The pen that won the war
@src Bíró; Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum

László Bíró was a Hungarian journalist. He was annoyed by fountain pens, which leaked, and smudged. At a newspaper printing plant, he saw that the ink used for printing dried quickly. He thought it might work in a pen, but it was too thick to flow. His brother, a chemist, helped him to make a pen with a small ball at the tip, which picked up thick ink from a reservoir and rolled it onto the paper. They patented it in 1938.

Bíró fled the Nazis, and moved to Argentina in 1940. The British Royal Air Force bought thousands of his pens during the war, since they worked at high altitudes where fountain pens leaked. Later the French Baron Bich simplified the manufacture. His company, Bic, sold the pen at a very low price. The ballpoint is still the best-selling pen. Bíró's birthday, September 29, is the Day of the Inventor in Argentina.

# The first electronic television
@src Schwartz, The Last Lone Inventor, 2002

Philo Farnsworth was a farm boy from Idaho. At the age of fourteen, plowing a field, he realized that an image could be captured in horizontal lines, like the rows of a plowed field, and be sent as an electronic signal. He explained it to his chemistry teacher, who kept his sketch. At twenty-one, in a laboratory in San Francisco, he succeeded. On September 7, 1927, he sent an electronic image of a straight line.

A struggle over patents followed with a large company, RCA. The teacher's sketch, which he produced, helped Farnsworth to win. By the time that television became popular after the Second World War, his patents had nearly run out, and he gained little. In his later years, he turned to nuclear fusion. He appeared once on television, on a game show, in 1957. The mechanical television of John Logie Baird, in Britain, came first, in 1926, but the electronic form won.

# Was QWERTY made to slow typists?
@src Liebowitz & Margolis, Journal of Law and Economics, 1990; Yasuoka & Yasuoka, 2011
@conf mixed

The keyboard layout used on most computers, called QWERTY after the first six letters on the top row, is awkward. Common letters are placed far from the strongest fingers. A popular story says that it was designed on purpose to slow down typists, so that the mechanical typewriter wouldn't jam. The story was widely repeated, and used as an example of how a poor standard can lock itself in.

Historians now doubt it. The typewriter was invented by Christopher Latham Sholes, in Milwaukee, in the 1860s. The layout evolved over several years, and there's evidence that it was shaped by the needs of telegraph operators, who transcribed messages. It was sold by Remington in 1873. Rival layouts, such as Dvorak, have not been shown to be much faster. The truth is still debated, but the neat story may be too neat.
