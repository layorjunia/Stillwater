# The loom that read cards
@src Jacquard, patent of 1804; Babbage, about 1830

In the early 1800s the French weaver Joseph Marie Jacquard built a loom that could weave complicated patterns by itself. A chain of stiff cards with holes punched in them passed through the machine. Each card matched one row of the design, and the holes told the loom which threads to lift. To weave a different pattern, the weaver only had to change the cards.

It was a way to store instructions outside the machine. Some weavers feared for their jobs, and in Lyon they attacked the looms, but it spread. About 1830 the English mathematician Charles Babbage took up the idea of punched cards, to control his planned Analytical Engine, a design for a general-purpose calculating machine. Cards continued to feed computers until the 1970s, and the first programs were stacks of them.

# The census that was counted by machine
@src Census Bureau; Hollerith, 1890

The United States counts its people every ten years, and by the 1880s this had become a problem. The census of 1880 took more than eight years to tabulate, by hand, and the population kept growing. Officials feared that the next one would not be finished before the one after it began. In 1888 the Census Bureau held a competition for a better method, and a young engineer named Herman Hollerith won.

His machine read cards with holes punched for each person's age, sex and other facts. As a card passed through, pins went through the holes and made an electrical circuit, which advanced a counter. Operators could handle about 7,000 cards a day. Experts had estimated that without the machines, the 1890 census would have taken 13 years to tabulate, and with them it took much less. Hollerith set up a company, which in time, after mergers, was renamed International Business Machines in 1924.

# Two inventors of the chip
@src Kilby, 1958; Noyce, 1959

In the summer of 1958 an engineer named Jack Kilby, new at Texas Instruments, had the idea that all the parts of a circuit could be made from the same piece of semiconductor. On September 12 he showed a working circuit, built from a sliver of germanium, with fine wires linking the parts. It was crude, but it worked, and it was the first integrated circuit.

Months later, at Fairchild Semiconductor, Robert Noyce thought of a version that did away with the wires, by laying thin metal lines over a layer of oxide on silicon, using a process invented by his colleague Jean Hoerni. That was the version that could be made in large numbers. The courts gave Kilby credit for the idea and Noyce for the process, and the companies shared their licenses in 1966. Kilby won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2000. Noyce had died in 1977.

# A calculator and the first microprocessor
@src Intel, 1971

In 1969 a Japanese company, Busicom, asked the young company Intel to design a set of chips for a line of printing calculators. The first plan needed a dozen chips, each built for one task. Intel's Ted Hoff and Stan Mazor suggested a simpler design, with one general chip that could be programmed to do different jobs. Federico Faggin, who joined Intel in 1970, and Masatoshi Shima of Busicom worked out the details.

The result was the Intel 4004, a chip that handled four bits at a time, with 2,300 transistors and a clock speed of 740 kilohertz. Intel advertised it in November 1971 as a computer on a chip. Intel bought back the rights from Busicom, which was in financial trouble, in return for the money that Busicom had paid. It is widely called the first commercial microprocessor, and its descendants run phones, cars and machines today.

# Three dots, from across the ocean
@src Marconi, December 12, 1901

On December 12, 1901, on a cold hill in Newfoundland, the Italian inventor Guglielmo Marconi and his assistant George Kemp sat with a telephone earpiece pressed to the ear. They had flown a kite, to raise a long wire that served as an antenna, after the wind broke a balloon that they had planned to use. At a station in Poldhu, in Cornwall, England, a transmitter was sending the Morse code for the letter S, three short dots, again and again.

Marconi said that he heard them. The distance was about 3,400 kilometers. Many scientists doubted it, since radio waves were thought to travel in straight lines, and so should not curve around the Earth. The claim could not be checked, since only the two men had heard it. Later and better tests showed that the signals can in fact travel far, by reflecting from a layer in the upper atmosphere. In 1909 Marconi shared the Nobel Prize in Physics.

# The song that nearly broke the MP3
@src Brandenburg and Fraunhofer, 1988 to 1993

In the late 1980s the German engineer Karlheinz Brandenburg was working on how to compress music to a small size. His idea was to throw away the parts of a sound that the ear cannot hear anyway, such as a quiet tone right after a loud one. To test it he used many pieces of music. One of them caused a problem. It was Tom's Diner, a song by Suzanne Vega, sung without any instruments.

A single voice, with nothing else to mask the flaws, was very hard to compress, and in his early version the voice was badly distorted. Brandenburg listened to the song hundreds of times, as he refined the method with colleagues, including Jim Johnston of AT&T. The result was the third layer of the MPEG-1 audio standard, which was named MP3. It made music files about a tenth of their size, and it started the age of downloaded and streamed music.

# Printing in layers
@src Hull, U.S. Patent 4,575,330, 1986

In 1983 an engineer named Chuck Hull, who worked with ultraviolet light to harden coatings on tables, had an idea. If he aimed a beam at the surface of a bath of liquid plastic, it would harden only the spot that the light touched. By hardening one thin layer, lowering it, and hardening the next, he could build up an object. He made a small black cup that way, and called the method stereolithography.

A team in France had filed a patent for the same process a few weeks earlier, in July 1984, but they let it lapse. Hull's patent was issued on March 11, 1986, and he founded 3D Systems, which sold its first machines in the late 1980s for about 100,000 dollars. At first the machines made models for designers. Later the technique, called 3D printing, was extended to metal, to concrete, and to parts for aircraft and medicine.

# The shot-down plane and your phone
@src Korean Air Lines Flight 007, 1983; Clinton, 2000

On September 1, 1983, Korean Air Lines Flight 007 strayed off its route and into Soviet airspace, and was shot down, with 269 people on board, after a navigation error. In the aftermath President Ronald Reagan said that the American satellite navigation system, GPS, then still being built for the military, would be made freely available for civilian use, to prevent such disasters.

The civilian signal, though, was deliberately degraded, in a program called Selective Availability, so that it was less accurate than the military one. On May 1, 2000, President Bill Clinton ordered that the degrading be stopped, and the switch was made just after midnight. Overnight, civilian receivers became much more accurate. Today GPS guides cars, phones, farm machinery and airplanes, and it keeps the clocks of banks and networks in time.

# The cloudy liquid that was almost thrown out
@src Kwolek, DuPont, 1965

In 1965 the chemist Stephanie Kwolek, who worked for DuPont, was looking for a new fiber, one stronger and stiffer than the nylon that her company already made. She mixed a polymer in a solvent, and got a liquid that was thin and cloudy. Solutions like that were usually thrown away, since they were expected to be thick and clear. She asked a technician to spin it into fibers, in a machine with tiny holes called a spinneret.

The technician at first was reluctant, because he thought that it would clog the machine. When he finally did, the fiber was strong, five times stronger than steel for its weight. It was named Kevlar, and it was sold from 1971. It is used in bulletproof vests, tires, ropes and cables, and in the hulls of boats. It is a good example of looking carefully at an odd result, and not just discarding it.

# A gray brick that sold a hundred million
@src Nintendo, 1989

In 1989 Nintendo released the Game Boy in Japan, on April 21. It had a small, gray-green screen with no color, and no backlight, and it ran for many hours on four batteries. Its designer was Gunpei Yokoi, an engineer whose rule was to use old, cheap and well-understood technology in new ways, which he called lateral thinking with withered technology. His rivals' machines had color screens and cost more, and ran out of battery in a few hours.

In North America and Europe, the Game Boy was sold with a game called Tetris, a puzzle of falling blocks that had come from Russia. Anyone could play it, and it drew adults as well as children. Together, the Game Boy and its successor the Game Boy Color sold more than 118 million units. It showed that a game machine did not need the best technology to succeed, and that a good game, and a price, and battery life, could matter more.

# The picture that taught computers to compress
@series people
@src Hooker, Playboy, 1972; Signal and Image Processing Institute, 1973

In 1973 a group of researchers at the University of Southern California needed a picture to test a method of image compression. They were in a hurry. Someone walked in with a copy of a magazine, and they scanned the top part of the centerfold. It was a photo of a model, Lena Forsén, taken by Dwight Hooker, in the November 1972 issue of Playboy, and it was a lucky choice.

It had faces, fine detail, smooth areas and texture, so it showed what a method did well or badly. Others adopted it, and it became the standard test picture in image processing for decades. It was not licensed. In 1991 Playboy raised its objection, though in time it let it pass. Some journals have since asked authors not to use it, because it was taken from a nude photo. It is a reminder that a convenient choice can set a standard.
