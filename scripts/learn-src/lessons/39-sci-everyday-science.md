# Why the sky is blue
@src Rayleigh, Philosophical Magazine, 1871

Sunlight looks white, but it is a mixture of all colors. When it passes through the air, the gas molecules scatter some of it in all directions. In 1871 Lord Rayleigh worked out that short wavelengths, which are blue, are scattered far more strongly than long wavelengths, which are red. The scattering rises very steeply as the wavelength gets shorter.

So when you look at the sky away from the Sun, you see blue light that has been bounced toward you from all over the sky. Violet is scattered even more, but the Sun gives out less violet, the upper atmosphere absorbs some, and our eyes are less sensitive to it. At sunset, the light has to pass through much more air. The blue has been scattered out of the beam, and the reds and oranges are what's left.

# Why ice floats
@src Pauling, Journal of the American Chemical Society, 1935

Most solids are denser than their liquids, so they sink. Ice is a rare exception, and it matters a great deal. Water molecules form bonds with their neighbors, called hydrogen bonds. In liquid water, the molecules keep moving, and the bonds break and re-form constantly. When water freezes, the molecules lock into an open, six-sided pattern that holds them farther apart than in the liquid.

That makes ice take up about nine percent more space than the same water, so it is less dense and it floats. Water is also densest at about four degrees Celsius, just above freezing, so in a cold lake the coldest water ends up at the top, and it freezes there first. The ice then acts as a lid that insulates the water below. Fish and other living things can survive a winter under the ice. If ice sank, lakes would freeze from the bottom up.

# Does hot water freeze faster?
@src Burridge & Linden, Scientific Reports, 2016; Burridge & Hallstadius, 2020
@conf contested

In 1963 a Tanzanian schoolboy named Erasto Mpemba noticed that his hot ice cream mixture froze before the cold one. The idea that hot water can freeze faster than cold, now called the Mpemba effect, has been argued over since. Many explanations have been proposed, such as evaporation, dissolved gases and the way convection moves heat.

In 2016 Henry Burridge and Paul Linden carried out careful experiments and found no evidence for the effect. In 2020 Burridge and Oscar Hallstadius found that it could be produced, but only in particular conditions, such as with the container walls roughened to speed up the start of freezing. So the effect is not general, and it depends on how freezing is measured. It's a good case of a popular claim whose truth depends on the details of the test.

# The honeycomb
@src Hales, Discrete & Computational Geometry, 2001

Bees build their wax cells in a pattern of six-sided hexagons. For well over a thousand years it was thought that this is the most efficient way to divide a flat surface into equal cells with the least wall. The ancient Greek mathematician Pappus argued that bees choose the shape to save wax, and no one could prove that it is really the best.

The proof came in 1999, when the mathematician Thomas Hales showed that no other arrangement of equal cells has a shorter total boundary. It was called the honeycomb conjecture. Bees don't do geometry. Some research suggests that the cells start out round, and that the warm wax flows into hexagons where the circles press together. Natural selection favors bees that waste little wax, so the result is an excellent solution reached without any calculation.

# Why leaves change color
@src Plant physiology textbooks; Archetti et al., Trends in Ecology & Evolution, 2009

In the summer, a leaf is green because it is full of chlorophyll, the pigment that captures sunlight for photosynthesis. The leaf also contains yellow and orange pigments called carotenoids, but the green hides them. As the days get shorter and cooler in the fall, the tree stops making chlorophyll and breaks down what's left, to reuse the nutrients.

As the green fades, the yellow and orange that were there all along appear. The reds and purples are different. They come from anthocyanins, pigments that the leaf makes new in the fall. Why trees would spend energy on this is still debated. One idea is that the red acts as a sunscreen while the tree recovers its nutrients, and another is that it is a signal that warns insects away.

# Why onions make you cry
@src Imai et al., Nature, 2002

When you cut an onion, you break open its cells. Chemicals that were stored apart then mix, and a series of reactions begins. For years, scientists thought that a single enzyme was involved. In 2002 a team of researchers from a Japanese food company, led by Shinsuke Imai, found that a second enzyme was also needed, which they called lachrymatory factor synthase.

The enzyme makes a volatile gas that floats up from the board and reaches your eyes. It irritates the nerves in the surface of the eye, and the eye makes tears to wash it away. Later, researchers used the finding to develop onions with very little of the enzyme, and they cause few tears. Some of the old tricks, such as chilling the onion before you cut it, work by slowing the reactions down.

# Birds with a compass
@src Wiltschko & Wiltschko, Science, 1972; Hore & Mouritsen, Annual Review of Biophysics, 2016

Birds that migrate thousands of miles, and then return to the same small place, need a way to find their way. In the 1960s and 1970s, Wolfgang and Roswitha Wiltschko showed that European robins can sense Earth's magnetic field. The birds kept at night in cages tried to hop in the direction of their migration, and when the magnetic field around them was changed, their hopping direction changed too.

Their compass was unusual. It didn't tell north from south by the direction of the field. It sensed the angle at which the field lines slope toward the ground. How the sensing works is still debated. One leading idea is that light-sensitive molecules in the eye, called cryptochromes, form pairs of electrons whose behavior depends on the field, which would mean that birds could in some sense see the magnetic field.

# How bats see with sound
@src Spallanzani, 1793; Griffin, 1938

In the 1790s the Italian scientist Lazzaro Spallanzani did a cruel experiment. He blinded bats and released them in a room hung with threads. The bats flew around without hitting anything. When he covered their ears or mouths instead, they crashed. He concluded that bats find their way using their ears, but no one believed him, since sound that you can't hear seemed impossible.

In 1938 a Harvard student named Donald Griffin and a physicist, George Pierce, used a device that could detect ultrasound. They found that bats were making a stream of high-pitched cries, beyond human hearing. Griffin later named the ability echolocation. A bat listens for the echoes of its calls, and from the delay and the pattern, it builds up a picture of insects and obstacles. Some bats can catch an insect in total darkness.

# The mirror test
@src Gallup, Science, 1970; Prior, Schwarz & Güntürkün, PLoS Biology, 2008
@conf mixed

In 1970 the psychologist Gordon Gallup put chimpanzees in front of a mirror for several days. Then he marked their faces with a dye while they were under anesthesia. When the chimps woke up and looked in the mirror, they touched the mark on their own faces. They seemed to know that the image was themselves.

Since then, the mirror test has been given to many animals. Great apes, dolphins, elephants and some birds have passed it. In 2008 a study found that magpies tried to remove a sticker from their own throats when they saw it in a mirror. Whether passing the test shows self-awareness is debated, and some animals may recognize themselves in other ways, such as by smell. A failure doesn't prove that an animal has no sense of self, since many animals aren't interested in mirrors.

# The five-second rule
@src Miranda & Schaffner, Applied and Environmental Microbiology, 2016

Many people believe that if you pick food up within five seconds of dropping it, it's safe to eat. In 2016 Robyn Miranda and Donald Schaffner at Rutgers University tested this. They dropped four foods, which were watermelon, bread, buttered bread and gummy candy, on four surfaces coated with a harmless bacterium. They varied the time the food touched the surface, and counted the bacteria that transferred.

Bacteria sometimes transferred in less than a second, and longer contact usually meant more. But the type of food and surface mattered even more. Watermelon, which is wet, picked up the most. Gummy candy picked up the least. Carpet passed on fewer bacteria than tile or steel. So the rule is wrong, in the sense that time isn't the only factor. If a wet food falls on a hard floor, it's picked up in a flash.

# Why blood types matter
@src Landsteiner, 1901

Before 1901, a blood transfusion was a gamble. Sometimes it saved a patient, and sometimes it killed. The Austrian doctor Karl Landsteiner mixed blood samples from his colleagues and himself, and saw that some combinations clumped together and others did not. From this he found that people could be sorted into groups, now called A, B and O. A fourth group, AB, was found soon afterward.

The groups differ in the sugar markers on the surface of red blood cells. The immune system attacks blood with markers that it doesn't recognize, so a mismatch causes a dangerous reaction. Matching the types made transfusions safe, and it won Landsteiner a Nobel Prize in 1930. A separate system, the Rh factor, was added later. It is why O negative blood is so valuable. It has none of the major markers, so almost anyone can receive it in an emergency.

# We are not ten times bacteria
@src Sender, Fuchs & Milo, PLoS Biology, 2016

For decades, textbooks said that the body has ten times more bacterial cells than human cells. It's a vivid fact, and it was repeated everywhere. But no one could say where it came from. It seems to trace back to a rough calculation made in the 1970s. In 2016 Ron Sender, Shai Fuchs and Ron Milo went back and counted again, using what is known about the sizes of organs and the numbers of cells in them.

They estimated about thirty trillion human cells and about thirty-eight trillion bacteria in a typical adult man, a ratio closer to one and a third to one. The most numerous cells in the body are blood cells, not the bacteria in the gut. The new numbers have a lot of uncertainty. But the dramatic ten to one claim was far off, and it shows how a number can survive for decades without being checked.

# How wings really lift
@src Anderson & Eberhardt, Understanding Flight, 2001

You may have heard that an airplane wing is curved on top, so that air has to travel farther over the top and must speed up to meet the air under the wing at the other side. This is called the equal transit time explanation. It's wrong. The air over the top gets there well before the air under the wing, and nothing makes the two meet.

A wing lifts because it pushes air downward. It is tilted, and its shape guides the air so that the flow turns down behind it. By Newton's third law, the air pushes the wing up. The pressure differences that you hear about in the Bernoulli explanation are real, but they are a result of the wing turning the air, not the cause. A flat sheet at an angle can fly too, and so can a plane upside down.

# Lightning does strike twice
@src NOAA National Weather Service, lightning safety

The saying is that lightning never strikes the same place twice. It's wrong. Tall, pointed objects are struck again and again. The Empire State Building in New York is hit many times each year, and some storms hit it more than once in the same few minutes. A lightning rod does the same job on a house. It gives lightning a safe path to the ground.

Lightning looks for the easiest route, and it often takes the same one. The danger for people is real. In the United States, lightning kills people every year, and most of them are outdoors. The National Weather Service's advice is simple. When thunder roars, go indoors, and wait about half an hour after the last thunder before going outside. The idea that you are safe if it isn't raining is false.

# Dogs read pointing
@src Hare, Brown, Williamson & Tomasello, Science, 2002

If you point at something, your dog will probably look where you point. That seems trivial, but in 2002 Brian Hare and his colleagues showed that it isn't. They hid food under one of two cups, and then pointed at the right one. Dogs found the food, and they did better than chimpanzees and wolves raised by people.

Even young puppies could do it, with little experience of humans. The researchers argued that dogs have been shaped by thousands of years of living with people, and that they became especially good at reading our signals. It seems to be a case of domestication changing not just how an animal looks, but how it thinks. Your dog is looking at your face, and at where your hand points, for clues.

# The foxes that became tame
@src Trut, American Scientist, 1999

In 1959 the Russian geneticist Dmitri Belyaev began an experiment in Siberia. He took silver foxes from fur farms and bred only the tamest ones, those that showed the least fear of people. In each generation, he chose the least aggressive animals and let them breed. He wanted to know whether taming alone could reproduce the changes seen in dogs.

Within a few generations the foxes were wagging their tails and licking their keepers' hands. And other traits appeared that no one had selected for. Some had floppy ears, curly tails, and patches of white in their fur. The experiment, which has continued for decades under Lyudmila Trut, suggests that selecting for behavior can change the body too. It may help to explain why so many domestic animals share similar features.

# The Comet's windows
@src Court of Inquiry into the Comet accidents, 1955
@series tech

In 1952 the de Havilland Comet became the world's first jet airliner. It flew faster and higher than any plane in service. Then, in 1954, two Comets broke apart in the air within three months of each other, one near the island of Elba and one near Naples. Many people died, and the planes were grounded.

Investigators recovered wreckage and tested a Comet fuselage in a tank of water, pressurizing and releasing it again and again to mimic flights. It failed after a few thousand cycles, with a crack that began at the corner of a window. The stress was concentrated at the sharp corners of the windows, and the metal had weakened from fatigue. Later aircraft have windows with rounded corners. The disaster led to major changes in how aircraft are designed and tested.

# Trees keep a diary
@src Douglass, 1920s; Schulman, 1958

Each year, most trees in temperate climates add a ring of new wood. A year with plenty of water makes a wide ring, and a dry one makes a narrow ring. In the early 1900s the astronomer Andrew Ellicott Douglass noticed that trees in the same region showed the same pattern of wide and narrow rings. He used it to match one tree to another, and so to date the wood.

The method is called dendrochronology. By overlapping the rings of living trees with those of old beams and dead trees, scientists have built records going back thousands of years. Some bristlecone pines in California are about four thousand years old. Tree rings are used to date buildings and ships, and to study past droughts and climate. Every tree is a record of its own weather.

# The smell of rain
@src Bear & Thomas, Nature, 1964; Joung & Buie, Nature Communications, 2015

After a long dry spell, rain on the ground produces a distinctive earthy smell. In 1964 two Australian scientists, Isabel Joy Bear and Richard Thomas, gave it a name, petrichor, from the Greek words for stone and for the fluid that flows in the veins of the gods. They found that it comes from an oil released by plants and soils in dry weather.

Part of the smell is a chemical called geosmin, made by soil bacteria. Humans are very sensitive to it, and can detect it at extremely low concentrations. In 2015 Young Soo Joung and Cullen Buie filmed raindrops with high speed cameras. They saw that each drop traps tiny bubbles of air when it lands, and the bubbles burst and spray a fine mist of smell into the air. That's how the scent reaches you so quickly.

# The coffee ring
@src Deegan et al., Nature, 1997

When a drop of coffee dries on a table, it leaves a ring with a pale center. You'd expect the stain to be evenly spread. In 1997 Robert Deegan and his colleagues at the University of Chicago explained why. The edge of the drop is stuck to the surface, so as water evaporates, the edge can't shrink. A flow of liquid from the middle carries the particles outward to the edge.

The particles pile up in a ring. The effect appears in many liquids that contain particles, such as paint and ink, and it's a nuisance in printing and in making coatings. Scientists found that particles of other shapes, such as ovals, don't pile up in the same way, and that the effect can be reduced. It's a small puzzle that you can see at your kitchen table, with a deep explanation.
