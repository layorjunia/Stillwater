# Supply and demand
@src Marshall, 1890

When something is scarce, its price tends to rise. When it's plentiful, it falls. The English economist Alfred Marshall drew the now-famous crossing curves of supply and demand in his 1890 textbook. One curve shows how much buyers want at each price, and the other shows how much sellers will provide.

The point where they cross is the price at which the amount that sellers want to sell equals the amount that buyers want to buy. It's a simple picture, but it explains a lot about how markets work. It's why umbrellas cost more in a downpour, why concert tickets rise when a show is popular, and why a bumper crop of apples brings down the price. Prices are signals that tell everybody what's scarce.

# Marginal thinking
@src Menger; Jevons; Walras, 1871-74

Water is essential for life but cheap, and diamonds are useless for survival but expensive. Economists puzzled over this paradox until the 1870s, when Carl Menger, William Stanley Jevons and Léon Walras each realized that value depends on the next unit, not the total.

Because you already have plenty of water, one more glass is worth little, while one more diamond is scarce. Decisions are made at the margin. It's a way of thinking that helps in everyday choices. Instead of asking whether something is good, you ask whether a little more of it is worth the cost. Is one more hour of work worth more than one more hour of sleep? The answer depends on how much of each you've already had.

# Comparative advantage
@src Ricardo, 1817

In 1817 the economist David Ricardo showed something surprising: two parties can both gain from trade even if one is better at everything. Imagine a lawyer who also types faster than her assistant. She still comes out ahead by letting the assistant type, because her time is worth more spent on legal work.

The same logic applies to countries, and it's the main reason that economists generally favor trade. Each country does best by focusing on what it does relatively well, and trading for the rest. It doesn't mean that everyone benefits equally, or that nobody loses a job. The theory is about the total, and the losers can be real. But it explains why trade, on the whole, makes people richer and not poorer.

# Adam Smith: the pin factory and the invisible hand
@src Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776)

In The Wealth of Nations, published in 1776, Adam Smith described a pin factory where the work was divided into about eighteen distinct steps. He said ten workers, each specializing in a few tasks, could produce about 48,000 pins a day, while a single worker doing everything might make one pin a day, or at most twenty. The division of labor makes everyone far more productive.

The phrase "invisible hand" is one of the best-known in economics, and Smith used it only once in the book. He argued that people pursuing their own interests may, without intending it, end up helping society. Yet he was no simple cheerleader for business. In the same book, he warned that merchants rarely meet together without it ending in a conspiracy against the public. Smith held both ideas, that markets work well and that they need watching.

# Inflation
@src Standard macroeconomics text

Inflation means that prices in general are rising, so each dollar buys less. Many central banks aim for about 2 percent a year, which they consider a healthy level. A little inflation encourages people to spend and invest, and makes it easier for wages to adjust.

Even 2 percent adds up. At that rate, prices double in about 35 years. At 7 percent, they double in about 10. That's why money sitting in a drawer slowly loses what it can buy, and why a savings account that pays less than inflation is losing value in real terms. When you compare what something cost in the past with what it costs now, it's worth adjusting for inflation, since a dollar in 1980 bought far more than a dollar does today.

# Hyperinflation
@src Hanke & Kwok, 2009

When a government prints too much money, prices can spiral out of control. In Germany in 1923, prices at one point doubled every few days, and people were paid twice a day and rushed to spend the money before it lost value. Wheelbarrows of banknotes were needed to buy a loaf of bread.

In Zimbabwe, in November 2008, monthly inflation reached an estimated 80 billion percent. When money stops working, people turn to barter or foreign currencies. Savings are wiped out, and the social order can be badly shaken. Hyperinflation is rare, and it usually follows war, collapse or reckless government finance. It's a stark example of how much we rely on money keeping its value, something we usually take for granted.

# Central banks and fiat money
@src Federal Reserve Act, 1913

A central bank sets short-term interest rates to influence the economy: lowering them to encourage borrowing and spending, and raising them to cool down inflation. The US Federal Reserve was created in 1913, after a series of bank panics, especially one in 1907. It has what's called a dual mandate from Congress, to keep prices stable and to promote maximum employment.

The dollar bill in your wallet has no gold behind it, and it isn't worth much as paper. It's money because the government says it is and because everyone accepts it. That's called fiat money, from the Latin for "let it be done." Until 1971, the US dollar could be exchanged for gold at a fixed rate, and when President Nixon ended that, the world moved fully to fiat currencies. Most money today is just numbers in bank accounts.

# Diversification
@src Markowitz, 1952

"Don't put all your eggs in one basket" has a mathematical backing. In 1952 Harry Markowitz showed that if you combine investments that don't all move in the same direction at the same time, you can reduce your overall risk without lowering your expected return.

He won a Nobel Prize for it in 1990. It's often called the only free lunch in finance. A portfolio of many different stocks is much less risky than any one of them, because when one falls, another may rise. The benefit has limits, since in a severe crash nearly everything falls together. But over time, spreading your money across many kinds of assets is one of the most reliable ways to protect it, and it needs no special skill.

# Index funds
@src Bogle, 1976; S&P SPIVA reports

An index fund simply buys all the stocks in a market, rather than trying to pick winners. John Bogle founded the first one available to ordinary investors in 1976, at Vanguard, and he was ridiculed for it at the time. People called it "Bogle's folly," because settling for average seemed un-American.

Reports that compare funds, such as the S&P SPIVA scorecards, regularly find that over a 15-year period, around nine in ten professional stock funds do worse than the index they're trying to beat. Part of the reason is the fees they charge. An index fund is cheap, because it doesn't need anyone to do research. It's a sobering lesson that, for most people, the simplest strategy beats most of the experts.

# The efficient market hypothesis
@src Fama, 1970
@conf mixed

Eugene Fama proposed in 1970 that stock prices already reflect all publicly available information, so it's very hard to consistently beat the market. If a stock were obviously a bargain, so many buyers would rush in that the price would rise until the bargain disappeared.

Other economists, like Robert Shiller, argued that markets can be driven by emotion and bubbles. In 2013 Fama and Shiller, with another economist, shared the Nobel Prize in economics, an indication of how both views have supporters. It's still debated. A reasonable position is that markets are hard to beat but not perfectly rational, and that anyone who claims to be able to predict them should be treated with caution.

# Tulip mania
@src Goldgar, 2007; Garber, 1990
@conf mixed

In the 1630s tulip bulbs in the Netherlands sold at extraordinary prices, and the craze is retold as the classic example of a speculative bubble that ruined thousands. The story says that a single bulb could cost as much as a house.

Modern historians, like Anne Goldgar, who looked through the records, say it was much smaller than the legend. Few people seem to have been ruined, and the evidence is thin. The story has been told so many times that the myth became the lesson. It's a nice irony that a story about people being carried away by a craze has itself been repeated, for centuries, with little checking. Whatever happened, bubbles in other times were certainly real.

# Lump sum or averaging in?
@src Vanguard, 2012

If you have a pile of money to invest, is it better to invest it all at once, or to spread it out over several months? A 2012 Vanguard study found that investing all at once did better about two-thirds of the time, simply because markets tend to rise more often than they fall.

Spreading it out makes you less likely to regret putting everything in just before a drop, so it's a trade-off. Averaging in is a way of buying peace of mind, and a modest price for it. The best choice depends on how much a sudden fall would hurt you. What matters most is not the method, but that you don't wait on the sidelines for the "perfect" moment, which no one can identify in advance.

# The time value of money and Franklin's bequest
@src Franklin, 1790

Would you rather have $100 today or $100 in a year? Most people say today, and they're right. You could invest the money and have more later, and prices will likely have risen. Economists "discount" future money to reflect this. It's the principle behind interest on loans and savings, and it's how the value of any investment or business is worked out.

When Benjamin Franklin died in 1790, he left 1,000 pounds each to the cities of Boston and Philadelphia, to be loaned out at interest and not fully used for 200 years. By 1990 the funds had grown to millions of dollars: about $5 million in Boston's fund and $2 million in Philadelphia's. It's a vivid example of the power of compound interest over a long time.

# The tragedy of the commons
@src Hardin, 1968; Ostrom, 1990

In 1968 the ecologist Garrett Hardin described a pasture open to all herders. Each herder gains by adding another cow, while the cost of overgrazing is shared by everyone, so the pasture gets destroyed. It's an image for many shared resources, such as fisheries and the atmosphere.

Elinor Ostrom, who won the 2009 Nobel Prize in economics, showed that many communities have managed shared resources well by creating their own rules. Swiss villages have shared alpine pastures for centuries, and Maine lobster fishermen have set their own limits. So the tragedy isn't inevitable. It tends to happen when there are no rules, no trust and no way to enforce an agreement, and it can often be avoided when people can talk and set limits.

# The prisoner's dilemma
@src Flood & Dresher, 1950; Axelrod, 1984

Two suspects are held separately. Each is offered a deal to betray the other. Whatever the other does, betraying seems best for you. But if both betray, they do worse than if both had stayed quiet.

Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher devised the game in 1950, and Albert Tucker told the prison story. In Robert Axelrod's computer tournaments around 1980, the winning strategy was "tit for tat": cooperate first, then copy the other player's last move. It was simple and forgiving, and it did better than much more complicated strategies. It's a powerful lesson. In repeated dealings, being nice, but not a pushover, tends to pay.

# The Nash equilibrium
@src Nash, 1950

A Nash equilibrium is a situation in which no player can do better by changing only their own choice, given what the others are doing. John Nash proved in 1950 that every game with a finite number of players and choices has at least one.

It describes stable outcomes, not necessarily good ones, as the prisoner's dilemma shows, where both players betraying is a stable result that's bad for both. Nash won the Nobel Prize in 1994, after decades of struggling with mental illness, a story told in the book and movie A Beautiful Mind. The idea is used to understand everything from auctions to arms races to traffic. It tells us where a situation might settle, and not where it ought to.

# Network effects
@src Katz & Shapiro, 1985
@conf mixed

Some things become more valuable as more people use them. A telephone is useless if you're the only one who has one, and valuable if everyone you know does. That's a network effect. Social networks, payment systems and messaging apps all depend on it.

Metcalfe's law says that the value of a network grows with the square of the number of users, but studies of real networks suggest it grows more slowly. It helps explain why one social network or payment system can come to dominate. Once most people are on one, nobody wants to leave, even if a better one is available. It also explains why new services often give away a lot at first, in order to reach the point where the network takes off.

# The market for lemons
@src Akerlof, 1970

In 1970 the economist George Akerlof wrote a paper about the used-car market. Sellers know whether their car is good or a "lemon," but buyers can't tell, so buyers are only willing to pay an average price. At that price, owners of good cars won't sell, and the market fills up with lemons.

The paper showed how a lack of information can make markets fail. He shared the 2001 Nobel Prize for it. Akerlof's paper was rejected by several journals before it was published, because reviewers thought it was trivial or wrong. The idea has been applied to insurance, jobs and loans. It's the reason why warranties, inspections and reputations are valuable. They help the buyer trust something they can't check for themselves.

# Moral hazard
@src Standard economics text

When someone else bears the cost of a risk, people tend to take more of it. A driver with full insurance may be a little less careful. A bank that expects the government to bail it out if it fails may place bigger bets.

Insurers try to limit it with deductibles and co-payments, so that the person still has something to lose. The term comes from the insurance business, where it was first used to describe policyholders who were careless because they were covered. The concept helps explain a lot of modern financial history, including the crisis of 2008. It's not that insured people are bad. It's that everyone, if they are shielded from the consequences, tends to behave a little differently.

# Externalities
@src Pigou, 1920

An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on someone who didn't choose to take part. A factory's pollution harms people downwind, a negative externality. A neighbor's beautiful garden pleases passers-by, a positive one.

In 1920 the economist Arthur Pigou argued that a tax on the polluter can make the price of a product reflect its true cost to society. Many modern carbon taxes follow the idea. Without such a tax, the price of a product leaves out the harm done to others, so people buy too much of it. Externalities help explain why markets, left alone, don't always give the best result, and why some things, like clean air, need rules or prices to be protected.

# Public goods and free riders
@src Samuelson, 1954

Some things, like street lighting, clean air or national defense, benefit everyone, whether or not they pay. That creates a free-rider problem: if you can use it either way, why pay? Voluntary contributions tend to fall short.

That's the reason economists give for why governments usually pay for such goods with taxes, to make sure that everyone chips in. Street lighting is a classic case, since you can't easily stop a passer-by from using the light. Public radio and open-source software face the same problem and rely on donations from the people who care. It's a useful way to see why a few things, which markets can't easily provide, are paid for in common.

# The Coase theorem
@src Coase, 1960

Ronald Coase argued in a 1960 paper that if people can bargain easily with each other, they'll reach an efficient arrangement no matter who starts out holding the legal rights. For example, a factory and its neighbors could settle the problem of smoke by negotiation, without a government rule.

In real life, the costs of getting everyone together and agreeing are often too high, so the theorem works best for small groups. Coase himself stressed that, and he thought the real lesson was that we should look at how costly it is to make deals. The idea has been influential in law as well as economics. It suggests that a good first step to a dispute is to ask whether the people involved could simply talk and make a deal.

# Price elasticity
@src Marshall, 1890

Price elasticity is how much demand changes when the price changes. For things people need and can't easily replace, like gasoline in the short run, demand barely drops when the price goes up. That's low elasticity.

For things with lots of substitutes, like one brand of soda, a small price rise sends buyers elsewhere. That's high elasticity. Businesses and governments use it to predict how changes in price will play out. A tax on cigarettes, for example, raises more revenue if smokers don't cut back much, and reduces smoking more if they do. It's a handy idea for understanding why some price increases cause an uproar and others pass unnoticed.

# GDP and what it misses
@src Kuznets, 1934

Gross domestic product measures the total value of goods and services a country produces. Simon Kuznets, who developed the measure in the 1930s, warned in a 1934 report to Congress that the welfare of a nation can scarcely be inferred from a measurement of national income.

GDP counts cleaning up after a disaster as growth, but it doesn't count unpaid housework, leisure or the health of the environment. A country can grow richer while its people become sicker or lonelier. That doesn't make GDP useless. It's a good measure of the size of an economy. But it's a poor measure of how well people are living, which is why researchers also look at health, education and happiness.

# The Gini coefficient
@src Gini, 1912

The Gini coefficient is a single number that measures how unequal an income distribution is, from 0, where everyone has the same, to 1, where one person has everything. The Italian statistician Corrado Gini introduced it in 1912.

It's handy for comparing countries. Scandinavian countries are around 0.25 to 0.28, and some other countries are above 0.6. It doesn't show where in the distribution the inequality lies, so two countries with the same number can look quite different. And it says nothing about how rich a country is overall. It's a rough but useful summary, and it's best used alongside other facts about how people live.

# The minimum wage debate and natural experiments
@src Card & Krueger, 1994; Nobel Prize in Economics, 2021
@conf mixed

Does raising the minimum wage cost jobs? In 1994 the economists David Card and Alan Krueger compared fast-food restaurants in New Jersey, which had raised its minimum wage, with neighboring Pennsylvania, which hadn't. They found no loss of jobs in New Jersey. Other studies have found different results, so the debate continues.

Economists can't usually run controlled experiments on whole economies. But sometimes events create something close: a lottery for the military draft, a state border where laws differ or a sudden change in a rule. These are called natural experiments. In 2021 David Card, Joshua Angrist and Guido Imbens shared the Nobel Prize in economics for developing ways to learn about cause and effect from them.

# Nudges
@src Thaler & Sunstein, 2008; Maier et al., 2022
@conf mixed

A nudge is a small change in how choices are presented that steers people toward a better decision, without forbidding anything, such as making organ donation or saving for retirement the default. Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein popularized the idea in their 2008 book.

A 2022 review found that, once publication bias is accounted for, the average effect shrinks to nearly zero, though some well-designed nudges clearly work. The lesson is that it's not enough to find a clever idea. It needs to be tested, again and again. Defaults seem to be among the strongest and most dependable, since doing nothing is easy. Other kinds of nudges, such as reminders and slogans, are less certain.

# Veblen goods
@src Veblen, 1899

Normally, when a price goes up, people buy less. But for some luxury goods, demand can rise as the price rises, because the high price is part of the appeal. In 1899 the economist Thorstein Veblen wrote about "conspicuous consumption," buying things to show status.

Designer handbags, luxury watches and certain sports cars fit. Ordinary goods don't work that way. If a luxury brand cut its prices sharply, its customers might stop buying, because the thing they were buying was exclusivity. It's one of the rare exceptions to the rule that demand falls as price rises. Veblen was a sharp critic of the wealthy of his time, and his phrase "conspicuous consumption" is still in everyday use.

# Cash flow versus profit
@src Standard accounting text

A company can show a profit on paper and still run out of cash. Profit counts sales when they're made, even if customers haven't paid yet. Cash flow is the money that's actually in the bank.

Many businesses that look healthy fail because customers pay slowly while suppliers and employees need to be paid right away. It's why experienced owners say "cash is king." A growing company can be especially vulnerable, since it has to buy materials and hire people before the money from new sales arrives. If you run a small business, or are thinking of starting one, tracking cash each week is one of the most useful habits you can build.

# Margin versus markup
@src Standard accounting text

Margin and markup are easy to mix up. Markup is profit as a percentage of what the item cost you. Margin is profit as a percentage of the price you charge.

If you buy something for $60 and sell it for $100, the profit is $40, so the markup is about 67 percent and the margin is 40 percent. Confusing the two is a common cause of pricing too low. A shop owner who wants a 40 percent margin but adds a 40 percent markup ends up with a margin of only about 29 percent. The same dollars give two different percentages, so it's worth being clear about which one you mean.

# Unit economics and the break-even point
@src Standard business text

Unit economics asks whether you make money on each individual sale, after counting everything it costs to make the product and to win the customer. Suppose it costs $50 in advertising to get a customer who brings in $30 of profit. Every sale loses $20, and growing faster just loses money faster. It's worth working out before you try to scale a business.

The break-even point is how much you need to sell to cover all your costs. You work it out by dividing your fixed costs, such as rent and salaries, by the profit you make on each unit. Below that number, you lose money, and above it, every additional sale adds to your profit. These are among the first numbers to know before starting a business or launching a product.

# Disruptive innovation
@src Christensen, 1997; Lepore, 2014
@conf mixed

In 1997 the Harvard professor Clayton Christensen argued that successful companies can be overtaken by cheaper, simpler products that start at the bottom of the market, and improve until they threaten the leaders. Steel mini-mills and small disk drives were among his examples. It became enormously influential.

Critics, including the historian Jill Lepore in a 2014 article, said that his examples were chosen after the fact, and that many companies he called disrupted did fine. So it's better as a way of describing the past than predicting the future. The idea holds something true, that leaders can be blind to threats from below, but it isn't a law. It's a story that sometimes fits and sometimes doesn't.

# The minimum viable product
@src Ries, The Lean Startup (2011)

A minimum viable product is the simplest version of a product that you can put in front of real customers to learn whether they want it. Eric Ries made the term popular in his 2011 book The Lean Startup.

The idea is to learn early, with little cost, before spending months building something nobody wants. It's about testing assumptions, not about shipping something poor. A founder might start with a simple page that describes a product and see if anyone signs up, before building any of it. The same approach works outside business. Before you commit a lot of time to a project, it's wise to find the smallest test that would tell you whether it's worth doing.

# Opportunity cost of time
@src Standard economics text

Money can be saved for later, but time can't. Every hour is spent on something, and spending it on one thing means not spending it on another. You can't earn more of it, and you can't put it in the bank.

That's why the question "Is this worth doing?" is a weak test, because nearly anything has some value. A better question is "What else could I do with this hour?" That brings the real cost into view. It's a way of seeing that saying yes to one thing is always saying no to something else, so protecting your time is the same as protecting what matters most to you.
