# Koch and the germ of tuberculosis
@src Koch, 1882; Koch, Mittheilungen, 1884

In the nineteenth century, tuberculosis was one of the leading causes of death in Europe. It was thought to be hereditary, or caused by bad air. On March 24, 1882, the German physician Robert Koch announced to a meeting in Berlin that he had found the cause. It was a rod-shaped bacterium that he had seen under the microscope, by staining tissue from diseased lungs, and grown in pure culture.

Koch developed a set of rules for proving that a germ causes a disease. The microbe should be found in every case, grown in pure culture, cause the disease when given to a healthy animal, and then be found again in the sick animal. They're known as Koch's postulates. They guided the identification of the causes of many diseases. He won the Nobel Prize in 1905. March 24 is now World Tuberculosis Day.

# No humbug
@src Massachusetts General Hospital records, 1846

Before the 1840s, surgery was performed on fully conscious patients, held down by assistants. Surgeons prized speed, and an amputation might be finished in less than a minute. On October 16, 1846, in an operating theater at Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston, a dentist named William Morton demonstrated ether, a vapor that he had his patient breathe.

The surgeon John Collins Warren removed a tumor from the neck of a printer named Gilbert Abbott. The patient felt no pain. Warren is said to have turned to the audience and said, gentlemen, this is no humbug. The news spread across the world within weeks. The room is now a museum called the Ether Dome. Surgery changed from a desperate last resort to something that could be done slowly and carefully.

# The first X-ray
@src Röntgen, 1895

In November 1895 Wilhelm Röntgen, a physics professor in Würzburg, Germany, was experimenting with a glass tube from which the air had been removed, and through which electricity passed. He noticed that a screen coated with a chemical glowed, though the tube was covered with black cardboard and the screen was across the room. Something invisible was passing through.

He called the rays X, for unknown. They passed through flesh but not through bone. On December 22 he made a photograph of his wife's hand, which showed her bones and her wedding ring. Within weeks, doctors across Europe and America were using X-rays to find broken bones and bullets. Röntgen received the first Nobel Prize in Physics in 1901, and refused to patent his discovery, so that it could be used freely.

# Insulin
@src Banting, Best, Collip & Macleod, 1921 to 1923

Before 1922, a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes was a death sentence. The only treatment was a starvation diet, which prolonged life by a few months. In 1921 a young surgeon, Frederick Banting, and a student assistant, Charles Best, at the University of Toronto, extracted a substance from the pancreas of dogs. It lowered the blood sugar of diabetic dogs.

In January 1922 they gave a purified extract to Leonard Thompson, a fourteen-year-old who was dying in a hospital in Toronto. His blood sugar fell, and he improved. Soon, hospitals were filled with children who were revived, in what was called a resurrection. Banting and the chemist James Collip agreed to sell the patent to the university for one dollar. Banting and John Macleod received the Nobel Prize in 1923, and Banting shared his prize money with Best.

# The magic bullet
@src Ehrlich & Hata, 1910

In the early 1900s, the German scientist Paul Ehrlich had a dream. He wanted to find a chemical that would kill the microbes that cause a disease, without harming the patient. He called it a magic bullet. He tested hundreds of compounds made from arsenic against the microbe that causes syphilis, a feared disease at the time with no effective treatment.

In 1909 Ehrlich and his collaborator, Sahachiro Hata, found that the six hundred and sixth compound that they tried was effective. It was marketed in 1910 as Salvarsan. It was the first modern drug designed to attack an infectious organism. It had serious side effects, and was replaced by penicillin in the 1940s. But it started the age of chemotherapy, and the idea that a disease could be targeted by a designed molecule.

# Reading the human genome
@src International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium, Nature, 2001; Nurk et al., Science, 2022

The Human Genome Project began in 1990. Its aim was to read the complete sequence of the three billion letters of human DNA. It was an international effort, led in the United States by the National Institutes of Health, with groups in Britain, Japan, France, Germany and China. A private company, Celera, led by Craig Venter, competed with it, and in 2000 the two sides jointly announced a first draft.

The project was declared complete in 2003, at a cost of about three billion dollars. It had left some gaps in regions that were hard to sequence. In 2022 a team called the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium filled them, and published the first truly complete human genome. Sequencing a genome now costs a few hundred dollars and takes a day. The project made it possible to find the genes behind many diseases.

# Henrietta Lacks and the immortal cells
@src Skloot, The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks, 2010

In January 1951 Henrietta Lacks, a thirty-year-old African American mother of five from Baltimore, went to Johns Hopkins Hospital with a tumor in her cervix. Doctors took a sample of the tumor, as they did with many patients, without telling her. She died in October of that year. The sample, though, gave scientists something that they had been trying to get for decades. Her cells grew in the laboratory and kept dividing, and didn't die.

They were called HeLa cells, and they became the first human cell line. They were used to develop the polio vaccine, and in research on cancer, viruses and genes, and have been sent into space. Her family was not told for decades. In 2013 they reached an agreement with the National Institutes of Health on access to her genome data. The story is central to the debate about consent in research.

# Copying DNA
@src Mullis, 1983; Saiki et al., Science, 1988

Imagine that you have a single molecule of DNA, from a crime scene or an ancient bone, and you need billions of copies to study it. In 1983 Kary Mullis, a chemist working for a biotech company in California, had an idea while driving along a highway at night. He realized that a repeated cycle of heating and cooling, with the right ingredients, could double the amount of DNA each time.

The method is called the polymerase chain reaction, or PCR. After thirty cycles, one molecule becomes about a billion. An enzyme that withstood the heat was needed, and it was found in a bacterium that lives in the hot springs of Yellowstone. Mullis received the Nobel Prize in 1993. PCR is used in forensic science, in diagnosing infections, and in tests for the coronavirus.

# Salt, sugar and water
@src Lancet, 1978; Nalin, Cash & Rahman, The Lancet, 1968

Cholera and other diarrheal diseases kill by dehydration. For a long time, the only treatment was a drip of fluid into a vein, which required equipment and trained staff that were not available in poor places. In the 1960s researchers, including David Nalin and Richard Cash working in Dhaka and Calcutta, found that a solution of salt and glucose, taken by mouth, was absorbed by the gut even in a person with cholera.

This oral rehydration therapy can be made at home from clean water, sugar and salt. In 1978 the British medical journal The Lancet said that it was potentially the most important medical advance of the century. It's estimated to have saved many millions of lives, mostly of children. It's a case of an advance that came not from a new drug, but from understanding a simple mechanism.

# The wooden spoon
@src Cochrane, Effectiveness and Efficiency, 1972

Archie Cochrane was a Scottish doctor who was held as a prisoner of war during the Second World War. In a camp, he ran a trial of yeast for a group of prisoners suffering from swelling, and he saw how powerful a controlled test could be. After the war, he was struck by how many medical treatments were used with no evidence.

In 1972 he published a short book, Effectiveness and Efficiency, arguing that all treatments should be tested in randomized controlled trials, and that the results should be gathered in one place and made available. He once gave a wooden spoon to the specialty that, in his view, had the least solid evidence, obstetrics. The Cochrane Collaboration, founded in 1993 and named in his honor, now produces reviews of the evidence on health care. His message was that good intentions are not enough.

# The Framingham Heart Study
@src Dawber, Meadors & Moore, American Journal of Public Health, 1951; Kannel et al., 1961

In 1948 doctors in Framingham, Massachusetts, a town about twenty miles west of Boston, invited residents to take part in a study of heart disease. They recruited more than five thousand healthy adults, and examined them every two years, measuring blood pressure, cholesterol, weight and habits, and watched who got sick.

The study has continued for more than seventy years, and now includes their children and grandchildren. In 1961 it reported that high blood pressure, high cholesterol and smoking increased the chance of heart disease, and the term risk factor was coined. Later work added diabetes, obesity and physical inactivity. Before Framingham, heart disease was often thought to be an inevitable part of aging. The study showed that much of it can be prevented.

# Smoking and lung cancer
@src Doll & Hill, British Medical Journal, 1950 and 1954; Doll et al., 2004

In the first half of the twentieth century, lung cancer was becoming more common, and smoking was very widespread. In 1950 two British researchers, Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill, compared the habits of patients with lung cancer and those without, and found that the cancer patients were much more likely to have smoked. Many experts were skeptical, and thought that something else might be the cause.

In 1951 they began following more than thirty-four thousand British doctors, and in 1954 they reported that smokers died of lung cancer far more often than non-smokers. The doctors' study went on for fifty years. In 2004 the results showed that long-term smokers lost about ten years of life on average, and that stopping, even in middle age, regained much of it. It was a landmark in showing how epidemiology can identify a cause.

# The genes that keep time
@src Konopka & Benzer, PNAS, 1971; Hall, Rosbash & Young, Nobel Prize, 2017

Almost all living things have an internal clock that runs on a cycle of about twenty-four hours. In 1971 Ronald Konopka and Seymour Benzer at Caltech looked for the cause. They treated fruit flies with chemicals that caused mutations, and found flies with clocks that were too fast, too slow, or absent. The mutations were in a single gene, which they called period.

In the 1980s and 1990s Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young cloned the gene, and showed how it works. The cell makes a protein that builds up at night, and then switches off its own gene. As the protein breaks down, the cycle starts again. They received the Nobel Prize in 2017. The clock genes are found in humans too, and help to explain jet lag, shift work and why some people are early birds.

# The caps on our chromosomes
@src Blackburn, Greider & Szostak, Nobel Prize, 2009

Each of our chromosomes has a protective cap at both ends, like the plastic tip of a shoelace. These caps are called telomeres. Every time that a cell divides, a little of the telomere is lost. When it becomes too short, the cell stops dividing or dies. In this way, telomeres act as a kind of counter for the number of divisions a cell has made.

Elizabeth Blackburn and Jack Szostak discovered how telomeres are protected, and in 1984 Blackburn and her student Carol Greider found an enzyme, telomerase, that builds them up again. Cancer cells use telomerase to divide without limit. The three shared the Nobel Prize in 2009. Telomere length has been linked to aging and stress, but the claims of products that promise to lengthen telomeres are not well supported.

# How cells recycle themselves
@src Ohsumi, Nobel Prize, 2016

Cells have a system for breaking down and recycling their own parts. Damaged proteins and worn-out structures are wrapped in a membrane, and delivered to a compartment that digests them. The process is called autophagy, from Greek words for self-eating. It was observed in the 1960s, but no one knew how it worked.

In the early 1990s Yoshinori Ohsumi, a Japanese biologist, studied it in yeast. He starved the cells, which forced them to recycle, and looked for the genes that were needed. He found about fifteen of them. Similar genes exist in humans. Faults in autophagy are linked to cancer, to Parkinson's disease and to diabetes. Ohsumi received the Nobel Prize in 2016. Cells, like households, need to take out the trash as well as to bring things in.

# How mRNA vaccines came to be
@src Karikó & Weissman, Immunity, 2005; Nobel Prize, 2023

Messenger RNA carries the instructions from a gene to the machinery that builds a protein. The idea of using it as a medicine, by giving cells the instructions to make a protein that trains the immune system, is old. The problem was that the body treated the injected RNA as an invader, and attacked it, causing a strong inflammatory reaction.

Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman worked on the problem for years at the University of Pennsylvania. Karikó struggled to get grants, and was at one point demoted. In 2005 they reported that changing one of the building blocks of the RNA hid it from the immune system, and allowed it to make more protein. This discovery was used in the vaccines against COVID-19, which were developed and used in 2020 and 2021. They received the Nobel Prize in 2023.
