# The experiment that came in a dream
@src Loewi, An autobiographic sketch, 1960; Nobel Prize, 1936

For a long time scientists argued about how a nerve passes its signal on to the next cell, or to a muscle. Some thought that it was an electrical spark, and others that it was a chemical. By his own account, in 1921 the Austrian pharmacologist Otto Loewi woke in the night with an idea for an experiment that could decide it, scribbled a few notes, and fell asleep again. In the morning he could not read them, and the idea was gone. The next night he dreamed it again, and this time he went straight to his laboratory.

He took two frog hearts, and kept the nerve on one of them. When he stimulated the nerve, the heart slowed. He then took the fluid that had bathed that heart and poured it over the second heart, which also slowed. Something chemical had passed from one to the other. He called it vagus substance, and it was later identified as acetylcholine. In 1936 Loewi shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Henry Dale.

# A Nobel Prize for the lobotomy
@src Nobel Prize, 1949; Freeman and Watts, 1936

In 1935 the Portuguese neurologist Egas Moniz began to cut the connections between the front of the brain and the rest, to treat severe mental illness. He called it leucotomy. In 1936 the American doctors Walter Freeman and James Watts did the first such operation in the United States, and called it a lobotomy. In 1949 Moniz shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The committee cited the value of the operation in certain psychoses.

In 1946 Freeman began to use a quicker method, which went through the eye socket, and which he took around the country. About 50,000 people had lobotomies in the United States, most in the late 1940s and early 1950s. Some patients calmed. Many were left dull, passive or disabled, such as Rosemary Kennedy, who was operated on in 1941. The practice faded in the 1950s, as drugs for psychosis arrived. It is a warning about a treatment that was widely praised, without proper trials.

# Finding the engram
@src Lashley, 1950; Liu et al., Nature, 2012

Where in the brain is a memory stored? In the 1920s and 1930s the psychologist Karl Lashley trained rats to run mazes, and then cut out different parts of their cortex. The more cortex he removed, the worse the rats did, but it did not seem to matter which part. He concluded that memories are spread across the cortex, and spent years looking for the engram, which is the physical trace of a memory, without finding it.

In 2012 Xu Liu, Steve Ramirez and Susumu Tonegawa tried a new tool. In mice, they marked the cells in a part of the hippocampus that were active while the mice learned to fear a box, and made those cells sensitive to light. Later, in a different box, they switched on the marked cells with a beam of light. The mice froze, as if they were afraid. A small, specific set of cells could bring back a memory. It is now thought that memories are stored by groups of cells that can be located.

# The brain map that redrew itself
@src Merzenich et al., Neuroscience, 1983; Journal of Comparative Neurology, 1984

The surface of the brain has a map of the body. Each patch of the sensory cortex receives touch from one part of the skin, and for the hand, the fingers are laid out in order. For a long time it was thought that this map was fixed in adults. In the early 1980s Michael Merzenich and his colleagues tested that in monkeys. They cut a nerve that brings touch from part of the hand, and later recorded from the cortex.

The patch of cortex that had belonged to the cut nerve did not go silent. Within weeks and months it was taken over by touch from the neighboring skin, and the new map was still orderly. When they removed a finger, the neighboring fingers spread into the empty territory in a similar way. The adult brain can redraw its maps after an injury. The finding opened the study of how experience and damage change the brain, and of recovery after strokes.

# The disease of the funeral feast
@src Gajdusek, Nobel Prize lecture, 1976; Prusiner, 1982

In the 1950s a fatal disease was killing many of the Fore people, in the highlands of Papua New Guinea. Its name was kuru, which means shaking. Victims lost control of their movements, and died within a year. Many thought that it was inherited. The American doctor Carleton Gajdusek went to the region and, with local people, studied it. He found that it spread through funeral rites, in which relatives handled the bodies of the dead and, in some cases, ate them.

Gajdusek showed in the 1960s that kuru could be passed to chimpanzees, by injecting them with brain tissue, and that it was caused by something that took years to act. He shared the Nobel Prize in 1976. Later Stanley Prusiner proposed that such diseases are caused by a misfolded protein, which he called a prion, and he received the Nobel Prize in 1997. Kuru faded after the rites were given up. It belongs to a family of diseases that includes scrapie in sheep and mad cow disease.

# Seeing things that aren't there, in a clear mind
@series brain
@src Bonnet, 1700s; reviews of studies of people with low vision

Some people who lose their sight begin to see things that are not there. They may see faces, patterns, people in old-fashioned clothes, or animals. They are not confused, and they know that the pictures are not real. The condition is called Charles Bonnet syndrome, after a Swiss naturalist who wrote in the 1700s about his grandfather, who lost his sight and saw such visions. Many patients keep quiet about it, because they fear that others will think that they are mad.

Estimates of how common it is vary widely, from under one percent to about a third of people with low vision, depending on the study. One analysis of the studies found that about one in five people over forty with low vision had it. The leading idea is that the visual part of the brain, deprived of input, begins to make images of its own. It helps people a great deal to be told what the condition is, and that it is not a sign of madness. It shows that what we see is partly something that the brain builds.

# The woman who could not forget
@conf mixed
@src Parker, Cahill & McGaugh, Neurocase, 2006; LePort et al., 2012

In 2006 the neuroscientists Elizabeth Parker, Larry Cahill and James McGaugh reported a case of a woman whom they called AJ. She could recall an extraordinary number of the days of her life, with the date, the weather and what she had done. The memories came without effort, and she could not turn them off. They were, she said, nonstop, uncontrollable and automatic. She did not use any tricks to remember, as memory champions do.

The scientists named it hyperthymestic syndrome, and it is now called highly superior autobiographical memory. In 2012 Aurora LePort and her colleagues studied eleven people with the ability. They tested them on their memories of their own lives, and on standard tests, and scanned their brains. It is very rare. It shows that the memory for a life can be separate from other kinds of memory, and that remembering everything may be a burden.

# Neurons that count
@src Nieder, Freedman & Miller, Science, 2002

Can a single nerve cell be said to represent the number three? In 2002 Andreas Nieder, David Freedman and Earl Miller trained monkeys to judge whether two successive displays had the same number of dots. The numbers ran from one to five. They changed the size and position of the dots from trial to trial, so that the monkeys could not rely on those cues. Then they recorded the activity of 352 neurons in the prefrontal cortex.

Many neurons had a favorite number. A neuron that liked three fired most for three dots, a little less for two or four, and still less for one or five. The tuning was sharper for small numbers than for large ones, which fits the way people and animals find it easier to tell two from three than eight from nine. The finding suggests that a sense of number is built into the brain. It is part of the basis from which human arithmetic grew.

# Brodmann's map
@src Brodmann, Vergleichende Lokalisationslehre der Grosshirnrinde, 1909

In the early 1900s, the German anatomist Korbinian Brodmann looked at slices of human and animal brains under the microscope. He stained them to show the cells, and noticed that the cortex was not uniform. In some places the layers of cells were thick, and in others thin, and the types of cells differed. He drew lines around the regions that looked alike.

In 1909 he published a map with 52 areas of the human cortex, each with a number. The numbers are still used. Area 17 is the primary visual cortex, area 4 is the primary motor cortex, and areas 44 and 45 are a part of Broca's area. He did not know what each region did, since he had only looked at the cells. Later work with injuries and scans showed that many of his regions do have distinct jobs. His map shows that structure points to function.

# The dye that stopped at the brain
@src Ehrlich, 1885; Goldmann, 1913

In the 1880s the German scientist Paul Ehrlich injected a dye into the blood of animals. The dye stained every organ, except one. The brain stayed pale. Ehrlich thought that the brain simply did not take up the dye. Some years later his student Edwin Goldmann tried the opposite. He injected the dye into the fluid that surrounds the brain and spinal cord. Now the brain was stained, but the rest of the body was not.

The two results together showed that there is a barrier between the blood and the brain. We now know that it is made of tightly joined cells in the walls of the brain's blood vessels. It keeps out many germs and toxins, and it controls what enters. It also makes it hard to get drugs into the brain, which is a difficulty for treating many diseases of the brain. Much of the work in designing such drugs is about finding a way across.
