# Socrates
@src Plato, Apology; Xenophon, Memorabilia

Socrates lived in Athens in the fifth century BC and wrote nothing. What we know of him comes from his students, above all Plato, and from the historian Xenophon. He spent his days in the marketplace asking people what they meant by words such as justice, courage and virtue. His questions showed that people who were sure that they knew often could not explain.

In 399 BC he was put on trial on charges of not honoring the city's gods and of corrupting the young. He was found guilty and sentenced to death by drinking hemlock. In Plato's account, he said that the unexamined life is not worth living. His method of patient questioning is still used in teaching, in law and in therapy.

# Plato
@src Plato, Republic; Diogenes Laërtius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers

Plato was a student of Socrates, and after his teacher's death, he traveled and then returned to Athens. Around 387 BC he founded the Academy, a school just outside the city. It lasted for centuries, and it's the origin of the word academic. His most famous student there was Aristotle.

Plato wrote dialogues, in which Socrates and others argue about big questions. In the Republic, he describes an ideal city, and the allegory of the cave. In it, prisoners who have only seen shadows on a wall mistake them for reality, until one is freed and sees the sun. Plato's point was that what we take for the real world may be only a shadow of something truer, and that education is a turning of the mind toward the light.

# Aristotle
@src Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Politics, History of Animals

Aristotle studied at Plato's Academy for about twenty years, and later tutored the young Alexander the Great. In 335 BC he founded his own school in Athens, the Lyceum. He wrote on logic, physics, biology, ethics, politics and poetry, and what survives of his work fills many volumes.

He was a careful observer. He dissected animals and described hundreds of species, including the way a shark's embryo is attached by a kind of placenta, which was not confirmed for centuries. He was also wrong about many things, such as the idea that heavier objects fall faster, and that Earth is the center of the universe. His authority was so great that his errors held on for almost two thousand years. It is a lesson in how a great mind can be right and wrong at once.

# Confucius
@src The Analects

Confucius was a teacher in China who lived from about 551 to 479 BC. He came from a modest family, and he tried to get a post as an adviser to the rulers of his time, but with little success. He spent his later years teaching a group of students. After his death, they recorded his sayings in a book called the Analects.

His teaching focused on how people should behave toward one another. He stressed respect for family, honesty, and learning as a lifelong task. He thought that a good ruler governs by example, and not by force. His ideas shaped Chinese education and government for over two thousand years, including the system of examinations for public office. He also gave the rule of not doing to others what you would not want done to yourself.

# Epictetus
@src Epictetus, Discourses, recorded by Arrian

Epictetus was born a slave in the Roman Empire around the year 50 AD, in what is now Turkey. His owner allowed him to study philosophy, and after he was freed, he became a teacher in Rome and later in Greece. He wrote nothing himself. His student Arrian wrote down his lectures, which survive as the Discourses and a short handbook.

His central idea is that some things are up to us and others are not. Our opinions, desires and actions are in our power. The body, property, reputation and the actions of others are not. Peace comes from focusing on the first and accepting the second. The idea is the root of modern cognitive therapy. Several of its founders cited a line of his, that people are disturbed not by things, but by the views that they take of them.

# Marcus Aurelius
@src Marcus Aurelius, Meditations

Marcus Aurelius was emperor of Rome from 161 to 180 AD, in a time of war, plague and rebellion. He was also a Stoic philosopher. During his campaigns on the northern frontier, he wrote notes to himself in Greek, in the evenings. They were never meant to be published. They survive as the Meditations.

The notes are reminders. Take the long view, since all things pass. Do your duty, even when you'd prefer to stay in bed. Do not be angry at people who are acting out of ignorance. He wrote about the shortness of life and the need to act with fairness. The book has been read for almost two thousand years, and it is notable that it was written by the most powerful man of his time, who was working on his own character.

# Hippocrates
@src Hippocratic Corpus

Hippocrates was a Greek physician who lived on the island of Kos in the fifth century BC. Little is known about his life. He's remembered because the collection of medical writings attributed to him, the Hippocratic Corpus, takes a new approach. It says that diseases have natural causes, and not divine ones.

The physicians in this tradition observed patients closely, kept records of the course of an illness, and valued rest, diet and fresh air. They wrote that the physician should help, or at least do no harm. The famous oath was probably not written by Hippocrates, and the writings are the work of many authors. But the idea that medicine should be based on observation, and on the patient's wellbeing, has lasted. The title father of medicine is a tribute to that change in thinking.

# Galen
@src Galen, On the Usefulness of the Parts

Galen was a Greek physician who was born in Pergamon in about 129 AD. He began as a doctor to gladiators, where he saw many wounds and learned anatomy. Later he went to Rome, where he treated emperors. He wrote hundreds of works, and he dissected animals, such as pigs and monkeys, since human dissection was forbidden.

His descriptions of the human body were therefore partly wrong, since he applied what he saw in animals to people. But his authority was enormous. For over one thousand years, in Europe and the Islamic world, his books were the standard text in medicine. It took the careful dissections of Andreas Vesalius in 1543 to show where Galen had been mistaken. His example shows how a good method with the wrong material can lead to lasting errors.

# Ptolemy
@src Ptolemy, Almagest

Ptolemy was a Greek astronomer who worked in Alexandria, in Egypt, in the second century AD. His great book, the Almagest, set out a mathematical model of the heavens, with the Earth at the center. The Sun, Moon and planets moved on circles within circles, called epicycles, which explained the odd loops that planets seem to make against the stars.

The model worked well. It could predict the positions of the planets with good accuracy, and so it was used for about fourteen hundred years, in Europe and the Islamic world. Ptolemy also wrote a book on geography, which gave the coordinates of thousands of places. When Copernicus proposed a Sun-centered model in 1543, he kept many of Ptolemy's methods. A wrong theory can still be a good tool for prediction.

# Al-Razi
@src Al-Razi, A Treatise on Smallpox and Measles

Al-Razi, known in Europe as Rhazes, was a Persian physician who lived from about 865 to 925. He worked in Rayy, now near Tehran, and in Baghdad, where he led a hospital. He wrote more than a hundred books on medicine, philosophy and alchemy.

He is best known for his treatise on smallpox and measles, in which he was the first to describe the two diseases as separate. He wrote about how to tell them apart by the rash and the fever, and how to treat the patient. He also wrote a book of doubts about Galen, arguing that a physician should not accept authority without checking it against experience. He also wrote on the ethics of medicine, and on the need to treat the poor as carefully as the rich.

# Ibn Khaldun
@src Ibn Khaldun, Muqaddimah, 1377

Ibn Khaldun was born in Tunis in 1332. He worked as an adviser and judge to rulers across North Africa and Spain, and his life was full of political danger. In 1375 he withdrew to a fortress in Algeria to write. There, over a few months, he produced the introduction to a history of the world, called the Muqaddimah.

In it he tried to explain why states rise and fall. He argued that societies have laws that can be studied, like nature. A group with strong solidarity, which he called asabiyyah, conquers a settled civilization. But after a few generations, comfort and luxury weaken it, and a new group takes over. Many historians regard him as an early founder of sociology, and of economics. He saw history as something that can be understood by looking at causes.

# Fibonacci
@src Fibonacci, Liber Abaci, 1202

Leonardo of Pisa, nicknamed Fibonacci, was born in about 1170 in Italy. As a boy, he traveled with his father, a merchant, to North Africa, where he learned the Hindu-Arabic system of numerals. He saw that it was much easier to calculate with than Roman numerals.

In 1202 he wrote a book, Liber Abaci, that explained the new system to Europe. It showed how to write the numbers from zero to nine, how to do arithmetic, and how to use them for business, for example in currency exchange and interest. The book included a puzzle about the breeding of rabbits, which gives a sequence of numbers now named after him. Its influence was slow but huge. Without the system that he introduced, modern mathematics and accounting would be much harder.

# Montaigne
@src Montaigne, Essays, 1580

Michel de Montaigne was a French nobleman and lawyer who lived from 1533 to 1592. At thirty-eight, he retired to the tower of his family's château near Bordeaux, with a library, to think and write. He called his short pieces essais, meaning attempts or trials, and so he gave the essay its name.

The essays are about everything, such as friendship, death, cruelty, books, and the way that we are all different from one day to the next. Montaigne wrote honestly about himself, including his faults and his bad memory. His motto was Que sais-je? What do I know? He doubted that we can be certain about much. Writing in this way, from experience, with an open mind, was new, and it influenced writers from Shakespeare to modern essayists.

# Francis Bacon
@src Bacon, Novum Organum, 1620

Francis Bacon was an English lawyer, statesman and philosopher who lived from 1561 to 1626. He argued that knowledge should be built by careful observation and experiment, and not by relying on ancient authorities. In his book Novum Organum, he set out a new way of going about science, collecting facts, and drawing general rules from them.

He also listed the errors that mislead the mind, which he called idols. The idols of the tribe are mistakes that are natural to all of us, such as seeing more order than there is. The idols of the cave come from our own upbringing. The idols of the marketplace come from careless words, and the idols of the theater from fashionable theories. It was an early list of cognitive biases, written almost four hundred years ago.

# Descartes
@src Descartes, Discourse on Method, 1637

René Descartes was a French philosopher and mathematician who lived from 1596 to 1650. He wanted to find a basis for knowledge that could not be doubted. So he decided to doubt everything that he could. The senses can deceive us, and he might even be dreaming. But he could not doubt that he was doubting, and so thinking. This became the line, I think, therefore I am.

He also changed mathematics. By describing points on a plane with two numbers, he linked algebra and geometry, a system now known as Cartesian coordinates. He argued that mind and body are different kinds of thing, which led to a problem that still puzzles people, of how the two interact. Descartes lived in the Netherlands for much of his life, and died in Sweden, where he had been invited to tutor Queen Christina, in the cold of early mornings.

# Blaise Pascal
@src Pascal, Pensées, 1670

Blaise Pascal was born in France in 1623, and was a prodigy. As a teenager, he built one of the first mechanical calculators, to help his father, a tax official, with his sums. In 1654 he exchanged letters with Pierre de Fermat about a problem of dividing the stakes in an unfinished game of chance. The correspondence is regarded as the beginning of probability theory.

He also made discoveries in physics, showing that pressure in a fluid is transmitted equally in all directions. Later, he turned to religion, and wrote the Pensées, a collection of thoughts that includes his wager. It argues that believing in God is a good bet, since the possible gain is infinite and the cost small. Pascal was often ill, and died in 1662, aged thirty-nine.

# David Hume
@src Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, 1739

David Hume was a Scottish philosopher who lived from 1711 to 1776. He wrote his first great work, A Treatise of Human Nature, in his twenties, and it was ignored. He said that it fell dead-born from the press. He rewrote it, and later became famous, mainly as a historian.

Hume argued that all our ideas come from experience. He noticed that we never see one event cause another. We only see that one follows the other, again and again, and we get used to expecting it. Our belief in causes, he said, is a habit of the mind. He also pointed out that you cannot derive what ought to be from what is. The problem of induction, as it is called, still troubles philosophers, and the is-ought gap is a staple of ethics.

# Isaac Newton
@src Newton, Principia, 1687

Isaac Newton was born in England in 1642, in a farming family. He studied at Cambridge, and in the plague years of 1665 and 1666, when the university closed, he worked at home on mathematics, optics and gravity. In 1687 he published the Principia, which sets out the laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation.

The book showed that the same force that makes an apple fall also holds the Moon in orbit. It explained the tides and the paths of comets. Newton also invented calculus, and showed that white light is a mixture of colors. He was fierce in disputes, with Robert Hooke and with Gottfried Leibniz about calculus. In his later life, he ran the Royal Mint, and he spent a lot of time on alchemy and theology. He is buried in Westminster Abbey.

# Gottfried Leibniz
@src Leibniz, Nova Methodus, 1684

Gottfried Leibniz was a German mathematician and philosopher who lived from 1646 to 1716. He worked for noble families as a diplomat, a librarian and a lawyer, and he wrote on many subjects. In 1684 he published his version of calculus, independently of Newton, and the notation he used is the one that we use today, including the integral sign.

He also worked on the binary system of numbers, using only zero and one. He saw that it could be used to represent all numbers, and he built a calculating machine that could multiply and divide. A bitter quarrel over who had invented calculus first divided British and continental mathematicians for generations. Most historians now agree that each man found it on his own. Leibniz's binary idea waited about two and a half centuries to find its use in computers.

# Leonhard Euler
@src Euler, Introductio in analysin infinitorum, 1748

Leonhard Euler was a Swiss mathematician who lived from 1707 to 1783. He is regarded as one of the most productive mathematicians in history. His collected works fill dozens of large volumes, and cover number theory, geometry, calculus, physics and astronomy. He worked in Saint Petersburg and in Berlin.

He lost the sight of one eye in 1738, and was nearly blind by 1771. He kept working by dictating to assistants, and by doing the calculations in his head. Many of the symbols that we use were popularized by him, including the letter e for the base of natural logarithms, i for the square root of minus one, and the use of f(x) for a function. A story says that he could calculate faster than people could write down. His work in 1735 on the bridges of Königsberg was the start of graph theory.

# Antoine Lavoisier
@src Lavoisier, Traité élémentaire de chimie, 1789

Antoine Lavoisier was a French chemist who lived from 1743 to 1794. He used careful measurements with a balance, and not just observations, and changed chemistry into a quantitative science. He showed that when a substance burns, it combines with a part of the air, which he named oxygen. This replaced the earlier theory that burning released an element called phlogiston.

He also showed that matter is conserved in chemical reactions, and that water is made of hydrogen and oxygen. He wrote the first modern textbook of chemistry, and helped to design the system of naming chemicals that we use. During the French Revolution, he was arrested because he had been a tax collector, and guillotined in 1794. The mathematician Lagrange is said to have remarked that it took a moment to cut off his head, and a century to grow another like it.

# Charles Babbage
@src Babbage, Passages from the Life of a Philosopher, 1864

Charles Babbage was an English mathematician who lived from 1791 to 1871. He was annoyed by the mistakes in printed tables of numbers used for navigation, which were made by human calculators. In the 1820s he designed the Difference Engine, a mechanical device for calculating and printing tables, and received government money to build it.

The project stalled, in part because of disputes with his engineer. Meanwhile, Babbage had a bigger idea, the Analytical Engine, a machine that could be programmed using punched cards, with a store for numbers and a unit for calculating. It was never built in his lifetime. Ada Lovelace wrote notes about it that included what is considered an early program. In 1991 the Science Museum in London built a Difference Engine from his plans, and it worked.

# Paul Erdős
@src Hoffman, The Man Who Loved Only Numbers, 1998

Paul Erdős was a Hungarian mathematician who lived from 1913 to 1996. He had no home and no job in the usual sense. He traveled the world with two suitcases, staying with colleagues, and arriving with the words, my brain is open. He worked on problems with them, and moved on a few days later, leaving them with new ideas.

He wrote around fifteen hundred papers, more than almost any other mathematician, and with hundreds of different co-authors. Mathematicians define an Erdős number, which counts how many steps of co-authorship separate them from him. Someone who wrote a paper with Erdős has a number of one. He used a vocabulary of his own, calling children epsilons, and God the Supreme Fascist, who keeps the best proofs in a book.

# William James
@src James, The Principles of Psychology, 1890

William James was an American philosopher and psychologist who lived from 1842 to 1910. He trained as a doctor, but he suffered from depression and doubts about what to do with his life, and he found his way into teaching at Harvard. In 1890 he published The Principles of Psychology, after twelve years of work. It was a huge book, and it set the agenda for the field.

James described consciousness as a stream, always changing and continuous, and not a chain of separate ideas. He wrote about attention, habit, emotion and the self. He argued that habit is a flywheel of society, and that we should make our nervous system an ally. He also proposed that emotion follows the body's reaction, so that we are afraid because we run. He is the father of American psychology.

# Wilhelm Wundt
@src Boring, A History of Experimental Psychology, 1929

Wilhelm Wundt was a German physician and philosopher who lived from 1832 to 1920. In 1879 he set up a laboratory at the University of Leipzig, to study the mind by experiment. That year is usually given as the founding of psychology as a separate science.

His method was introspection, with trained observers reporting on their experiences when something was done to them, such as the sound of a metronome. He also measured reaction times. Students came from all over the world, and took his methods home, including many of the first American psychologists. Later psychologists criticized introspection as unreliable, and the field moved toward behavior. But Wundt's insistence that the mind could be studied scientifically was a turning point.

# John Bowlby
@src Bowlby, Attachment and Loss, 1969

John Bowlby was a British psychiatrist who lived from 1907 to 1990. He worked in a clinic for troubled children, and noticed that many had suffered early separations from their mothers. During the Second World War, and after, he studied children who had been separated from their families, in hospitals and orphanages.

In 1951 the World Health Organization asked him to write a report on the mental health of homeless children. He argued that infants need a warm, continuous relationship with a caregiver. He drew on ethology, the study of animal behavior, and suggested that attachment is an evolved system for survival. His ideas were first rejected by psychoanalysts. Mary Ainsworth's work with infants then gave them an empirical base, and attachment theory is now a major influence.

# Lev Vygotsky
@src Vygotsky, Mind in Society, 1978

Lev Vygotsky was a Russian psychologist who died of tuberculosis in 1934 at the age of thirty-seven. In a career of only about ten years, he wrote on language, play and learning. His work was banned for a time in the Soviet Union, and wasn't widely known in the West until it was translated in the 1960s and 70s.

He argued that thinking develops through social interaction. A child learns first with the help of other people, and then makes the skills their own. Language is a tool, first used to communicate with others, and then used silently, as inner speech, to guide thought. Children talk to themselves as they play, and he thought that this private speech helps them to plan. His idea of the zone of proximal development has had a deep influence on teaching.

# Kurt Lewin
@src Lewin, Field Theory in Social Science, 1951

Kurt Lewin was a German-American psychologist who lived from 1890 to 1947. He was Jewish, and left Germany in 1933 when the Nazis came to power, and eventually worked at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. He is considered the founder of social psychology. He argued that behavior depends on the person and on the environment together.

In a famous study, he and his colleagues organized boys' clubs under adult leaders who were authoritarian, democratic or permissive. They found that the democratic groups were the most productive and happy, while the authoritarian leaders produced either aggression or apathy. He also started action research, in which people study problems in order to solve them. He is credited with the saying that there is nothing so practical as a good theory.

# Carl Rogers
@src Rogers, On Becoming a Person, 1961

Carl Rogers was an American psychologist who lived from 1902 to 1987. He was dissatisfied with therapies in which the therapist told the client what was wrong. He developed the idea that the client has the capacity to grow, and that the therapist's job is to create the right conditions. He called it person-centered therapy.

The conditions, according to Rogers, are three. The therapist must be genuine, must accept the client without judgment, which he called unconditional positive regard, and must listen with empathy. He was also one of the first to record therapy sessions, and to study them scientifically. Research has found that the quality of the relationship between therapist and client predicts the outcome. His approach influenced counseling, education and the way that we think about good listening.

# Viktor Frankl
@src Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning, 1946

Viktor Frankl was an Austrian psychiatrist who lived from 1905 to 1997. In 1942 he and his family were sent to a Nazi ghetto, and then to concentration camps, including Auschwitz. His wife, his parents and his brother died. He survived, and in 1946 he wrote a book in nine days about what he had seen, called Man's Search for Meaning.

In it he argued that the main drive in humans is not pleasure or power, but meaning. He observed that prisoners who had a purpose, such as a person to see again or a task to finish, were more likely to endure. He developed a school of therapy, called logotherapy, that helps people find meaning. He often quoted Nietzsche, that he who has a why to live can bear almost any how. The book has sold millions of copies.

# Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky
@src Lewis, The Undoing Project, 2016

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky met at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem in the late 1960s. They were very different. Kahneman was an anxious, doubting man, and Tversky was a confident, quick-witted one. They became close friends, and for about a decade worked together almost every day, talking for hours, sometimes laughing so hard that people outside their office wondered what was going on.

Together they produced the research on heuristics and biases, and on prospect theory, that changed psychology and economics. They decided who would be listed first on each paper by turns. Tversky died of cancer in 1996, at fifty-nine. In 2002 Kahneman won the Nobel Prize in Economics, and said that Tversky would have shared it if he had lived, since the Nobel is not given after death.

# Santiago Ramón y Cajal
@src Ramón y Cajal, Recollections of My Life, 1937

Santiago Ramón y Cajal was a Spanish scientist who lived from 1852 to 1934. As a boy he wanted to be an artist, and his father, a doctor, pushed him toward medicine. He combined the two. Using a stain invented by the Italian Camillo Golgi, which colors a few nerve cells black, he looked at brain tissue under a microscope, and drew what he saw.

His drawings showed that the nervous system is made of separate cells, the neurons, which are not fused into a continuous net, as Golgi believed. This was the neuron doctrine. They shared the Nobel Prize in 1906, and made speeches that disagreed with each other. Cajal's drawings of neurons are so accurate and beautiful that they are still used in textbooks. He is regarded as the father of modern neuroscience.

# Charles Sherrington
@src Sherrington, The Integrative Action of the Nervous System, 1906

Charles Sherrington was a British physiologist who lived from 1857 to 1952. He studied reflexes, such as the way a leg jerks when the knee is tapped. He found that nerve cells do not simply pass a signal along, but add up the messages that arrive, and so decide whether to fire.

In 1897 he coined the word synapse, from a Greek word meaning to clasp, for the junction where one nerve cell communicates with another. At the time, no one could see it. He deduced that it must exist from the speed and delay of the reflexes that he measured. Electron microscopes confirmed it decades later. He shared the Nobel Prize in 1932, and wrote a book, Man on His Nature, which asks how the brain relates to the mind.

# Eric Kandel
@src Kandel, In Search of Memory, 2006

Eric Kandel was born in Vienna in 1929. In 1938, when he was nine, the Nazis took over Austria, and his family had to flee, and eventually reached New York. He studied history and literature, and then medicine. He was interested in how memory works, and decided to look for it in an animal with a very simple nervous system, a large sea slug called Aplysia.

The slug has only about twenty thousand neurons, some of them large enough to see. Kandel showed that learning, such as becoming used to a touch, changes the strength of the connections between particular neurons. Short-term memory involves changes to existing proteins, and long-term memory requires new proteins to be made, and new connections to grow. He shared the Nobel Prize in 2000. His work showed that memory has a physical basis in the synapse.

# Rita Levi-Montalcini
@src Levi-Montalcini, In Praise of Imperfection, 1988

Rita Levi-Montalcini was an Italian scientist who lived to be one hundred and three years old. She was born in 1909 in Turin. Her father didn't want her to go to university, but she persuaded him, and studied medicine. When the racial laws of Mussolini's government in 1938 barred Jewish people from academic jobs, she set up a small laboratory in her bedroom.

There, with a few simple tools, she studied the development of nerve fibers in chick embryos. After the war, she went to the United States, and in the early 1950s discovered nerve growth factor, a protein that helps nerve cells to grow and survive. It was the first of a family of growth factors. She shared the Nobel Prize in 1986. She continued to work in her laboratory into her nineties.

# Oliver Sacks
@src Sacks, The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat, 1985

Oliver Sacks was a British neurologist who lived from 1933 to 2015, and who spent most of his career in New York. He was known for writing about his patients in a way that treated them as people with a story, and not just as cases. His books include Awakenings, about patients who had been frozen for decades by a sleeping sickness and were briefly roused by a drug.

His best-known book, The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat, collected case histories of people with unusual disorders of the brain. One was a musician who could no longer recognize faces, but could identify objects by their shape. Another was a man who lost his sense of the position of his own body. The stories made millions of readers curious about how the brain works, and how it can fail.

# Noam Chomsky
@src Chomsky, Syntactic Structures, 1957

Noam Chomsky is an American linguist, born in 1928. In 1957 he published a short book, Syntactic Structures, that changed the study of language. He argued that the grammar of a language is a system of rules that can produce an unlimited number of sentences, and that a theory of language should explain how this is done.

In 1959 he wrote a famous review of B. F. Skinner's book on verbal behavior. He argued that children can't learn language by imitation and reward alone, because they produce sentences that they've never heard, and because the language that they hear is incomplete. He proposed that humans are born with a capacity for language. The review helped to end the dominance of behaviorism, and it shifted psychology toward the mind.
