# The small word that shows who is in charge
@src Kacewicz et al., Journal of Language and Social Psychology, 2014

Who is the boss in a conversation? Look at the pronouns. In 2014 Ewa Kacewicz, James Pennebaker and their colleagues reported five studies of how rank shows in the use of small words, such as I, we and you, by people in small groups. They compared people with higher and lower status, whether it was given by their role or arose in the group.

The people with higher status used fewer first-person singular pronouns, such as I, me and my, and more of the others, such as we and you. Pennebaker suggested that people of lower status are more focused on themselves, and those higher up look outward. It is a case in which little words that we hardly notice carry information. It is a tendency in groups, and not a way to judge the rank of one person from a sentence.

# Words that give away a lie
@conf mixed
@src Newman, Pennebaker, Berry & Richards, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2003

Can a computer tell a lie from the words alone? In 2003 Matthew Newman, James Pennebaker and their colleagues asked people to tell the truth and to lie on several topics, in writing and out loud. They used a text program, called LIWC, to count the kinds of words in what they said.

Liars used fewer first-person singular pronouns, such as I and me, fewer words that mark exceptions and distinctions, such as but and except, and more words for negative emotions and for movement. The idea was that a liar keeps a distance from the story, and finds it hard to build complex detail. Later reviews found that these signs are weak, and that they vary with the situation. No list of words works as a lie detector for one person.

# The humblebrag
@src Sezer, Gino & Norton, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2018

Complaining that you are tired of being asked for identification because you look so young is a humblebrag, which is a boast hidden behind a complaint or an act of modesty. In 2018 Ovul Sezer, Francesca Gino and Michael Norton ran nine studies of how it goes over, including a week-long diary study and an experiment in the field.

Humblebraggers were rated as less likable than people who bragged outright, or who simply complained. They were also seen as less competent, and people were less willing to do what they asked. The authors found that observers thought they were insincere. A plain boast at least gets some credit for honesty. If you want people to know about an achievement, it is better to say it simply, or to let someone else say it.

# Does swearing dull pain?
@conf mixed
@src Stephens, Atkins & Kingston, NeuroReport, 2009

Why do people swear when they hurt themselves? In 2009 Richard Stephens, John Atkins and Andrew Kingston asked 67 students to hold a hand in ice water for as long as they could. On one trial they repeated a swear word of their choice, and on the other a neutral word. Then they compared how long each person lasted, how much it hurt, and their heart rates.

Swearing let people keep their hand in the cold water longer, by about 40 seconds on average, and they rated the pain as lower. Their heart rates went up. The effect was not found in men who tended to think the worst about pain. The authors thought that swearing might trigger a stress response, though they later said they had not shown that. It won an Ig Nobel Prize in 2010, and it is a small study, but it is a rare one that backs a common habit.

# Is public speaking feared more than death?
@src Dwyer & Davidson, Communication Research Reports, 2012

It is a famous joke that people fear speaking in public more than death. It comes from a 1973 survey by a company called Bruskin, in which people picked their fears from a list. In 2012 Kathleen Dwyer and Marlina Davidson repeated the survey with 815 college students. They asked them in two ways.

When students were asked to check all of their fears from a list, public speaking was the one that they checked most often, and death came further down. But when they were asked to rank their top fears, death came first, and public speaking second, though not by much. The answer depends on how you ask. Fear of speaking is very common, and a lot of people also dread being judged. It is not that they would rather die.

# The five parts of a speech
@src Rhetorica ad Herennium, about the 80s BC

About two thousand years ago, a Roman teacher wrote a handbook for speakers. We do not know his name. The book is called Rhetorica ad Herennium, and for centuries it was believed to be by Cicero. It lists five tasks of a speaker. They are invention, which is finding what to say, arrangement, which is putting it in order, and style, which is choosing the words. The other two are memory, and delivery, which covers the voice and gestures.

The list was taken up by Cicero and later teachers, and it is still a good checklist. Most poor talks fail at one of the five. A talk with good content may be hard to follow because it is not arranged, or flat because it is read aloud in a monotone. The handbook also gives the earliest surviving description of the memory method of placing images of what you want to say in familiar places.

# A good person speaking well
@src Quintilian, Institutio Oratoria, about AD 95

Around AD 95 the Roman teacher Quintilian completed the Institutio Oratoria, a work in twelve books on how to educate an orator, from childhood on. His ideal, which he took from an older saying of Cato, was a good man skilled in speaking. He argued that skill without character is dangerous, because a speaker who is persuasive and dishonest does harm.

He also gave practical advice. He thought that teachers should be kind, that children learn through play, and that they should not be beaten. He wrote that a speaker should read widely, and write a lot, since writing is the best way to improve speaking. His book was lost for a time, and was found again in 1416, in a monastery in Switzerland, by an Italian scholar, Poggio Bracciolini. It then shaped the schools of the Renaissance.

# The anatomy of an argument
@src Toulmin, The Uses of Argument, 1958

In 1958 the philosopher Stephen Toulmin argued that everyday arguments do not look like the neat syllogisms of logic textbooks, and he gave a pattern for the parts of a good one. The claim is what you want people to accept. The grounds are the facts that you offer for it. The warrant is the rule that links the grounds to the claim.

The other parts are the backing, which supports the rule, the qualifier, which says how strong the claim is, and the rebuttal, which names the conditions under which it would fail. His example was Harry, who was born in Bermuda, and so is presumably a British subject, unless both of his parents were aliens. It is useful to ask of any argument what the warrant is. It is usually the part that is left unsaid, and it is often where the argument is weak.

# Fifty years of advice about the passive
@conf mixed
@src Pullum, The Chronicle of Higher Education, 2009

Many people were taught that the passive voice is weak and should be avoided. The advice goes back at least to The Elements of Style, by William Strunk and E. B. White, which tells writers to use the active voice. In 2009 the linguist Geoffrey Pullum wrote a sharp essay, arguing that the authors did not understand what a passive is. He noted that most of the examples that they gave of the passive in their rule were not passives.

Strunk and White did not ask that the passive be banned. They wrote that it is frequently convenient and sometimes necessary. Pullum's critics said that he was too harsh, and that the real aim of the advice was to avoid limp writing. The lesson is that a rule of thumb about writing should be tested against real sentences. The passive is useful, for example, when you want the object of an action to come first.
