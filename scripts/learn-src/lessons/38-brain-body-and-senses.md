# One night without sleep and the emotional brain
@src Yoo, Gujar, Hu, Jolesz & Walker, Current Biology, 2007

In 2007 Seung-Schik Yoo, Matthew Walker and their colleagues kept some healthy young adults awake for about thirty-five hours. Others slept normally. Then everyone lay in a brain scanner and looked at pictures that grew more and more disturbing. The researchers watched the amygdala, a small region that responds to threat and strong emotion.

In the people who hadn't slept, the amygdala reacted much more strongly to the unpleasant pictures. At the same time, its connection with the medial prefrontal cortex, a region that helps to calm and regulate emotion, was weaker. So a tired brain has a stronger alarm and a weaker brake. It's a good reason not to have the hardest conversation of the week at the end of a sleepless night.

# Caffeine, even at dinner
@src Drake, Roehrs, Shambroom & Roth, Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, 2013

In 2013 Christopher Drake and his colleagues tested caffeine at different times of day. Twelve healthy people took a dose of caffeine, about the amount in two large cups of coffee, either right at bedtime, three hours before, or six hours before. On other nights, they took a placebo. Their sleep was measured at home with a portable recording device.

Even when the caffeine was taken six hours before bed, sleep was shorter by roughly an hour. And people often didn't notice. When asked, many said that their sleep was fine, though the recordings showed otherwise. The study was small, but it fits with other work. If sleep is a problem, the afternoon coffee may be a cause that you can't feel.

# Why caffeine works
@src Porkka-Heiskanen et al., Science, 1997; Fredholm et al., 1999

The longer you are awake, the more sleepy you get, and the reason is partly a chemical called adenosine. It's a by-product of the brain's use of energy. In 1997 Tarja Porkka-Heiskanen and her colleagues showed in cats that adenosine builds up in a part of the brain that promotes wakefulness during waking, and falls during sleep. It acts as a kind of pressure gauge for sleep.

Caffeine blocks the receptors that adenosine acts on. It doesn't give you energy, and it doesn't remove sleep debt. It stops the brain from feeling the signal. When the caffeine wears off, the adenosine that has built up is still there, which is why a crash can follow. It explains why a good night's sleep works better than another coffee.

# The near-miss
@src Clark, Lawrence, Astley-Jones & Gray, Neuron, 2009

When a slot machine shows two cherries and a lemon, you didn't win, but it feels as though you nearly did. In 2009 Luke Clark and his colleagues tested what this does. People played a simplified slot machine in a brain scanner. Near-misses felt less pleasant than clear losses, and yet they made people want to keep playing.

The brain responded as well. Near-misses activated reward areas, including the ventral striatum, and also the insula, which are the regions activated by actual wins. The effect was stronger when people felt that they had some control, for example when they picked the symbols. Casinos and games exploit this. A near-miss isn't progress toward a win, since each spin is independent, but the brain treats it as encouragement.

# Why songs outlast names
@src Jacobsen et al., Brain, 2015

People with advanced Alzheimer's disease may fail to recognize their own children and yet sing every word of a song from their youth. It's a well known observation among carers, and it has a possible explanation. In 2015 Jörn-Henrik Jacobsen and his colleagues used brain scans to find which regions are involved in remembering music that people have known for a long time.

Then they looked at how those regions were affected by Alzheimer's. The regions, including the caudal anterior cingulate and a part of the motor planning area, showed relatively little shrinkage and little loss of activity compared with other parts of the brain. They did show the sticky protein that marks the disease. The finding suggests why musical memory can last when others fail. It's also a reason why music can be a good way to reach someone with dementia.

# The other-race effect
@src Meissner & Brigham, Psychology, Public Policy, and Law, 2001

People are better at recognizing faces of their own racial group than faces of others. In 2001 Christian Meissner and John Brigham combined the results of dozens of studies. They found that people were noticeably more accurate at recognizing faces from their own race, and were more likely to falsely say that they'd seen a face of another race before.

The effect appears in many groups around the world, and it's not a sign of prejudice. It seems to come from experience. We become expert at telling apart the kinds of faces that we see most, and we notice the features that vary among them. People who grow up in mixed communities have a smaller effect. It matters in the real world, for eyewitness identification, where mistakes about faces can have serious consequences.

# When an eyewitness is right
@src Wixted & Wells, Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2017

Eyewitnesses have a bad reputation, since mistaken identifications have put innocent people in prison. But the reputation is partly based on confidence expressed in court, long after the event. In 2017 John Wixted and Gary Wells reviewed the research on how well confidence goes with accuracy, and found that it depends on the conditions.

When a witness first identifies a suspect from a fair lineup, with no hints from anyone, and says immediately that they are highly confident, the identification is quite likely to be right. When the witness has been told, after the lineup, that they picked the right person, the confidence rises and it means much less. The lesson is that the first confidence is useful if it is collected carefully, and that later confidence can't be trusted.

# A warm cup, a warm judgment?
@src Williams & Bargh, Science, 2008; Lynott et al., Social Psychology, 2014
@conf contested

In 2008 Lawrence Williams and John Bargh reported that people who held a warm cup of coffee for a few seconds rated a stranger as having a warmer personality than people who held an iced coffee. The idea was that physical warmth and social warmth are linked in the brain. It became a famous example of embodied thinking, that the body can shape the way we judge.

Later, other labs tried to repeat the finding, and several failed to find the effect. One large effort in 2014 by Daniel Lynott and his colleagues found nothing. The idea hasn't been disproved, since the physical and social senses of warmth may be linked in other ways, but this specific result is no longer considered reliable. It's an example of how a striking finding can fade when it is tested again.

# Springing forward
@src Janszky & Ljung, New England Journal of Medicine, 2008; Jiddou et al., 2013

Every spring, clocks go forward an hour and most people lose an hour of sleep. Does it affect health? In 2008 Imre Janszky and Rickard Ljung looked at heart attacks in Sweden over many years. They found that the number of heart attacks rose in the first few days after the clocks went forward, and dropped a little after they went back in the fall.

In 2013 a group in Michigan, led by Mohammed Jiddou, found a similar rise in heart attacks on the Monday after the spring change. The increase was modest, and most of the people affected were probably at risk already. The explanation that fits best is the loss of sleep and a shift in the body clock. It's a reminder that small disruptions in sleep may matter. If your clocks change, protect your sleep for a few days.

# Why you can't remember being two
@src Bauer, 2015; Akers et al., Science, 2014
@conf mixed

Almost no one can remember events from before the age of about three. It's called childhood amnesia, and it's strange, since toddlers learn at a great rate and remember things for months. Young children can remember events, but those memories tend to fade away as they grow up.

One idea, tested in mice by Katherine Akers and her colleagues in 2014, is that the infant brain makes new neurons in the hippocampus so quickly that they may overwrite old memories. When the researchers slowed neuron growth in infant mice, the mice forgot less. Other explanations point to the late development of language and the sense of self. It's not settled, and several factors may play a part. But it shows that forgetting isn't always a failure of memory. It may be a side effect of growth.

# Déjà vu
@src Brown, Psychological Bulletin, 2003; Cleary et al., Consciousness and Cognition, 2012

About two thirds of people say that they've had déjà vu, the strange feeling that you've been somewhere before, though you know that you haven't. It's more common in young adults, in people who travel and remember their dreams, and when people are tired. It isn't a sign of any illness.

One explanation has some experimental support. In 2012 Anne Cleary and her colleagues showed people scenes in a virtual world. Some new scenes had the same layout as ones that they had seen earlier, though the objects were different. People felt déjà vu more often in these. The idea is that the brain recognizes the layout, but you can't recall the earlier scene that it matches. You get the feeling of familiarity without the memory.

# Aircraft noise and children's reading
@src Hygge, Evans & Bullinger, Psychological Science, 2002

In 1992 the main airport of Munich, Germany, was moved to a new site. This gave researchers an unusual chance to study noise. Staffan Hygge and his colleagues tested the memory and reading of children who lived near the old airport and near the new one, before and after the move.

After the old airport closed, the children who lived near it improved in long-term memory and in reading. The children near the new airport, where noise had just started, did worse in those two areas. Because the same children were tested over time, it's strong evidence that noise has an effect. It helps to explain why a quiet place to study can matter, and why noise in a home or a school isn't just an annoyance.

# Clutter and stress
@src Saxbe & Repetti, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2010
@conf mixed

In 2010 Darby Saxbe and Rena Repetti asked thirty couples to take a video tour of their homes. They said whatever came to mind. The researchers counted how often people described their homes with words such as cluttered, messy or unfinished, and how often with words such as restful or cozy. They also measured the people's cortisol, a stress hormone, over several days.

Women who described their homes in the cluttered terms had cortisol levels that stayed high through the day, rather than falling in the evening as it usually does. They were also more depressed. This was not true of the men. The study can't show that the clutter caused the stress, since a stressed person may see more clutter. But many people find that tidying makes them feel calmer, and it's a cheap thing to try.

# Following a scent trail
@src Porter et al., Nature Neuroscience, 2007

Dogs follow a scent along the ground, and most people would assume that we can't. In 2007 Jess Porter and her colleagues tested it. They blindfolded and gloved a group of students, put earmuffs on them, and asked them to crawl across a field and follow a trail of chocolate scent about ten meters long.

Most of the students managed to follow it, and they got better with practice. They zigzagged back and forth across the line, as dogs do. They did better with both nostrils open than with one blocked, which suggests that we compare the smell between the two sides, as a way to tell direction. Our sense of smell is better than we think. We simply don't use it much.

# The speed of a caress
@src Löken et al., Nature Neuroscience, 2009

Some of the nerve fibers in our skin are tuned for gentle touch. They're called C-tactile fibers, and they respond best to light stroking at about the speed of a slow caress. In 2009 Line Löken and her colleagues recorded from these fibers in people, while a soft brush was drawn over the skin at different speeds.

The fibers fired most to speeds of a few centimeters a second, and people rated those speeds as the most pleasant. Very fast or very slow strokes did less on both counts. The fibers are found mainly in hairy skin, such as the forearm and back. The finding suggests that part of the nervous system is built for pleasant social touch, and not only for sensing objects. It might be why a slow stroke on the arm feels comforting.

# The itch that spreads
@src Holle, Warne, Seth, Critchley & Ward, PNAS, 2012

If you've watched someone scratch, you may have felt an urge to do the same. In 2012 Henning Holle and his colleagues tested this by showing people videos of others scratching, and then measuring how much they scratched themselves. Watching a video of scratching made people scratch more than watching a video of someone doing something else.

The brain scans showed activity in regions related to itch and to movement. Some people were more prone to the contagion than others, and those who scored higher on neuroticism, the tendency to feel anxious and tense, tended to be more affected. Contagious itch is related to contagious yawning, and it suggests that the brain responds to other people's bodily states. It's an easy experiment. Try reading about a mosquito bite.
