# The nose that Napoleon did not shoot
@src Great Sphinx of Giza; Norden's drawings, 1737

A story often told to visitors to Egypt is that the Great Sphinx lost its nose when Napoleon's soldiers used it for target practice during the French campaign, between 1798 and 1801. It is not true. The nose was already missing long before Napoleon arrived, and early drawings show the face without it.

A Danish naval officer, Frederic Norden, visited Egypt in the 1730s and drew the Sphinx, and his sketches, published in 1755, show it with its face damaged and no nose. That is sixty years before the French army landed. Medieval Arab writers had also mentioned that the face had been mutilated.

The belief probably owes its life to the fame of Napoleon, who is blamed for many things, and to the tendency to credit the most famous person with whatever damage is visible. Who removed the nose, and when, has been a matter of dispute. The story that has been told about Napoleon is a good example of a legend that sticks because it names a villain.

# The fifth of May is not Independence Day
@src Battle of Puebla, May 5, 1862; Mexican Declaration of Independence, 1810

Many Americans think that Cinco de Mayo, the fifth of May, is Mexico's Independence Day. It is not. It marks the victory of the Mexican army over French forces at the Battle of Puebla on May 5, 1862. The French emperor Napoleon III had sent an army to Mexico, and though it was much better equipped, the Mexicans under General Ignacio Zaragoza held the town and won the battle.

Mexican independence from Spain is celebrated on September 16. It marks the Grito de Dolores, the call to arms by the priest Miguel Hidalgo in 1810, which began the war of independence. The country did not win independence until 1821.

The victory at Puebla was not the end of the war. The French returned with a larger force the next year and occupied Mexico City, and installed Maximilian of Austria as emperor, though he was overthrown a few years later. In Mexico the fifth of May is a regional observance, kept most in the state of Puebla. In the United States it has grown into a celebration of Mexican-American culture, which is much bigger than it is in most of Mexico.

# The monk who would not die
@src Felix Yusupov, memoirs; autopsy of Grigori Rasputin, December 1916

Grigori Rasputin was a Siberian peasant and mystic who gained great influence at the court of the last Russian emperor, Nicholas II, because the tsar's son was ill, and Rasputin seemed able to ease his suffering. In December 1916 a group of nobles killed him. The story of his death has become a legend. It says that he was given cakes and wine laced with enough cyanide to kill several men, and was not harmed. Then he was shot several times, and still lived, until he was thrown into the icy river, where he finally drowned.

It makes a striking tale, but there is little to support it. A contemporary autopsy reported that he was killed by gunshots. The only source of the tale of the poison and the long struggle is a sensational account in the memoirs of one of the conspirators, Prince Felix Yusupov.

Since it is the only source for the poison and the long struggle, historians treat the account with great caution. The real killing was a messy affair by a few men who panicked, and who did not agree among themselves on what they had done.

# The charge that never was
@src Charge at Krojanty, September 1, 1939; accounts of Polish cavalry

It is widely believed that in 1939, in the first days of the German invasion of Poland, Polish cavalry rode with lances and sabers against German tanks. It is told as a symbol of courage and of the folly of old methods against new. It did not happen in that way.

The story seems to come from a cavalry action at Krojanty on September 1, 1939, by the 18th Pomeranian Uhlans. They attacked a German infantry unit in the open and scattered it. Then German armored cars came out of a nearby forest and fired with machine guns. The Poles took heavy losses, and retreated. No tanks were used in the fight.

The legend started with a report by an Italian journalist, Indro Montanelli, who wrote about Polish horsemen charging tanks. German propaganda picked it up, as it fitted the image of a backward Polish army. In fact the Polish cavalry were trained to fight dismounted, and used horses for movement. The regiment at Krojanty was decorated the next day. The truth is a story of a successful charge, followed by a deadly surprise.

# The king who did not wear a star
@src Rescue of the Danish Jews, October 1943; U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum

A well-loved story says that when the Germans occupied Denmark in the Second World War and ordered the Jews to wear yellow stars, King Christian X wore one himself, and soon all Danes did. It is a moving tale, but it did not happen. Danish Jews were never made to wear a star or any other mark. The Germans did not impose that rule in Denmark.

What did happen was remarkable in a different way. In early October 1943 the Germans planned a roundup of the country's Jews, timed for the Jewish New Year, Rosh Hashanah. The plan was leaked, and Danes warned their Jewish neighbors. In a few weeks, fishing boats and other small craft carried some 7,200 Jews and about 700 of their non-Jewish relatives across the narrow strait to neutral Sweden.

The Danish police are reported to have refused to help with the arrests. The story of the king and the star seems to have grown up after the rescue, and was spread further by writers such as Leon Uris in his novel Exodus. The real rescue was done by a large number of ordinary people, and it saved the great majority of Denmark's Jews.

# The pilots who had fuel to come home
@src Studies of Japanese special attack units, 1944 and 1945

The kamikaze pilots of Japan, in the last year of the Second World War, flew planes loaded with bombs and crashed them into Allied ships. A common belief is that they were sent with only enough fuel for a one-way trip. It is not true.

Pilots were allowed to return without punishment, if they could not find a target or if their plane had mechanical trouble. Some did come back, and flew again. There was also a reason to carry extra fuel. A plane with a full tank made a larger fire and a bigger explosion when it hit its target.

The belief may have come from the fact that the missions were not planned for a return, so people assumed that the planes could not do so. The truth is no less grim. Thousands of young men died in these attacks, many of them students who were under pressure to volunteer, and the policy has been the subject of much debate. The detail of the fuel is a small one, but it shows how a reasonable-sounding guess becomes a fact.

# The woman who may not have sewn the flag
@src Congressional records; Francis Hopkinson; William Canby, 1870

Betsy Ross, a Philadelphia upholsterer, is famous as the woman who made the first American flag. She is said to have sewn it in 1776 at the request of George Washington, with a circle of thirteen stars. The picture appears in many schoolbooks. There is no good evidence that she did it.

The claim was first made publicly in 1870, almost a century later, by her grandson, William Canby, in a talk to the Historical Society of Pennsylvania. It was based on what family members said they remembered being told. No contemporary document supports it, though Ross was a real seamstress who made flags for the Pennsylvania navy.

The Continental Congress passed the flag resolution in 1777. The records of Congress credit Francis Hopkinson, a signer of the Declaration of Independence, with designing it, and he sent a bill to the government for his work. The story of Betsy Ross is a good example of how a family tradition, told with love, can become a national legend, and be hard to correct.

# What the Emancipation Proclamation did not do
@src Emancipation Proclamation, January 1, 1863; General Order No. 3, June 19, 1865; Thirteenth Amendment

It is often said that on January 1, 1863, Abraham Lincoln freed all the slaves in the United States with the Emancipation Proclamation. He did not. The proclamation applied only to the ten states that were still in rebellion. It did not cover the nearly half a million enslaved people in the border states that had stayed in the Union, or in parts of the South that were already held by Union forces.

In practice the proclamation only took effect as Union armies advanced, and as enslaved people left for the Union lines. It also made the abolition of slavery a goal of the war, and allowed Black men to enlist in the army and navy.

Juneteenth is the same. It marks June 19, 1865, when a Union general, Gordon Granger, announced in Galveston, Texas, that the enslaved were free. That order applied to Texas only. Slavery was ended in the whole country by the Thirteenth Amendment, which was ratified and proclaimed in December 1865. It banned slavery everywhere in the United States, except as a punishment for a crime.

# The cow that did not burn Chicago
@src Great Chicago Fire, October 1871; Chicago City Council resolution, 1997

The Great Chicago Fire began on the night of October 8, 1871, and burned for about two days. It killed some 300 people, and left about a third of the city's people without homes. The story that is told is that it started when a cow belonging to Mrs. O'Leary kicked over a lantern in her barn.

The tale is false. A newspaper reporter later admitted that he had made it up to give color to his articles. The fire did seem to start in or near the O'Leary barn, but no one knows how, and there is no evidence that the cow was involved. Catherine O'Leary herself said that she was in bed asleep.

The O'Learys suffered much blame, and were attacked in the press. Part of the reason was prejudice against Irish immigrants. In 1997 the Chicago City Council passed a resolution that cleared Mrs. O'Leary and her cow of blame. The cause of the fire remains uncertain. A dry summer, wooden buildings and strong winds explain why it spread, and a good story filled the gap where the facts were missing.

# The elephant and the war of the currents
@series history
@src Topsy, Luna Park, Coney Island, January 4, 1903

In 1903, at Luna Park on Coney Island, an elephant named Topsy was put to death by electrocution. She had killed a man, and her owners decided to destroy her. It is often said that the event was a demonstration by Thomas Edison, to show the dangers of the alternating current that was being promoted by his rivals, in the war of the currents.

It was not. Edison was never at Luna Park, and the war of the currents was over about ten years earlier. Alternating current had won. The idea that Edison was behind it comes in part from the fact that a film of the event was made by the Edison film company, which was in the business of recording newsworthy events.

The electrocution was meant as a spectacle and as a practical method. A first plan to hang her was dropped after objections from the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals. The death of Topsy is a sad one. The myth connects it with the best known electrical battle of the age, and with a famous name, and in doing so, hides the way that it actually came about.

# The names that were not changed at Ellis Island
@series history
@src Ellis Island records; ship manifests

A very common family story says that an immigrant's name was changed at Ellis Island. The official could not understand the name, or could not spell it, and wrote down something simpler. In truth, officials at Ellis Island did not record names in that way. They checked each person against the ship's manifest, a list prepared at the port of departure, usually by a clerk from that country who knew the spelling.

The inspectors at Ellis Island kept no other papers, and had no paper that would have let them change a surname. There was also no law that would have allowed it. In New York at the time, a person could change the spelling of a name simply by using a new one.

So when names changed, it was usually by the immigrants themselves. Some wanted to fit in, and some wanted to avoid being mocked. Others changed names years later, when they became citizens. The names that resulted are sometimes jokingly called Ellis Island specials. The story survives because it explains something that families do not otherwise know, and it puts the loss on a faceless official, which is easier than saying that their ancestors chose it.

# What Prohibition actually banned
@series history
@src Eighteenth Amendment, 1919; Volstead Act, 1920; Twenty-first Amendment, 1933

Many people think that during Prohibition in the 1920s it was illegal to drink alcohol in the United States. It was not. The Eighteenth Amendment, which was ratified in 1919, and the Volstead Act, which put it into effect, banned the production, sale and transport of intoxicating liquors. Possession and drinking were never made illegal.

That left some gaps. People who had stocked up before the ban began in January 1920 could keep drinking what they had. Wine could be made at home for personal use, in limited amounts. Doctors could prescribe whiskey, and the use of alcohol in religious services, such as the sacramental wine, was allowed.

The law was widely flouted. The illegal trade grew into a large business, and gave rise to organized crime and to speakeasies. Many people thought that it did more harm than good. Prohibition was ended in 1933 by the Twenty-first Amendment, the only time in American history that one amendment has repealed another. It is a good example of how a law can be remembered as stricter than its text.

# The night that America did not panic
@series history
@src Orson Welles, The War of the Worlds, radio broadcast, October 30, 1938

On the night before Halloween in 1938, Orson Welles and his radio company broadcast a radio play based on H. G. Wells' novel The War of the Worlds. It was written as a series of news bulletins about an invasion from Mars. The story has become famous. It says that the broadcast caused mass panic across the United States, and that thousands of people fled their homes.

Historians say that this is a myth. Few people were listening. At the time, the program had a small share of the radio audience, and most listeners were tuned to a popular rival show. There were reports of frightened callers and of some confusion, but no widespread panic.

The newspapers made the story big. They were competing with radio for advertising, and they were glad to show that radio could not be trusted. Their reports in the following days stressed isolated incidents, and the number of calls to emergency lines went up. Both Welles and the network, CBS, at first apologized. Later they saw that the legend helped them, and they embraced it. The myth is one of the best examples of how the press can create the news that it reports.

# Why the interstate highways were really built
@series history
@src Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956; Eisenhower administration

It is often said that President Eisenhower ordered the Interstate Highway System built mainly so that cities could be evacuated if there was a nuclear war, or so that troops could be moved. It is a popular claim. It is not the whole story.

There were military reasons. Eisenhower had seen the German autobahns during the Second World War, and as a young officer he had taken part in a slow army convoy across the country in 1919. The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 was officially called the National System of Interstate and Defense Highways. But the main reasons were civilian. Traffic was growing, roads were poor and dangerous, and there was a demand from drivers, truckers and businesses for a fast national road network.

The cost was to be paid mainly out of a tax on fuel. The system has been used to move goods and people more than to move armies. The myth gives a heroic, military meaning to a work of ordinary public policy. In the real story, it was a response to the needs of a country that was becoming dependent on cars.

# The citizen who did not give up his passport
@series people
@src W. E. B. Du Bois, 1963; U.S. State Department

William Edward Burghardt Du Bois was one of the most important African American thinkers and activists of the twentieth century. He helped found the NAACP, and wrote The Souls of Black Folk, in 1903. In his old age he moved to Ghana. It is often said that he gave up his American citizenship before he died. It is not quite true.

In early 1963 he was in Ghana, and he needed to have his passport renewed. The State Department refused, because of his membership in the Communist Party and his support for the Soviet Union. After leaving the embassy, he said that he would renounce his citizenship in protest. He did take Ghanaian citizenship. But he never actually gave up his American citizenship.

He died in Accra on August 27, 1963, at the age of 95, on the eve of the March on Washington. His story is a case of a man who was made to feel a stranger in his own country, and who chose to leave it in spirit, though not in law. The simple claim that he renounced his citizenship hides the part that the government played in forcing his hand.

# Did Kennedy call himself a doughnut?
@series words
@src John F. Kennedy, Berlin, June 26, 1963

On June 26, 1963, President John F. Kennedy spoke to a huge crowd in West Berlin, and ended with the words Ich bin ein Berliner. It is often said that he made a blunder. By adding the word ein, a, he is supposed to have said I am a jelly doughnut, since a Berliner is a kind of pastry. The story says that the crowd laughed.

It is wrong. Ich bin ein Berliner is standard German for I am a Berliner, meaning a citizen of the city, and it is what a speaker would say. The word ein is correct here, as it often is when a speaker says that he is a member of a group. The crowd cheered.

In addition, the pastry was not called a Berliner in Berlin, and it is not now. In the city the doughnut is known by another name, Pfannkuchen, and in other parts of Germany it has many names. A person in Berlin would not have thought of the pastry at once. The story is told because it is a funny one, and is flattering to people who like to catch a great man in a mistake.

# The housing project that never won a prize
@series history
@src Pruitt-Igoe, St. Louis, 1954 to 1972; Architectural Forum

The Pruitt-Igoe housing project in St. Louis, Missouri, was a group of 33 high-rise apartment blocks, built in the 1950s. It became notorious for crime and decay, and the city began to demolish the buildings in 1972. The first of them was blown up in front of the cameras. It has often been used as a symbol of the failure of modern architecture and of public housing.

A popular story says that the design had won a prize for its architecture. It had not. A magazine, Architectural Forum, did praise the plan before it was built, calling it the best high apartment of the year. But the project never won any awards. The firm that designed it did win an award for an earlier St. Louis project, and the two may have been mixed up.

The reasons for the failure are debated, and they include poor maintenance, cuts in funding, the loss of jobs in the city, and segregation, as well as the design itself. Putting the whole blame on the architecture makes a simple tale, but the causes were many. It is a case in which a myth about a prize helped to sharpen a lesson that may not be quite right.

# The soldiers who were not spat upon
@series history
@src Lembcke, The Spitting Image, 1998; contemporary records

It is widely believed that when American soldiers returned from the Vietnam War, anti-war protesters often spat on them. It is told in memoirs, and was depicted in films, such as the Rambo movie First Blood. But historians have found little or no evidence from the time that it happened.

The sociologist Jerry Lembcke, who is himself a veteran of the war, looked for it in news reports and records from the years of the war. He found stories of the kind, but very few, and the accounts do not hold up well. He argued that the image came later, and was shaped by films and by the politics of the 1970s and 1980s. The veterans were real, and many were treated with indifference or hostility, but the particular image may be an invention.

The debate is a sensitive one. It does not deny that many returning soldiers had a hard time, and felt that the public did not honor them. It asks whether a symbol has replaced the record. Some veterans say that they were spat upon, and a few cases may have occurred. The claim that it was common, though, is not well supported.

# The protest where no bras were burned
@series history
@src Miss America protest, Atlantic City, September 7, 1968; New York Radical Women

On September 7, 1968, about 200 to 400 women protested outside the Miss America pageant in Atlantic City, New Jersey. They were organized by a group called New York Radical Women. They said that the pageant treated women as objects to be judged. They set up a Freedom Trash Can, and dropped into it things that they said stood for the way women were made to look, such as bras, girdles, hairspray, makeup, false eyelashes and mops.

The image of bra burning came from here, but no bras were burned. A reporter for the New York Post, Lindsy Van Gelder, had written before the event that the protesters would burn their bras, as young men were burning draft cards, and the columnist Art Buchwald then spread the idea. The organizers said that they had planned a fire, but that the police would not allow it on the wooden boardwalk.

One local newspaper did report that some items burned in the can, and the point is disputed. Most accounts say that nothing was set alight. The myth has stuck so well that it has stood for the whole women's movement. It shows how a phrase that is repeated enough can replace what really happened.

# Did America ever love the Apollo program?
@series history
@src Gallup and other polls, 1960s and 1970s; Apollo 11, July 1969

The Apollo program is often remembered as a time when the whole country was united behind a great goal. Pictures of crowds watching the moon landing in 1969 give support to the idea. But the polls of the time tell another story.

Public support for the space program was never as strong as people remember. The belief that Apollo was worth the time and money peaked at 51 percent for a few months after the Apollo 11 landing in July 1969. Apart from that, it stood at between 35 and 45 percent in the surveys that were done through the 1960s. At times a majority said that it was not worth the cost.

Many Americans thought that the money should be spent on problems at home, such as poverty and the cities. The program was also expensive, taking a large share of the federal budget at its peak in the mid-1960s. What united the country was the moment of the landing, which was watched by a vast audience. It was not a lasting consensus. The memory of unity is partly a result of the glow of success, which came later.

# The drink that was not Kool-Aid
@series history
@src Jonestown, November 18, 1978; investigators' reports

The phrase drinking the Kool-Aid has come to mean following a leader blindly, even to destruction. It comes from the event at Jonestown, a settlement in Guyana, on November 18, 1978. More than 900 people, followers of the preacher Jim Jones, died in a mass murder and suicide, after drinking a mixture that contained poison.

The drink was not Kool-Aid, though. Investigators found that it was a similar product, called Flavor-Aid. It was mixed with cyanide and other drugs. The brand name Kool-Aid has stuck because it is better known, and it was used in news reports and later in speech.

The facts of the event are far more terrible than the phrase suggests. Many of the dead were children, and many people there were forced or tricked. A congressman, Leo Ryan, and four others had been murdered at an airstrip earlier that day. Some survivors and historians object to the phrase, since it makes light of a mass murder and suggests that all the victims were willing. A saying that began as a brand error now carries a story that is simpler than the truth.

# Left, right and the French seats
@series words
@src French National Assembly, 1789

We use left and right to talk about politics, and many people believe that the terms come from the Bible. Posts on social media often quote verses, such as Ecclesiastes 10:2, which says that a wise man's heart is at his right hand and a fool's at his left. This is not the origin of the political terms.

Historians say that the distinction came from France, in the summer of 1789, in the National Assembly that was meeting during the early days of the Revolution. A seating plan put the supporters of the king and the old order on the president's right, and the supporters of change on his left. The members who wanted to keep the king's power to veto laws sat on one side, and those who opposed it on the other.

The habit stuck, and spread to other countries. The words came to refer to a whole range of ideas about equality, tradition, and the role of the state. So the labels came from a seating chart, and not from scripture. It is an example of a phrase of common speech whose roots lie in an accident of a room, in a revolution.
