# Which half of the advertising is wasted?
@conf mixed
@src Quote Investigator on the Wanamaker saying; Printers' Ink, 1890

"Half the money I spend on advertising is wasted, and the trouble is I don't know which half." The line is usually credited to the Philadelphia merchant John Wanamaker. The researcher who runs Quote Investigator found that Wanamaker was given credit for it in 1919, but could not find a speech, letter or interview in which he said it.

A similar idea appeared in a trade magazine, Printers' Ink, in 1890. Close variants were credited to others, among them the soap maker Lord Leverhulme and the chewing gum maker William Wrigley Jr. So the saying is an old adage with an unclear author, and Wanamaker is only the best candidate.

The reason it has lasted is that it describes a real difficulty. A customer may have seen a newspaper ad, a window display, a friend's opinion and a coupon before buying, and no one can say which of these did the work. Better measurement has helped, but the problem of knowing what caused a sale has never gone away.

# The mall that was meant to be a town
@series history
@src Southdale Center, 1956; Victor Gruen, 1978 speech

Southdale Center in Edina, Minnesota, opened on October 8, 1956. It is widely described as the first fully enclosed, climate-controlled shopping mall in the United States, and it had two stories. The architect Victor Gruen designed it for the Dayton's department store, in part to bring shoppers out in the Minnesota winter.

Gruen was born in Vienna, and he had a different aim from the one that followed. He saw an enclosed shopping center as a way to give car-dependent suburbs something like a town center, with a place to meet and walk. His firm designed more than fifty malls in the United States before the middle of the 1970s.

But the idea spread in a form he did not like. In a 1978 speech in London he said that developers had bastardized his ideas, and he refused to take responsibility for the results. The number of traditional enclosed malls in America is said to have peaked at about 2,500 in the 1980s, and by 2022 it had fallen to about 700.

# The man who promised fifty percent in forty-five days
@series history
@src Ponzi, Boston, 1920; Smithsonian and U.S. Postal Service accounts

In 1920 an Italian immigrant in Boston named Charles Ponzi offered investors a profit of fifty percent in forty-five days, or a hundred percent in ninety. His explanation was a form of arbitrage. International reply coupons, which let a person pay for a return postage stamp, cost less in some countries than their face value in the United States, and he said he was buying them cheaply abroad and redeeming them at home.

The scheme did pay early investors, but it paid them out of the money from new ones. In August 1920 the Boston Post began to look into his returns, and there was a run on his company. Postal officials found that the volume of coupons needed to produce such profits did not exist. Ponzi was arrested on August 12 and charged with mail fraud.

Schemes of this kind are now named after him, though he did not invent the method. They tend to fall apart when new money slows down. A promise of high, steady returns that depends on a story no one can check is the warning sign that his case still illustrates.

# The investor whose returns never fell
@src Madoff, arrest December 11, 2008; sentence June 29, 2009

Bernard Madoff ran an investment firm in New York that was known for dependable returns. His clients included charities and well-known people, and he had been a respected figure on Wall Street. On December 11, 2008, he was arrested after his two sons reported him to the authorities.

The statements that his clients had received showed holdings of about $60 billion, and they were fictitious. The scheme was a Ponzi scheme, in which earlier clients were paid with money from later ones. Estimates of its size vary by source, from about $50 billion to about $65 billion in claimed balances, and the real losses to clients were lower.

Madoff pleaded guilty to eleven felony counts in March 2009. On June 29, 2009, Judge Denny Chin sentenced him to 150 years, the most that the law allowed, though his lawyers had asked for twelve. He died in prison in 2021. The case is often cited for a simple lesson. Returns that are too smooth for the market around them deserve a question.

# The blood tests that were not what they seemed
@src U.S. v. Holmes, 2022; Wall Street Journal reporting, 2015

Theranos was a California company, founded by Elizabeth Holmes, that said it could run many blood tests on a few drops from a finger. The idea was attractive, since it would spare people the needle. The company raised large sums from investors, and had famous people on its board.

In 2015 the Wall Street Journal reported that the company was in fact using commercially available blood analysis machines, adapted for small samples, for most of its tests. In January 2022 a jury convicted Holmes on four of eleven counts. One count was conspiracy to commit wire fraud against investors, and three were wire fraud counts, including transfers of more than $140 million. She was not convicted on the counts that dealt with patients.

On November 18, 2022, she was sentenced to eleven years and three months, less than the fifteen that prosecutors had asked for. The story is often told as one about a charming founder. It is also a case of a claim that was hard for outsiders to test, in a field where testing is exactly what matters.

# What was in the snake oil
@src Clark Stanley's Snake Oil Liniment, Bureau of Chemistry, 1916 and 1917; Smithsonian

In the 1890s a man named Clark Stanley sold a liniment called Snake Oil, and billed himself as the Rattlesnake King. It was meant to ease aches and pains. Many remedies of the time promised wonders and gave no details of what they contained.

In 1906 the United States passed the Pure Food and Drug Act, which required honest labeling. The government's chemists, in the Bureau of Chemistry, which became the Food and Drug Administration, tested Stanley's liniment. They found mineral oil, a small amount of fatty oil thought to be tallow, capsaicin from red peppers, and turpentine, along with camphor.

Stanley was fined twenty dollars for misbranding in 1917, and pleaded no contest. The phrase snake oil has since come to mean a worthless remedy sold with big claims. The fine was small, but the case helped to establish that what a label says must match what is in the bottle.

# The cigarette that was a lady's, until it wasn't
@src Marlboro, 1924; Leo Burnett, 1954 and 1955

Marlboro was introduced in 1924 as a cigarette for women, and its slogan was "Mild as May." In the early 1950s filters came into fashion, and filter cigarettes were seen as feminine and tasteless.

In 1954 Philip Morris repackaged Marlboro, and hired the Chicago advertising man Leo Burnett to change its image. Burnett chose the cowboy as a masculine symbol. The ads avoided health claims about the filter and showed rugged men in the outdoors. The campaign became one of the best known in advertising.

Sources disagree on the sales figures for the first years, so they are best treated with caution. What is not in doubt is the method. The cigarette itself was not changed very much. Its meaning was changed, by giving a product a new place in the mind of a different buyer. It is often used as the standard example of repositioning.

# The ad that ran once
@series history
@src Apple Macintosh commercial, Super Bowl XVIII, January 22, 1984

On January 22, 1984, during the third quarter of Super Bowl XVIII, a commercial for the new Apple Macintosh was shown. It was directed by Ridley Scott. It showed rows of gray workers listening to a giant face on a screen, and a woman in bright clothes running in to throw a hammer at it. It drew on the novel 1984, by George Orwell, and the gray world stood for the computer company IBM.

The commercial said that the Macintosh would be introduced two days later. Apple's board is reported to have disliked the ad, and to have wanted the time slot sold back. How that decision was reversed is not clear in the accounts. It was shown only once nationally, and also in a few local markets on December 31, 1983, which qualified it for advertising awards.

It is credited with starting the modern era of Super Bowl advertising, where big new campaigns are launched for a national audience at once. The commercial did not show the computer well. It sold an attitude, and the fact that people talked about it afterward did much of the work.

# One click, and a lawsuit
@series history
@src U.S. Patent 5,960,411; Amazon.com v. Barnesandnoble.com, 1999 to 2002

Buying online once meant typing an address and card number again for every order. Amazon's chief executive, Jeff Bezos, is said in a court filing to have conceived of letting customers buy with a single click, and the feature went into use in September 1997. Amazon filed for a patent on it that same month, and U.S. Patent No. 5,960,411 was issued on September 28, 1999.

About three weeks later Amazon sued its rival Barnesandnoble.com in Seattle, over a similar feature called Express Lane. On December 1, 1999, Judge Marsha Pechman granted a preliminary injunction that made the bookseller stop using it until the case was decided. The Federal Circuit vacated that injunction in early 2001, and the case was settled in 2002, on terms that were not made public.

The dispute was about whether such a simple idea could be patented. It also showed how much value a company placed on removing small steps in buying. Each field to fill in is a chance for a customer to stop, and one click is the shortest form of a purchase.

# What Black Friday really means
@series history
@src Snopes, Britannica and Philadelphia newspaper accounts

A story often told is that Black Friday got its name because stores go from red ink to black ink, from losses to profits, on the day after Thanksgiving. It is not the real origin. Snopes and others report that the earliest known version of that explanation dates from 1981.

The term came earlier, from Philadelphia. By 1961 the city's police were using it, in a sour way, for the day when huge crowds of shoppers and cars filled the downtown streets. The weekend of the Army and Navy football game added to the crowds. Accounts differ on exact dates, from the late 1950s to 1966, but all agree on the police.

Merchants disliked the dark meaning, and in the early 1960s tried to replace it with the idea of a good day. One account credits a retailer, Peter Strawbridge, with inventing the red and black story. It worked, and by the 1980s the name had spread nationwide. A word can start as a complaint and end as a sales event.

# The coupon that put a drink in a million hands
@series history
@src Coca-Cola Company history; Asa Candler, 1886 to 1913

Coca-Cola began as a drink sold at one pharmacy in Atlanta in 1886. When the businessman Asa Candler took over, he used a method that was new then. He handed out tickets, which could be exchanged at soda fountains for a free glass. Sources differ about the first ticket, giving a date between 1886 and 1888.

The fountain owners were reimbursed by the company, so they lost nothing, and the free drink was a way of getting people to try the taste. The company's own history says that between 1894 and 1913 about one in nine Americans had been given a free Coca-Cola, a total of some 8.5 million free drinks. It is a cumulative estimate and not a count, and it is widely repeated.

The method has been called the first coupon, though others may have used something like it. It rests on a simple idea. A product that few people have tasted is hard to sell, but one that has been tried for free can be bought out of habit. The cost of the free glass was a cost of advertising.

# The phone call that lasted ten hours
@src Zappos, December 8, 2012; Business Insider, 2016

On December 8, 2012, a customer service representative at the online shoe store Zappos, named Shaea Labus, spent 10 hours and 29 minutes on a single phone call. The customer wanted help choosing a pair of Ugg boots, and the call ended with a sale. It broke the company's own record for the longest call, which accounts give as a little under nine hours.

Records have been broken since. A call handled by Steven Weinstein in 2016 was reported to have lasted 10 hours and 43 minutes. Most call centers measure how short a call is, as a sign of efficiency. Zappos treated a long call as something to be proud of, since the first of its core values was to wow the customer through service.

The company's idea was that a customer treated well would come back, and tell others. Whether that pays for itself in each case is a fair question, and a ten hour call is an extreme example. But it shows that what is measured shapes what workers do, and that a business can choose to measure something else.

# The chicken that is sold at a loss
@src Costco earnings call, 2015; Snopes and NPR reports

For years Costco has sold a cooked rotisserie chicken for $4.99, even as other food prices rose. In a 2015 earnings call, the chief financial officer, Richard Galanti, described the company as willing to give up $30 million to $40 million a year in gross margin to keep the price there. By 2019 a senior vice president would not say whether the chicken still lost money.

It is a loss leader, a product sold at or below cost to bring people into the store. A food marketing professor, Ernest Baskin, explained that the aim is to draw shoppers who then buy other things. Costco's hot dog and soda combination, at $1.50 since the mid-1980s, works the same way. A story says that the former chief executive, Jim Sinegal, told a colleague that raising the price would be a firing offense, though accounts differ on who said it.

The idea is that shoppers remember the price of a few items, and judge the whole store by them. A famous low price on one thing can make everything else look fair. Snopes noted that the true cost to Costco is hard to know from outside the company.

# How shoppers move through a store
@series history
@src Underhill, Why We Buy: The Science of Shopping, 1999

Paco Underhill founded a research company, Envirosell, whose workers watched shoppers. His book Why We Buy, published in 1999, drew on thousands of hours of video, store maps and interviews. He wanted to see what people did in a store, and not what they said they did.

He described the entrance as a decompression zone, or landing strip. People who come in from the street need a few steps to slow down and adjust to the light, and to look around. A sign or display placed right at the door gets little notice, since the shopper's mind is still outside. He also described what is called the butt-brush effect. Shoppers dislike standing where other people may brush against them from behind, and in a narrow aisle some will leave a product and move on.

The findings were practical, on where to place a display and how wide to make an aisle. Some of the numbers repeated about his work cannot be traced to the book, so they are best taken as rules of thumb. The central point is that the behavior of people in a space can be seen, and that design can change it.

# The first store where you got your own groceries
@series history
@src Piggly Wiggly, Memphis, 1916; U.S. Patent 1,242,872

Before 1916 a customer in a grocery store usually asked a clerk, who fetched each item from behind a counter. On September 11, 1916, Clarence Saunders opened a new kind of store in Memphis, Tennessee, at 79 Jefferson Avenue. He called it Piggly Wiggly. The company counts its founding from September 6.

Customers took a basket, walked through aisles, and picked up goods that were priced and branded. They paid at checkouts at the front. It is generally described as the first true self-service grocery store. The shop needed fewer clerks, and shoppers could see everything on offer, and take their time.

Saunders filed a patent on a self-serving store on October 21, 1916, and it was granted on October 9, 1917, as U.S. Patent 1,242,872. A later one covered the design of the store. Stores of this kind spread quickly. The change put the package in charge of the sale, since the shopper now met the product with no one to explain it.

# Houses that came in the mail
@series history
@src Sears Modern Homes, 1908 to 1940; Hagley Library; Wikipedia summary

From 1908 to 1940 the retailer Sears, Roebuck and Company sold houses by mail. A buyer picked a design from a catalog, and the materials, already measured and cut, came by railroad, with instructions for putting them together. The company offered hundreds of designs, and some sources count 447 styles over the years.

Estimates of the number of houses sold vary. One source says between 70,000 and 75,000 from 1908 to 1940, while the Hagley Library reports more than 100,000 units, not counting cabins, garages and farm buildings. Sears also gave financing to some buyers who could not get a loan elsewhere, which opened the market to more families.

The program ended in the years around 1940, and sales through local offices lasted into 1942. Its success shows how far a trusted catalog could stretch. If people were willing to buy a house from a page of pictures, then a promise of quality, a guarantee and good financing mattered more than seeing the thing first.

# When the sales target became the product
@series think
@src CFPB action, September 2016; NPR and Fortune reports

In September 2016 federal regulators fined Wells Fargo a combined $185 million. Of it, $100 million went to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, which was the largest fine the bureau had imposed up to then. They said that bank employees had opened more than two million accounts that customers may not have authorized, about 565,000 credit cards and 1.5 million deposit accounts.

The cause, according to the regulators, was pressure to meet sales targets and win pay incentives. Employees moved money into new accounts without asking, issued debit cards and made up PINs, and in some cases created false email addresses. The bank fired about 5,300 employees over the sales practices between 2011 and 2016, and dropped branch sales quotas after the fine. It paid $110 million to affected customers in 2017.

Later estimates put the number of fake accounts nearer to 3.5 million. The case is taught as a warning about incentives. When a measure is made the goal, people find ways of meeting the measure, whether or not the customer is served, and the people at the bottom of the chain can end up carrying the blame.

# The company that laughed at Netflix
@conf mixed
@src Hastings and Randolph accounts; Fortune, 2023; Newsweek fact check

In early 2000 the founders of Netflix, Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph, are said to have offered to sell their company to the video rental chain Blockbuster for $50 million. Blockbuster's chief executive, John Antioco, declined. The founders have said that he appeared to be holding back laughter. Antioco has said that serious talks never took place.

Netflix then sent DVDs by mail, and made its name partly by having no late fees. Blockbuster is estimated to have collected about $800 million a year in late fees, which made them hard to give up. It dropped them in 2005, but the effort is reported to have backfired, in part because the terms were confusing.

Hastings has said that he is glad the deal did not happen. The story is often told as a case of a leader missing the future. A fairer way to read it is that a company living on a certain kind of revenue is slow to threaten that revenue, even when its customers dislike it. A rival with nothing to lose has an easier time.

# How a company hid its losses
@series think
@src Enron, bankruptcy December 2, 2001; Britannica and ABA Journal accounts

Enron was an energy company in Houston that was ranked among the largest in the United States. On December 2, 2001, it filed for bankruptcy. Its collapse followed the discovery that it had hidden debts and exaggerated its profits.

Two methods were at the center. Its trading business used mark-to-market accounting, which let it record the estimated future gains of a contract as income right away. And it moved debts and troubled assets into partnerships that were kept off its books, some run by its own finance chief, Andrew Fastow. When the value of those partnerships fell, the guarantees that Enron had given came due.

Its auditor, Arthur Andersen, was convicted of obstruction in 2002 for destroying documents, and lost its license to audit public companies. The Supreme Court reversed that conviction in 2005 because of a flawed instruction to the jury, but by then the firm had collapsed. Jeffrey Skilling and Kenneth Lay were convicted in 2006, Lay died before he was sentenced, and Fastow pleaded guilty and received six years.

# A luxury brand with no history
@series history
@src Toyota Lexus F1 project, 1983 to 1989; Lexus retrospective

By the 1980s Japanese cars had won a place in America as economical and reliable. But people who wanted a luxury car looked to Mercedes-Benz, BMW and Cadillac. Toyota decided to try for that market, and in 1983 began a secret project, called F1, to build a car that would equal them.

It took six years and more than a billion dollars. One account says that about 1,400 engineers worked on it, and some 450 prototypes were built. The LS 400 was shown at the Detroit auto show in January 1989, and production began on May 15, 1989. Sales began in September. Toyota did not use its own name for the car. It created a new brand, Lexus, with its own dealerships.

The first-year target was 16,000 cars, and it was reported to have been met. The lesson that is often drawn is that a company with a reputation for one kind of product may have to build a separate name to be believed in another. People judge a brand by what it has meant before, and Lexus had no past to overcome.

# Who pays for the shelf
@src FTC, Slotting Allowances in the Retail Grocery Industry, 2003

When a food company brings out a new product, it often has to pay the supermarket before the product gets onto the shelf. The payment is called a slotting allowance. The Federal Trade Commission studied it, and in November 2003 it released a staff report with case studies in five product categories.

Retailers say that the money offsets the costs of new products that fail, and the work of making room for them. Manufacturers say that it is a charge for access to a limited resource. The report also looked at pay-to-stay fees, which are charged to keep an established product in place. The FTC warned that the study was based on a small number of anonymous cases, and might not represent the whole industry.

Critics argue that the fees favor large companies that can afford them, and make it harder for small makers to compete. Supporters say that they allocate scarce shelf space to those who value it most. Either way, the arrangement is a reminder that what is on a shelf is partly the result of who paid to put it there.
