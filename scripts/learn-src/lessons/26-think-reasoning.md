# Easy to remember, so it must be common
@src Tversky & Kahneman, Cognitive Psychology, 1973

Are there more English words that start with the letter K, or words that have K as their third letter? In 1973 Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman asked people that question, and most said that more words start with K. In fact, there are about twice as many words with K in third place, such as "ask," "make" and "like."

We find words that start with a letter easier to recall than words with the letter in the middle, and so we judge them to be more common. The researchers called it the availability heuristic: we estimate how frequent something is by how easily examples come to mind. It's usually a good shortcut, but it's thrown off by whatever is vivid, recent or dramatic. A plane crash on the news makes flying seem riskier than the many quiet days of safe flights.

# After September 11, the roads were deadlier
@src Gigerenzer, Psychological Science, 2004

After the attacks of September 11, 2001, many Americans were afraid to fly, and drove instead. The psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer wondered what that did to the death toll. He compared the number of people who died in road accidents in the year after the attacks with what would have been expected from previous years, and estimated that there were about 1,500 more deaths than usual.

The people who drove had avoided a vivid risk, and had taken on a more dangerous but less visible one. Gigerenzer called it dread risk: we fear events that are sudden and catastrophic and kill many people at once, and underrate the same number of deaths when spread out over time and place. It's a sobering example of how a bias in how we judge risk can cost lives. Driving is, mile for mile, far more dangerous than flying.

# The cab that was seen at night
@src Kahneman & Tversky, 1972

In a famous problem, a cab was involved in a hit-and-run at night. In the city, 85 percent of the cabs are green and 15 percent are blue. A witness says that the cab was blue. Tests show that the witness is right 80 percent of the time in identifying the colors. What's the probability that the cab was blue?

Most people say 80 percent. The correct answer is about 41 percent. Because green cabs are so much more common, many of the witness's "blue" reports will be mistakes about green cabs. The error is called base rate neglect: we pay attention to the specific evidence and ignore how common the thing was to begin with. It's an important mistake to avoid in medicine and in law, since a good test for a rare condition can still give mostly false alarms.

# Counting people instead of percentages
@src Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, Psychological Review, 1995

Problems about probabilities can be hard, even for experts. In 1995 Gerd Gigerenzer and Ulrich Hoffrage showed that the way that the information is presented makes a big difference. Imagine that you're told that 1 percent of women have a certain disease, that a test finds it in 90 percent of those who have it, and that it falsely flags 9 percent of those who don't. What are the chances that a woman who tests positive has the disease? Most people, doctors included, overestimate it badly.

Now say it with counts. Out of 1,000 women, 10 have the disease and 9 of them test positive. Of the 990 who don't, about 89 test positive. So about 98 women test positive, and only 9 of them have the disease, which is about 9 percent. Put in this form, most people get it right. If a statistic confuses you, ask for it in numbers of people.

# The game show puzzle
@src vos Savant, Parade, 1990

In 1990 Marilyn vos Savant, a columnist in Parade magazine, answered a reader's question about a TV game show. There are three doors. Behind one is a car, and behind the others are goats. You pick a door, say number one. The host, who knows where the car is, opens another door, say number three, to show a goat. Then he asks if you want to switch to door two. Should you?

She said yes, and that switching wins two thirds of the time. Thousands of readers, including mathematicians, wrote in to say that she was wrong, and that the odds were even. She was right. When you first choose, you have a one-third chance of being right. The host's reveal doesn't change that, so the other door has the remaining two-thirds. If you doubt it, try it with three playing cards, and you'll see.

# Red has come up twenty-six times
@src Chen, Moskowitz & Shue, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2016

On August 18, 1913, at a casino in Monte Carlo, the roulette ball landed on black 26 times in a row. Gamblers lost huge sums betting on red, convinced that it was due. But the wheel has no memory, and every spin had the same chance. The belief that a streak must end is called the gambler's fallacy.

In 2016 Daniel Chen, Tobias Moskowitz and Kelly Shue found the same bias outside the casino. They analyzed decisions by asylum judges, loan officers and baseball umpires, and found that each was slightly less likely to make the same decision as the one before, even though there was no reason to do so. A judge who had just granted several cases was more likely to deny the next. It's a quiet bias that we all share, the sense that things have to even out.

# The bat and the ball
@src Frederick, Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2005

Try this: a bat and a ball cost $1.10 in total. The bat costs a dollar more than the ball. How much is the ball? Most people think of 10 cents immediately, and it feels right. But it's wrong. If the ball cost 10 cents, the bat would cost $1.10, and the total would be $1.20. The ball costs 5 cents.

In 2005 Shane Frederick included this question in a three-question test of what he called cognitive reflection, the tendency to stop and check an intuitive answer. About half of the students at top universities gave the wrong answer to this problem. It shows that a smooth, confident answer isn't always right, and that even clever people can be tricked by a quick intuition. A habit of pausing for a second and checking can catch many errors.

# Quick and slow thinking
@src Stanovich & West, 2000; Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow, 2011
@conf mixed

In his 2011 book, Daniel Kahneman used the terms System 1 and System 2 for two ways of thinking, which had been described by Keith Stanovich and Richard West. System 1 is fast, automatic and effortless, like knowing that 2 plus 2 is 4, or reading anger on a face. System 2 is slow, deliberate and effortful, like multiplying 17 by 24, and it's easily tired.

Many of our mistakes, in his account, come from System 1 being quick and System 2 being lazy. The framework is a useful metaphor, though many psychologists point out that the mind isn't really divided into two neat systems, and intuition can be both good and bad. A more careful way to put it is that some thinking is quick and effortless, and some needs effort and attention, and that it's wise to know when a decision is worth slowing down for.

# Bigger numbers, worse odds
@src Denes-Raj & Epstein, JPSP, 1994

In 1994 Veronika Denes-Raj and Seymour Epstein offered people a chance to win a dollar by drawing a red jelly bean from one of two bowls. One bowl held 1 red bean among 10, a 10 percent chance. The other held 9 red beans among 100, a 9 percent chance. Many chose the bowl with 9 red beans, though they knew that the odds were slightly worse.

They said that they knew it, but that it felt better to have more red beans to choose from. This is called denominator neglect: we focus on the number of winning chances and ignore the total. It's why a rate such as "1,286 out of 10,000" sounds more frightening than "12.86 percent," and why health risks stated in big numbers can be misleading. When comparing odds, bring both numbers to the same base.

# Saving 2,000 birds or 200,000
@src Desvousges et al., 1993

In 1993 William Desvousges and his colleagues asked people how much they'd be willing to pay to save migrating birds from drowning in oil ponds. Some people were told that the number of birds was 2,000, others 20,000 and others 200,000. The average amounts that they said that they'd pay were about $80, $78 and $88.

The number of lives saved had almost no effect on the willingness to pay. It's called scope insensitivity. We react to the feeling that a problem evokes, a bird in oil, and not to the size of the problem. The finding has been repeated for many topics, from saving lives to cleaning lakes. It suggests that when deciding how to give or spend, we should force ourselves to think about numbers, because our feelings won't do the arithmetic.

# When feelings do the judging
@src Finucane et al., Journal of Behavioral Decision Making, 2000

In logic, the risks and benefits of something, such as nuclear power or pesticides, are separate. Yet in 2000 Melissa Finucane, Paul Slovic and their colleagues found that people's judgments about them are strongly linked. If they like something, they say that it has high benefits and low risks. If they dislike it, they say that it has low benefits and high risks.

The researchers called it the affect heuristic: we let our gut feelings guide our judgment. When people were rushed, the link grew stronger, and when they were given information that something had high benefits, they changed their view of its risks, without being given any new information about the risks. It's an important thing to notice in yourself. When a decision feels easy because you like or dislike something, ask whether you've assessed the facts or only the feeling.

# Better the devil you know
@src Ellsberg, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 1961

In 1961 the economist Daniel Ellsberg, who later became famous for leaking the Pentagon Papers, described a puzzle. An urn holds 90 balls. 30 of them are red. The other 60 are black or yellow, in an unknown mix. You can bet on red, or on black, and you win if your color is drawn. Most people prefer to bet on red, where the odds are known, to black, where they aren't.

But then, in a second version, most people prefer a bet on black or yellow together, whose odds are known, to red or yellow, whose odds aren't. The two choices contradict each other if we think about probability consistently. The pattern is called ambiguity aversion: we dislike not knowing the odds, even when it makes no logical difference. It shapes how we invest, how we choose doctors, and what we're willing to try.

# A sure thing is special
@src Allais, Econometrica, 1953

In 1953 the French economist Maurice Allais proposed a problem that troubled the theory of rational choice. In one choice, people prefer a sure gain of a million dollars to a gamble with a high chance of getting five million and a small chance of getting nothing. In a second choice, between two gambles with small chances of winning, they prefer the one with the larger prize, though it's the same gamble with the chances scaled down.

These preferences are inconsistent with expected utility theory. Going from a 100 percent chance to 99 percent feels like a much bigger drop than from 11 percent to 10 percent. Kahneman and Tversky called this the certainty effect. It helps to explain why people pay a premium for insurance and warranties that remove the last bit of risk, and why a guarantee can be so attractive.

# Why a loss and a gain are not mirror images
@src Kahneman & Tversky, Econometrica, 1979

In 1979 Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky published a paper that changed economics, called prospect theory. They found that people don't judge outcomes by final wealth, as classical theory assumed, but as gains and losses relative to a reference point, often the status quo. Losses weigh more than gains. And we're risk-averse when choosing between gains, but risk-seeking between losses.

For example, most people take a sure gain of $500 over a coin flip for $1,000, but they prefer a coin flip to lose $1,000 over a sure loss of $500. The same amounts, framed differently, lead to opposite choices. Kahneman was awarded the 2002 Nobel Prize in economics for this work, and Tversky would have shared it had he not died in 1996. It gave a realistic picture of how people decide.

# When confidence is a poor guide
@src Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow, 2011

Kahneman told a story from his time in the Israeli army in the 1950s, when he was asked to design a way to select officer candidates. He and his colleagues watched soldiers in a group task and rated their leadership, and felt confident about their predictions. But when they checked how the soldiers did later, their predictions were barely better than guesses.

Yet, to his astonishment, he and his colleagues continued to feel just as confident about the next group. He called it the illusion of validity. A story that seems coherent makes us confident, whether or not it's true. The feeling of certainty comes from how well the evidence fits together, and not from how good the evidence is. It's a reason to keep track of your predictions, and to measure how often they are right.

# When to trust your gut
@src Kahneman & Klein, American Psychologist, 2009

Daniel Kahneman, who studied the errors of intuition, and Gary Klein, who studied the successes of expert intuition, such as firefighters who sense that a floor is about to collapse, seemed to be on opposite sides. In 2009 they wrote a paper together to find out where they agreed. They concluded that intuition can be trusted when two conditions are met.

First, the environment must be regular enough to be predictable, as in chess, firefighting or nursing. Second, the person must have had long practice and quick feedback, so that they've learned the patterns. In unpredictable fields, such as predicting political events or stocks, confident intuition isn't reliable, however experienced the person. A good test is to ask whether you've had many chances to learn, and whether the world gives clear feedback.

# Expert predictions
@src Tetlock, Expert Political Judgment, 2005

In 1984 Philip Tetlock began asking experts, such as academics, diplomats and journalists, to make predictions about world events, and he kept going for 20 years. He collected about 28,000 forecasts from 284 experts. When he checked the results, the experts did only a little better than random guesses, and not much better than a simple rule such as predicting that things would stay the same.

There was a pattern in who did better. Those he called hedgehogs, who knew one big thing and applied it to everything, did worse. The foxes, who knew many small things, drew from many sources and were willing to change their minds, did better. Fame and confidence went with poorer predictions. It's a call for humility about forecasts, and for keeping score.

# Simple formulas beat experts
@src Meehl, 1954; Grove et al., Psychological Assessment, 2000

In 1954 the psychologist Paul Meehl published a short book that caused an uproar. He compared predictions by clinicians, using their judgment, with predictions from simple statistical formulas based on the same information, such as predicting who would succeed in school or recover from illness. In nearly every case, the formulas did as well or better.

In 2000 William Grove and his colleagues combined 136 studies and confirmed it: mechanical predictions were as good as or better than expert judgment in almost all of them, and clearly better in many. Experts add value by knowing what to look at. But they tend to be inconsistent, swayed by moods and by irrelevant details. A good use of the finding is to let a checklist or a formula do the final combining, and keep human judgment for gathering the information.

# Smart people reason with their politics
@src Kahan, Peters, Dawson & Slovic, 2013, 2017
@conf mixed

In 2013 Dan Kahan and his colleagues asked people to look at a small table of data and say what it showed. In one version, the table was about whether a skin cream worked. In another, the same numbers were labeled as showing the effect of a ban on handguns on crime. People who were good at math did well on the skin cream version. On the gun version, they did much worse when the correct answer contradicted their politics.

The people with the best number skills, in other words, weren't more objective. They used their skills to find a way to the conclusion they wanted. The study is often used to argue that facts alone don't settle political arguments. The size of the effect is debated and there are replications of it with different results. But it's consistent with other work on motivated reasoning, and a reminder to ask whether you'd interpret the evidence the same way if it supported the other side.

# Why beliefs outlast their evidence
@src Ross, Lepper & Hubbard, JPSP, 1975

In 1975 Lee Ross and his colleagues gave students a task: to tell the difference between real suicide notes and fake ones. They gave the students feedback on how well they were doing, which was made up. Some were told that they were very good, and some that they were very bad. Then the researchers explained that the feedback had been completely random, and had nothing to do with their ability.

Even after they'd been told, the students who had been told they were good still believed that they were good at the task, and those who had been told that they were bad believed that they were bad. The beliefs had taken on a life of their own. It's called belief perseverance. Once we've built an explanation for something, it doesn't disappear when the evidence is withdrawn. That's a reason to be careful about the first story you tell about anything.

# The 2-4-6 puzzle
@src Wason, 1960

In 1960 Peter Wason showed people three numbers, 2, 4, 6, and said that they followed a rule. The task was to discover the rule by proposing other sets of three numbers, and being told whether each fit. Most people quickly guessed that the rule was "numbers increasing by two," and tested it with 8, 10, 12 and 20, 22, 24. Each time they were told yes.

They were confident and wrong. The real rule was just "any increasing numbers." They'd never tried a set that would break their idea, such as 1, 2, 3, or 5, 3, 1. We tend to test a hypothesis by looking for confirming examples, which can never prove it, and avoid looking for ones that could prove it false. The remedy is to ask, "What would show me I'm wrong?" and then go and look for that.

# The easy name
@src Alter & Oppenheimer, Psychological Science, 2006

Does it matter if a company has a name that's easy to say? In 2006 Adam Alter and Daniel Oppenheimer looked at companies that had recently gone public on the stock exchange. In the first day of trading, those with names that were easy to pronounce did better than those with hard names. The effect was modest and faded with time.

The researchers think that easy-to-process names feel familiar and trustworthy. It's part of a pattern called processing fluency: things that are easy for the mind to handle seem to be better, truer and safer. It affects how we judge claims, products and people. It's a good idea to ask yourself whether you like something because it's good, or because it's easy to grasp.

# How a statement is framed
@src Levin & Gaeth, Journal of Consumer Research, 1988

In 1988 Irwin Levin and Gary Gaeth asked people to taste ground beef and rate it. Some were told that it was "75 percent lean," and others that it was "25 percent fat." It's the same beef. The people who were given the lean label rated it as better tasting and less greasy.

Positive and negative framings of the same facts lead to different reactions, which is known as attribute framing. It's used by food companies, and also by doctors, when they talk about surgery as having a 90 percent survival rate or a 10 percent death rate. It's good to recognize that the way a fact is presented may change your reaction. A useful trick is to restate it in the opposite terms and see if it feels different.

# Near and far
@src Trope & Liberman, Psychological Review, 2010

In 2010 Yaacov Trope and Nira Liberman summarized a theory about how we think about things that are close or far away, in time, space or in terms of how likely they are, or how well we know the people involved. It's called construal level theory. Things that are far away are thought about abstractly, in terms of why and what for, while those that are close are seen concretely, in terms of how and the details.

For example, a trip next year is a dream of adventure and relaxation, while a trip next week is a list of tasks and tickets. We often make plans while our minds are in a far state and then are surprised by the details. It helps to think about the same decision in both ways, and to ask both why you're doing something and how you'll go about it.

# The wisdom of the crowd in your own head
@src Vul & Pashler, Psychological Science, 2008

The wisdom of crowds says that the average of many people's guesses is often better than most individual guesses. In 2008 Edward Vul and Harold Pashler asked whether you could get a similar benefit from a single mind. They asked people questions, such as the year of a historical event, and then asked them to guess a second time, without telling them the first answer. The average of the two guesses was more accurate than either one alone.

The effect was larger if the second guess was made three weeks later, and not right away. The explanation is that each guess is a sample from what you know, with its own random error, and averaging cancels some of it. If you have to make an estimate, and you can, make it twice, with a break between, and use the average.
