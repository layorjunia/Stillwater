# Rats in a playground
@src Rosenzweig, Bennett & Diamond, Scientific American, 1972; Diamond et al., 1964

In the 1960s Mark Rosenzweig, Edward Bennett and Marian Diamond raised young rats in two different settings. Some lived in standard cages, alone or in pairs, with little to do. Others lived in larger cages with other rats and a changing set of toys, ladders and wheels. The environment was enriched.

After a few weeks, the researchers looked at the animals' brains. The rats from the enriched cages had a thicker cortex, and more connections between neurons, than those that had lived in bare cages. They also did better on maze tasks. It was one of the first clear demonstrations that the brain changes with experience, and that it does so in adults too. It was a break from the view that the brain was fixed after childhood.

# A GPS in the brain
@src O'Keefe & Dostrovsky, 1971; Hafting et al., Nature, 2005

In 1971 John O'Keefe recorded from single neurons in the hippocampus of rats as they ran around a box. He found cells that fired only when the rat was in one particular place, and not elsewhere. He called them place cells. Each cell seemed to stand for one location, as if the brain had a map of the room.

In 2005 May-Britt Moser, Edvard Moser and their colleagues found a second kind of cell in a neighboring region. These grid cells fire at many points arranged in a regular triangular pattern across the space, like a lattice laid over the floor. Together, the cells seem to make a system of positioning. O'Keefe and the Mosers shared the Nobel Prize in 2014. The same region is damaged early in Alzheimer's disease, which may explain why people with the disease get lost.

# The Jennifer Aniston neuron
@src Quiroga et al., Nature, 2005

In 2005 Rodrigo Quiroga and his colleagues recorded from single neurons in the brains of patients with epilepsy, who had electrodes in place before surgery. They showed the patients many pictures of people, places and objects. Then they looked for cells that fired. One cell in one patient fired when the patient saw different pictures of the actress Jennifer Aniston, and not when they saw other faces.

Other cells responded to Halle Berry, or to a particular building, and some also responded to the person's written name. The cells seem to represent a concept, not a particular image. They are called concept cells. The finding was a surprise, since it suggested that the brain can code for a particular idea with a small set of neurons. It doesn't mean that there's one neuron for one person. Many cells probably work together to represent each idea.

# Neurons that see edges
@src Hubel & Wiesel, Journal of Physiology, 1959

In 1959 David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel were recording from single neurons in the visual cortex of a cat. They tried to make a cell fire by showing it spots of light on a screen. Nothing worked for hours. Then, by accident, as they changed a glass slide in the projector, a cell burst into activity. It was responding to the edge of the slide as it moved across the screen.

They went on to show that neurons in the primary visual cortex respond to lines of a particular angle, and to movement in a particular direction. Higher up the pathway, cells responded to more complex shapes. The visual system builds a picture in stages, from simple pieces to whole objects. Hubel and Wiesel won the Nobel Prize in 1981. It's a good example of a discovery that came from a mistake, and from patient attention.

# How learning to read changes the brain
@src Dehaene et al., Science, 2010

Reading is a recent invention. Humans have used writing for only a few thousand years, which is too short for the brain to have evolved a special region for it. So how do we read? In 2010 Stanislas Dehaene and his colleagues scanned the brains of people who had learned to read as adults, and of people of the same background who had never learned.

Learning to read changed the visual cortex. A region on the left side, now called the visual word form area, responded strongly to written words in people who could read. In people who couldn't, it was much less active, and the region responded to faces instead. The brain seems to recycle an area that is good at recognizing shapes, and to train it for letters. It shows that culture can reshape the brain, and that learning in adulthood can leave a physical mark.

# Do musicians have different brains?
@src Schlaug et al., Neuropsychologia, 1995
@conf mixed

In 1995 Gottfried Schlaug and his colleagues compared the brains of professional musicians with those of people who were not musicians. They found that a part of the corpus callosum, the thick band of fibers that connects the two halves of the brain, was larger in the musicians. The difference was greatest in those who had begun to study music before the age of seven.

It was taken as a sign that early music lessons change the brain. Later studies have found other differences, such as in the auditory cortex and in areas for finger movement. But it's hard to separate cause and effect. People who become musicians may have had different brains to start with. A few studies that followed children over time suggest that training does produce changes. The truth probably involves both.

# Teenagers and the audience
@src Gardner & Steinberg, Developmental Psychology, 2005

Why do teenagers take more risks when they're with friends? In 2005 Margo Gardner and Laurence Steinberg tested this with a computer driving game. Teenagers, college students and adults played alone or with two same-age friends watching. In the game, they decided whether to run a yellow light, and the risk was that a wall might appear and crash the car.

Teenagers took substantially more risks when their friends were watching than when they were alone. College-age students took more risks too, but not as many more. Adults were not much affected. The researchers suggested that the presence of peers activates the brain's reward system more strongly in adolescence. It's a reason why rules about passengers for new teen drivers save lives. It isn't that teenagers can't reason. It's that the audience changes what feels worth it.

# The brain before the aha
@src Jung-Beeman et al., PLoS Biology, 2004

You work on a puzzle, and the answer suddenly pops into your head, and it feels sure. In 2004 Mark Jung-Beeman and his colleagues studied what happens in the brain at that moment. People solved word puzzles in which three words are given, and the task is to find a fourth that goes with all three. Some solutions came gradually, and others came as a sudden flash of insight.

When the answer arrived as an insight, a burst of high-frequency electrical activity appeared over the right temporal lobe, about a third of a second before the solution. Before that, there had been a short pause of the visual areas, as if the brain were turning its attention inward. The finding suggests that insight is not just a feeling. It's a distinct process, and it may rely on the brain forming a distant connection on its own.
