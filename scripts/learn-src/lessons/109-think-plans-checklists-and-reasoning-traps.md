# The opera house that cost fourteen times the plan
@src Sydney Opera House; Flyvbjerg, studies of large projects

In 1957 a competition was held to design an opera house for Sydney, and it was won by a young Danish architect, Jørn Utzon, with a design of white shells on a harbor point. The government estimated that it would cost about 7 million Australian dollars. Construction began before the design for the shells had been worked out. The engineers had to solve how to build them, and it took years.

Costs climbed. In 1966, with the building not close to finished, the project had cost about 23 million dollars, and Utzon resigned, after a dispute over payments. The Opera House opened in October 1973, about ten years late, and at a cost of 102 million dollars, more than fourteen times the first estimate. The planner Bent Flyvbjerg, who studies large projects, has called it a standard example. Planners tend to underestimate the cost of what has not been done before, and to start before the hard parts are solved.

# The highway that cost nearly six times as much
@src Central Artery/Tunnel Project, Boston, 1982 to 2007

In the 1980s Boston began to bury its elevated highway, and to build a tunnel to the airport. The project was called the Central Artery/Tunnel Project, and most people called it the Big Dig. The first price tag was about 2.6 billion dollars. When it was finished in 2007, after two decades, the cost reported was close to 14.8 billion dollars, nearly six times as much, without counting the interest on the loans, which brought the total to far more.

There were also problems with quality. In July 2006, in a tunnel, a ceiling panel weighing several tons fell onto a car, and a passenger, Milena Del Valle, was killed. The investigators found that the wrong kind of epoxy had been used to hold the ceiling bolts. The state later reached a settlement with the contractors of more than 450 million dollars. The Big Dig now stands for how large public works can grow past their budgets.

# The list that stopped infections
@src Pronovost et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2006

Hospital patients in intensive care are often given a central line, a long tube placed in a large vein. It can carry germs into the bloodstream, and cause serious infection. Peter Pronovost, a critical care doctor at Johns Hopkins, wrote down five steps that the experts agreed on. They were washing hands, cleaning the patient's skin with a strong antiseptic, covering the patient completely with sterile drapes, avoiding one risky site, and removing the line when it was no longer needed.

Starting in 2003 he asked 103 intensive care units in Michigan to follow a checklist of those steps, with a nurse empowered to stop a doctor who skipped one. Within three months, the median rate of infection fell from 2.7 per 1,000 line-days to zero, and it stayed low for at least a year and a half. It was estimated to have saved many lives and a great deal of money. The steps were not new. The checklist made sure that they were done every time.

# The baseball team with the cheap roster
@src Oakland Athletics, 2002; Lewis, Moneyball, 2003

In 2002 the Oakland Athletics had one of the smallest payrolls in baseball, a fraction of what teams like the New York Yankees spent. Yet the A's won 103 games and the division, and at one point won twenty games in a row, which was the longest streak in the history of the American League. The general manager, Billy Beane, and his assistant, Paul DePodesta, had given up the old ways of judging players.

Scouts had favored young men who looked like athletes. Beane relied on data. He valued a statistic that scouts undervalued, which was on-base percentage, how often a batter reaches base, because it is closely linked to scoring runs. Players with good records on it were cheaper, since other teams paid less attention. Michael Lewis wrote about it in his book Moneyball. Other teams copied the approach, and the advantage shrank. It showed that an overlooked measure can be worth more than a conventional one.

# Telling a patient what a positive test means
@src Hoffrage & Gigerenzer, Academic Medicine, 1998

Suppose that one woman in a hundred has breast cancer. A mammogram detects 90 out of 100 who have it, and gives a false alarm to 9 out of 100 who do not. A woman has a positive test. What is the chance that she has cancer? Many people, including many doctors, say that it is about 90 percent. The correct answer is about nine percent, or about one in eleven.

The numbers are easier to handle in natural frequencies. Of 1,000 women, 10 have cancer, and 9 of them test positive. Of the 990 without, about 89 test positive. So of about 98 positives, 9 have cancer. In 1998 Ulrich Hoffrage and Gerd Gigerenzer showed that doctors do much better with this kind of statement. In one study, 16 of 24 physicians found the right answer when the information was put in natural frequencies. The way that numbers are shown can make us confused, or clear.

# The cards that people get wrong
@src Wason, 1966; Cosmides, Cognition, 1989

Here are four cards, which show E, K, 4 and 7. Each has a letter on one side and a number on the other. The rule is that if a card has a vowel on one side, it has an even number on the other. Which cards must you turn over to check the rule? Most people pick E and 4. The right answer is E and 7, because a 7 with a vowel behind it would break the rule. Fewer than one person in five gets it right.

In 1982 Richard Griggs and James Cox found that when the same logic was dressed as a rule about drinking, most people got it right. The rule was that if a person is drinking beer, he or she must be over a certain age. In 1989 the psychologist Leda Cosmides argued that people are good at detecting cheaters in social agreements. The logic is the same. It is a reminder that reasoning depends on content and context.

# When the conclusion is believable
@src Evans, Barston & Pollard, Memory and Cognition, 1983

A syllogism is an argument with two premises and a conclusion. Whether it is valid depends only on its form. Here is an invalid one. No addictive things are inexpensive. Some cigarettes are inexpensive. Therefore some cigarettes are not addictive. The conclusion is believable, but it does not follow. Many people accept it, since it fits what they know.

In 1983 Jonathan Evans, Julie Barston and Paul Pollard gave people such arguments, some valid and some invalid, some with believable conclusions and some not. People accepted 89 percent of valid arguments with believable conclusions, and 71 percent of invalid ones with believable conclusions. They accepted 56 percent of valid arguments with unbelievable conclusions, and just 10 percent of invalid ones with unbelievable conclusions. Belief is a strong influence, mostly on arguments that are flawed. It shows why we should be most careful with conclusions that we like.

# The bat, the ball, and the lily pad
@src Frederick, Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2005

A bat and a ball cost 1.10 dollars in all. The bat costs a dollar more than the ball. How much does the ball cost? Most people think of 10 cents right away, and it is wrong. The ball costs 5 cents, since the bat then costs 1.05, and the two make 1.10. It is the first of three questions that the economist Shane Frederick used in 2005 as a cognitive reflection test.

In a second question, five machines make five widgets in five minutes. How long do 100 machines take to make 100? The answer is five minutes, not 100. In the third, a patch of lily pads doubles every day, and covers a whole lake in 48 days. How long to cover half? The answer is 47, and not 24. Across 35 studies with 3,428 people, the average score was 1.24 out of three. The test measures whether you stop to check a first impulse.

# Sentences that sound deep and say nothing
@src Pennycook et al., Judgment and Decision Making, 2015

The philosopher Harry Frankfurt wrote an essay in 1986, On Bullshit, which argued that bullshit is not the same as lying. A liar cares about the truth, since he wants to hide it. A bullshitter does not care whether what he says is true. He wants to impress. In 2015 Gordon Pennycook and his colleagues asked how readily people accept it. They made up sentences from fashionable words, such as wholeness quiets infinite phenomena, which are grammatical but have no meaning.

People were asked to rate how profound they were, alongside real quotations. Many rated the empty sentences as at least somewhat profound. Those who did so scored lower on tests of analytic thinking, and higher on belief in the supernatural. People who were good at spotting the empty ones were not just doubters of everything. They could tell the difference. Vagueness can sound wise, and thinking carefully is some protection.
