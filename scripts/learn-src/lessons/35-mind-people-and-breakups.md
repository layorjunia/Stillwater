# When prophecy fails
@src Festinger, Riecken & Schachter, When Prophecy Fails, 1956
@conf mixed

In 1954 a small group in the American Midwest believed the world would end in a great flood on December 21. A woman in the group said she was receiving messages from beings in space who would rescue the believers by flying saucer at midnight. The psychologist Leon Festinger and his colleagues joined the group in secret to see what would happen when the prophecy failed.

Midnight passed and nothing came. After a few hours of distress, the leader announced that the group's faith had saved the world. The members, who had expected to keep quiet, began to seek publicity. Festinger saw it as dissonance. People who had given up jobs and homes found it easier to rethink the evidence than to admit a huge mistake. Later scholars found that other failed prophecies didn't always lead to more recruiting, so it's a vivid case, not a rule.

# From Jerusalem to Jericho
@src Darley & Batson, JPSP, 1973

In 1973 John Darley and Daniel Batson studied seminary students, people training for the ministry. Each student was asked to give a short talk, some on the parable of the Good Samaritan, and was sent to another building to give it. On the way, they passed a man slumped in a doorway, coughing and groaning. Would they stop?

What mattered was whether they were in a hurry. Students who were told that they were late stopped to help only about ten percent of the time, while those who weren't rushed stopped about six times in ten. Whether they were about to talk about the Good Samaritan made little difference. The study is a classic case of the power of a situation. Good intentions can give way when we are hurried, and we don't always notice why.

# The nurses and the phone call
@src Hofling et al., 1966; Rank & Jacobson, 1977
@conf mixed

In 1966 Charles Hofling and his colleagues phoned nurses on hospital wards. A man who said he was a doctor, whom the nurses didn't know, told each one to give a patient twice the maximum safe dose of a drug. The order broke several hospital rules. In the study, nearly all of the nurses started to do it, and were stopped by an observer.

The study was cited as a powerful example of obedience to authority. But in 1977 Lawrence Rank and Alan Jacobson ran a version in which the drug was a familiar one, and the nurses could talk with colleagues. Very few of them obeyed. The difference suggests that people may follow orders when they are uncertain or unsupported, and that knowledge and a chance to ask someone else can make a large difference.

# Venting doesn't help
@src Bushman, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2002

Many people believe that it helps to let your anger out, by hitting a pillow or shouting. In 2002 Brad Bushman tested this. Students were insulted by another person, and then were told to hit a punching bag. Some were asked to think of the person who had insulted them while they did it. Others were told to think of getting fit. A third group sat quietly.

Afterward, all of them had a chance to blast the other person with loud noise. Those who had hit the bag while thinking about the person were the most aggressive. Those who sat quietly were the least. Venting seems to keep the anger going, and it rehearses it. Things that lower arousal, such as slow breathing or a walk, tend to work better for calming down.

# Learning what you think by watching what you do
@src Bem, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 1972

We assume that our attitudes come first and our actions follow. In 1972 Daryl Bem proposed that it sometimes works the other way round. When we're not sure what we feel, we figure it out by watching what we do, in the same way that we'd read another person. If you find that you keep going to a place, you may conclude that you like it.

Bem supported the idea by showing that outside observers could guess people's attitudes well just by hearing what they did. It fits other findings, such as the effect of doing small favors. The idea has a practical side. If you want to feel more generous or more committed, it can help to act that way first. Our feelings are partly an interpretation of our own behavior.

# Babies and the helpful shape
@src Hamlin, Wynn & Bloom, Nature, 2007
@conf mixed

In 2007 Kiley Hamlin and her colleagues showed babies a simple play. A shape with eyes tried to climb a hill. One shape helped it by pushing it up, and another got in its way and pushed it down. Afterward, the babies were offered the two shapes to reach for. Babies of six and ten months mostly reached for the helper.

It was taken as a sign that even babies have a sense of good and bad behavior. Some later studies have found the same thing, and some have not been able to repeat it, and there is a lively debate about what the babies understand. They may be drawn to the shape that does what they expect, or to one that moves in a pleasing way. The question of how early morality starts is still open.

# Toddlers who help
@src Warneken & Tomasello, Science, 2006

In 2006 Felix Warneken and Michael Tomasello tested whether very young children will help a stranger without being asked or rewarded. An adult dropped a marker on the floor and reached for it without success, or couldn't open a cupboard because his hands were full. The children, who were eighteen months old, did not have to be asked.

Most of them picked up the marker and handed it back, or opened the door, and they did it more than when the adult was not trying to reach it. Chimpanzees also helped in some of the tests, though less. The study suggests that helping starts very early, and that it doesn't depend on being taught or rewarded. It seems that children have a natural wish to help, before language is fully there.

# The check-in message
@src Liu, Rim, Min & Min, JPSP, 2023

Have you ever thought of texting an old friend, and then held back? In 2023 Peggy Liu and her colleagues ran thirteen experiments with several thousand people to look at how we judge such messages. People who reached out, by a note, a call or a small gift, guessed how much the other person would like it. The other people were asked how it felt to receive it.

The senders consistently underestimated how much the receivers appreciated it. The reason seemed to be that senders thought about how awkward or ordinary the message was, while receivers were pleased and surprised that someone had thought of them. The effect was weaker when the contact was expected. It's a good reason to send the message. It will probably mean more to them than you think.

# Who am I without you?
@src Slotter, Gardner & Finkel, PSPB, 2010

After a breakup, many people say that they feel lost, and not just sad. In 2010 Erica Slotter, Wendi Gardner and Eli Finkel tested this in several studies. They found that people's sense of who they were became less clear after a romantic breakup. They were less sure of their own traits and more confused about themselves.

The loss of clarity was linked to more distress, and not just to the loss of the partner. In one study, people who wrote about their breakup used more words like confused and uncertain than people who wrote about other events. It makes sense. In a close relationship, much of how we see ourselves is bound up with the other person. It helps to know that the feeling of being lost is common, and that rebuilding a clear sense of yourself takes time.

# Being kind to yourself after a separation
@src Sbarra, Smith & Mehl, Psychological Science, 2012

In 2012 David Sbarra, Hillary Smith and Matthias Mehl followed over a hundred adults who had recently separated from their spouses. At the start, each person talked for four minutes about their feelings about the separation. Trained listeners rated how self-compassionate the talk was. Did the person treat themselves kindly, see the experience as part of ordinary human life, and avoid exaggerating the pain?

People who were rated higher in self-compassion at the start had less distress about the separation over the next nine months. This was true even after taking into account other traits, such as how neurotic they were, and how bad the separation was. The study can't prove that self-compassion caused the better outcome. But it fits a good deal of other research. Talking to yourself as you would to a friend is a skill that can be practiced.

# Time does not heal all wounds
@src Lucas, Psychological Science, 2005

Does time heal after a divorce? In 2005 Richard Lucas studied the answers of thousands of people in Germany who had been asked about their life satisfaction every year, for as long as eighteen years. He looked at what happened to those who divorced.

On average, satisfaction fell as the divorce got close, and then rose again over the years. But on average it didn't quite return to where it had been before. Some of the gap came from before the divorce, since people who divorced were less satisfied even earlier. And averages hide variety. Some people were happier after divorce than they had been before, and others never fully recovered. The title says it well. Time helps most people, but not everyone, and not all the way.

# Looking at your ex online
@src Marshall, Cyberpsychology, Behavior and Social Networking, 2012
@conf mixed

After a breakup, it's tempting to look at an ex's social media. In 2012 Tara Marshall surveyed people who had ended a romantic relationship, and asked how often they checked their former partner's profile. Those who checked more often reported more distress, more longing for the ex and less personal growth.

The study can't tell us which way the link goes. People who are struggling may look more, or looking may make them struggle more. But other work also suggests that continued exposure keeps the attachment alive. Many people find it helpful to mute or unfollow for a while, to make it easier to heal. It's not a rule, and there are times when contact is needed, for example when children are involved. But the evidence leans toward less looking.

# Rebounds may not be so bad
@src Brumbaugh & Fraley, Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, 2015
@conf mixed

A rebound relationship has a bad reputation. People are warned that jumping into a new relationship quickly after a breakup is a mistake. In 2015 Claudia Brumbaugh and Chris Fraley surveyed people who had recently ended a relationship, and compared those who had started something new quickly with those who had not.

The ones who started dating sooner reported feeling better on several measures. They reported a higher sense of well-being, and they were less attached to the ex. The study was a survey, so it can't show that the new relationship was the cause, since people who recover faster may also date sooner. The result is a reminder that the usual warnings are not well supported. How ready you are matters more than how long you've waited.

# When parents' conflict does the harm
@src Amato, Loomis & Booth, Social Forces, 1995

Do children do better if their parents stay together? In 1995 Paul Amato, Laura Loomis and Alan Booth followed a large sample of families for many years. They asked how young adults were doing, and whether their parents had divorced, and how much the parents had fought.

The answer depended on the level of conflict. When parents had a high-conflict marriage, children did better after divorce than if the marriage continued. When the marriage had been low in conflict, the children did worse if it ended. The researchers noted that many marriages that end are the high-conflict kind, but not all. These are averages, and each child is different, but it suggests that ongoing conflict between parents is more damaging than the split.

# Sleepless nights, harsher fights
@src Gordon & Chen, Social Psychological and Personality Science, 2014

In 2014 Amie Gordon and Serena Chen studied how sleep affects conflict in close relationships. In a diary study, couples recorded how well they slept and how much they argued. After nights of poor sleep, people reported more conflict with their partner the next day, and they were less able to resolve it.

The researchers suggested that tiredness makes it harder to see things from the other person's point of view and to hold back from an angry reply. It isn't a reason to excuse bad behavior, but it's a useful rule. If you're both exhausted, a serious talk may go better after a night's rest.

# Hungry and angry
@src Bushman, DeWall, Pond & Hanus, PNAS, 2014
@conf mixed

In 2014 Brad Bushman and his colleagues asked more than a hundred married couples to take part for three weeks. Each person measured their blood sugar every morning and evening, and each evening stuck pins into a voodoo doll that represented their spouse, to show how angry they were. They could use as many pins as they liked, from zero to fifty-one.

The lower a person's blood sugar was, the more pins they used. In a second part, those with low glucose also blasted their spouse with louder noise in a game. The idea is that the brain needs fuel for self-control. Other studies on glucose and willpower have been mixed, so the explanation isn't settled. But snacking before a hard conversation can't hurt, and it is a cheap thing to try.

# Moving in step
@src Wiltermuth & Heath, Psychological Science, 2009
@conf mixed

In 2009 Scott Wiltermuth and Chip Heath had groups of students do things together. Some walked around the campus in time with each other, or sang a song in unison. Others walked or sang without trying to match. Afterward, everyone played a game in which they could cooperate or act selfishly.

The groups who had moved in sync were more willing to cooperate, even when it cost them something. The idea is that acting in sync makes people feel like a unit. It has been suggested as one reason why armies march, congregations sing, and teams do drills together. Some later studies have found smaller effects, but the idea fits a wide body of work. Doing something in time with others can create a feeling of belonging.

# Dressed for authority
@src Bickman, Journal of Applied Social Psychology, 1974

In 1974 Leonard Bickman ran an experiment on a New York street. A man stopped people and gave an order, for example to pick up a paper bag or to give a coin to a stranger standing by a parking meter. Sometimes he was dressed in a regular jacket and tie, sometimes as a milkman, and sometimes as a security guard.

Passersby were most likely to obey when he was in the guard's uniform. They obeyed even when he walked away and couldn't check. The uniform had a power that the man didn't have. We tend to react to symbols of authority more than to the actual person, and we often do it without thinking. It's worth asking, when you're about to do as you're told, who is telling you and why.

# Do habits spread between friends?
@src Christakis & Fowler, New England Journal of Medicine, 2007
@conf contested

In 2007 Nicholas Christakis and James Fowler analyzed a very large study that had followed thousands of people over thirty years. They found that a person's chance of becoming obese rose by around 57 percent if a friend became obese. The effect was also seen for siblings and spouses. They proposed that behaviors and norms spread through social networks.

The idea became popular, and it has also been criticized. Friends tend to be alike to begin with, and they share environments, so it's hard to separate influence from similarity. Some critics argue that the methods can't show contagion at all. The question matters. Our habits are surely shaped by the people around us. How much, and how it works, is still debated.

# The monkey and the cucumber
@src Brosnan & de Waal, Nature, 2003
@conf mixed

In 2003 Sarah Brosnan and Frans de Waal trained capuchin monkeys to hand over a small token in return for a slice of cucumber. The monkeys were happy to do it. Then a monkey in the next cage was given a grape, which they like much better, for the same task. The first monkey was still given cucumber.

Many of the monkeys that saw the other get a grape refused to eat, and some threw the cucumber out of the cage. The researchers interpreted it as a sense of unfairness, like the human dislike of unequal pay. Others have suggested that the monkeys were just reacting to seeing a better reward, and not to inequity as such. The debate continues, but the video is a good illustration of how much we compare.
