# Sandwich
@src Oxford English Dictionary; Rodger, The Insatiable Earl, 1993

The sandwich is named after John Montagu, the fourth Earl of Sandwich, an English nobleman who lived in the eighteenth century. The most common story is that he was so absorbed in a card game that he asked for meat between two slices of bread, so that he could eat without leaving the table or getting his fingers greasy. Others at the table began to order what he was having.

The earl's own life was busy, as he was First Lord of the Admiralty, and some historians think that he ate at his desk. Either way, the name is well documented from the 1760s. The earl also gave his name to the Sandwich Islands, now Hawaii, which Captain Cook named in his honor. A man known for a snack and for the sea has two places that bear his title.

# Cardigan
@src Oxford English Dictionary

The cardigan is a knitted jacket that buttons down the front, and it's named after James Brudenell, the seventh Earl of Cardigan. He was a British cavalry officer who led the Charge of the Light Brigade in 1854, during the Crimean War. It was a doomed attack that became famous through a poem by Tennyson.

The knitted waistcoat came into fashion in the years after the war. According to the usual explanation, officers wore knitted garments like this one to keep out the cold, and it's said that the earl's example gave the garment its name. The exact route is hard to prove, but the dictionary records the word from the 1860s. Another garment, the raglan sleeve, comes from the name of another commander in the same war, Lord Raglan.

# Silhouette
@src Oxford English Dictionary

A silhouette is a dark outline, like a cut-paper portrait. The word comes from Étienne de Silhouette, who was the French finance minister in 1759. He tried to save the government money with harsh taxes and cuts, and he was widely mocked. Anything cheap or done on the cheap came to be called à la Silhouette.

Cut-paper portraits were an inexpensive alternative to painted ones, and they were popular in the years that followed. They became known by his name, probably in a sneering way. The word spread and widened to mean any dark shape against a light background. The minister was in office for less than a year, but he is remembered every time someone sees a shape against the sunset.

# Mesmerize
@src Oxford English Dictionary

To be mesmerized is to be held spellbound. The word comes from Franz Anton Mesmer, an Austrian doctor who worked in Vienna and Paris in the late eighteenth century. He claimed to cure illness by moving magnets, and later his hands, over patients. He said that an invisible fluid, which he called animal magnetism, flowed through the body.

In 1784 a commission in France, which included Benjamin Franklin and the chemist Antoine Lavoisier, tested his claims. They found that the effects came from the patients' imagination and not from any fluid. The treatment was discredited, but it left a legacy. Mesmer's technique was an early form of what became hypnosis, and his name survives in the English language as a word for fascination.

# Nicotine
@src Oxford English Dictionary

The chemical in tobacco that causes addiction is named after Jean Nicot, a French diplomat. In the 1550s he was the French ambassador in Lisbon. There, he heard of the tobacco plant, which had come from the Americas, and he sent seeds and leaves to the French court. He promoted it as a medicine, and it was said to cure headaches and other ailments.

The plant was named Nicotiana in his honor, and when the active chemical was isolated in the early nineteenth century, it was called nicotine. Nicot was also the author of one of the first dictionaries of the French language. A man who compiled words gave his name to a plant, and a drug that killed millions.

# Maverick
@src Oxford English Dictionary

A maverick is a person who doesn't go along with a group. The word comes from Samuel Maverick, a Texas lawyer and landowner in the nineteenth century. Maverick didn't brand his cattle, unlike his neighbors. One account says that he did it out of disinterest, and another that he did it deliberately to claim any unbranded cattle as his.

Neighbors started to call any unbranded calf a maverick, since it belonged to no one. Gradually, the word came to describe an unbranded animal that strayed from the herd, and from there, a person who is independent in thought. It has appeared in English since the 1860s. It's one of the few words to have begun as a rancher's habit.

# Guillotine
@src Oxford English Dictionary

The guillotine is named after Joseph-Ignace Guillotin, a French physician. He did not invent it, and he did not die by it, though many people think that he did. In 1789 Guillotin, who was a member of the National Assembly, proposed that executions should be quick and painless, and the same for all classes. At the time, nobles were beheaded, and commoners hanged or broken on the wheel.

A surgeon named Antoine Louis designed a machine for the purpose, and a German engineer built it. The public began to call it the guillotine, which upset Guillotin, whose family later petitioned to change the name. They couldn't, so they changed their own. He died in 1814 of natural causes. It's an odd fate to be attached to a machine that you only hoped would be kinder.

# Dollar
@src Oxford English Dictionary

The word dollar goes back to a silver coin from a town in Bohemia, in what is now the Czech Republic. In the early sixteenth century, silver was mined in the valley of Joachimsthal, which means Joachim's valley. The mint there struck large silver coins called Joachimsthalers. People shortened it to thaler.

The coin was widely used across Europe, and the word traveled. In Dutch it was daler, and in English it became dollar. The Spanish silver coin, called the peso, was known as the Spanish dollar, and was used in the American colonies. When the United States chose a name for its currency in 1792, it took the familiar word. A mining town in Bohemia gave a name to the money of many countries.

# Lunatic
@src Oxford English Dictionary

A lunatic once meant someone whose mind was thought to be affected by the Moon. The word comes from the Latin luna, meaning moon. In ancient and medieval times, many people believed that the phases of the Moon caused madness, epilepsy and other disorders. The word has been in English since the thirteenth century.

The belief was so strong that it lasted for centuries. Studies of whether psychiatric admissions or crime rise at full moon have generally found no effect. The phrase lunatic fringe and the word loony, a shortened form of lunatic, came later. The word is now rarely used in medicine, since it's considered offensive. But it's a record of an old idea that the sky could control the mind.

# Vaccine
@src Oxford English Dictionary

The word vaccine comes from the Latin vacca, meaning cow. In 1796 the English doctor Edward Jenner noticed that milkmaids who had caught cowpox, a mild disease, did not get smallpox. He tested the idea by taking material from a cowpox sore on a milkmaid, and putting it into a scratch on a boy's arm. The boy later resisted smallpox.

Jenner called his method vaccination, from variolae vaccinae, the cow's pox. Later, Louis Pasteur extended the term to protection against other diseases, in honor of Jenner. The name has stuck for more than two hundred years. A word for a cow now stands for one of the most important medical advances in history. Smallpox was declared eradicated in 1980.

# Gossip
@src Oxford English Dictionary

The word gossip comes from the Old English godsibb, meaning a godparent, from god and sibb, a relative. It meant a person who is close, a sponsor at a baptism. Over time, it came to mean a close friend, usually a woman, and especially one who came to be with a woman at a birth.

By the sixteenth century, the word had taken on the meaning of someone who chatters idly about other people's affairs. It makes sense, since close friends are the ones who talk about everyone. Later, the word came to mean the talk itself, and not just the person who does it. The history of the word is also a history of the way that people who are close tend to share news about others.

# Villain
@src Oxford English Dictionary

In medieval times, a villain was a farm worker who was tied to a lord's estate. The word comes from the Latin villa, a country house or farm. A villein was a peasant, of a lower rank than a free man, and worked on the land of his lord. He was not necessarily wicked.

Because the nobles looked down on peasants, the word came to mean a person who was rude and low in behavior. By the sixteenth century, it meant a person who is wicked. The word shows how an insult can become a word for a crime, when a powerful group describes a weaker one. Words like churl and boor changed in similar ways, from a social rank to a flaw of character.

# Checkmate
@src Oxford English Dictionary

In chess, checkmate means that the king can't escape capture, and the game is over. The word comes from Persian, shāh māt, which is usually translated as the king is helpless, or the king is at a loss. Chess came to Europe from Persia, after reaching there from India, where the game began.

Some people say that the phrase meant the king is dead. It's a popular story, but the more likely meaning is that the king is defeated. The word came into Old French as eschec mat, and then into English. The word check, which gives a warning that the king is under attack, comes from the same Persian word for king. So the game's language has been traveling for over a thousand years.

# Algebra
@src Oxford English Dictionary; Katz, A History of Mathematics, 2009

The word algebra comes from Arabic, al-jabr, which means restoring or completion. It appears in the title of a book written by the mathematician al-Khwarizmi in Baghdad in about the year 820. The book is called Al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wa'l-muqabala, which means The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing.

Al-jabr referred to the operation of moving a negative term to the other side of an equation, to make it positive. The book set out rules for solving linear and quadratic equations, using words and geometry, and not symbols. The Latin translation of the book, in the twelfth century, introduced the word to Europe. It gave us both algebra and, from the author's name, the word algorithm.

# Ketchup
@src Oxford English Dictionary

Ketchup began as a fermented fish sauce. The word probably comes from the Hokkien Chinese kê-tsiap, a sauce made of fish brine. Sailors and traders brought it to Southeast Asia, and then to Britain in the seventeenth century. The British made their own versions, with mushrooms, walnuts and anchovies.

There was no tomato in ketchup until the late eighteenth century. Early American recipes, from about 1800, began to use tomatoes, and in the nineteenth century, the sweet tomato version became the standard. Sugar was added in large amounts when it became a commercial product. The sauce you put on fries began its life as the salty liquid of fermented fish, on the other side of the world.

# Salary
@src Oxford English Dictionary
@conf mixed

The word salary comes from the Latin salarium, which is connected to sal, meaning salt. Many people say that Roman soldiers were paid in salt, which is why we speak of being worth our salt. That story is popular, but historians are not sure that it's right. Pliny the Elder wrote that the word came from salt, but the Romans paid soldiers in coins.

Another explanation is that the salarium was an allowance to buy salt, which was a basic necessity and an item of trade. Or it may have been the money for the soldiers to travel on the Via Salaria, the salt road, to Rome. The connection with salt is real, but the details are disputed. It's a good example of a word whose popular story is more certain than the evidence.
