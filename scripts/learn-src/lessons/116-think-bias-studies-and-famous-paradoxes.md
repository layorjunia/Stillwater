# Why one child moves us more than a thousand
@series mind
@conf mixed
@src Small, Loewenstein & Slovic, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 2007

The psychologist Paul Slovic noticed that people who will give to help one named child often feel little when told of thousands in need. He called the effect compassion fade, and he and Deborah Small wrote of a collapse of compassion. The usual explanation is that feeling depends on being able to picture a victim, and that a crowd is hard to picture.

In 2007 Small, George Loewenstein and Slovic ran four field experiments with ordinary citizens. In one, people were shown an appeal about Rokia, a seven-year-old girl in Mali. When her story was given with statistics about the many others in need, donations went down and not up.

Then they tried teaching people about the bias. When people were prompted to think carefully, they gave less to the single identified child, but they did not give more to the statistical group. Information drove down the feeling and did nothing to replace it. The authors found one small gain. People who were told about the effect became more consistent in how they treated the two kinds of victims.

# Whose fault is the mess?
@series mind
@src Ross & Sicoly, J. Personality and Social Psychology, 1979

Ask each member of a couple what share of the housework he or she does, and add up the answers. Often the total is more than 100 percent. In 1979 the psychologists Michael Ross and Fiore Sicoly studied this, and called it an egocentric bias.

They asked 37 married couples to estimate their own responsibility for a list of household activities, such as preparing breakfast, shopping and making important decisions. They also studied discussion groups of students, intercollegiate basketball players and groups in the laboratory. In each case people remembered their own contributions to a joint product more easily than those of others, and they claimed more responsibility for it.

The reason they gave is not vanity. It is availability. Your own efforts are vivid to you, since you were there for each one, while those of a partner happen out of your view. You remember what you did, and you do not remember what you did not see. A popular account of the study notes that couples even overclaimed responsibility for causing arguments. The sensible response is to expect that everyone feels they do more than their share.

# The vaccine that frightens parents more than the disease
@series think
@src Ritov & Baron, J. Behavioral Decision Making, 1990

Imagine a flu that is expected to kill 10 children in every 10,000. A vaccine prevents the flu, but it can itself kill some children. Would you vaccinate your child? In 1990 Ilana Ritov and Jonathan Baron put questions of this kind to people, and many chose not to vaccinate even when the vaccine's risk was lower than the risk of the disease.

In their later work, many people preferred not to vaccinate when the disease killed 10 in 10,000 and the vaccine killed 5 in 10,000 or fewer. A child who dies of the vaccine would be a result of something the parent did. A child who dies of the flu is a result of something the parent did not do. People judge harm from action as worse than harm from inaction. The pattern is called omission bias.

It is not universal. The authors noted that the effect can reverse when the person making the choice is seen as responsible for those affected, such as a doctor, who may feel pushed to act. The lesson is that doing nothing is also a choice with consequences. If the numbers say that acting reduces harm, the feeling that inaction is safer is a bias.

# How a TV show changed how men rated a photo
@series mind
@conf mixed
@src Kenrick & Gutierres, J. Personality and Social Psychology, 1980

A contrast effect is a change in how we judge something because of what we saw just before. A gray square looks lighter after a dark one. The oldest known description is by the eleventh-century scholar Ibn al-Haytham, who noticed that spots of paint look darker against a white background and paler on a black one.

In 1980 Douglas Kenrick and Sara Gutierres studied it with people. Male students who were watching the television show Charlie's Angels, whose three main characters were very attractive, were asked to rate a photograph of a woman. They rated her as less attractive than did men who had been watching another program.

Nothing about the woman had changed. The standard in the men's heads had. The finding is used to explain why people may feel less satisfied with their own appearance, or their partner's, after looking at the pictures in magazines and on screens. It applies to many things. A moderate price looks cheap after a very high one, and a good grade looks poor if the last paper was great.

# The same proposal, three different senders
@series think
@src Ross & Stillinger, 1988; Stillinger et al., 1991

A proposal can be judged on its merits, or on who made it. In the late 1980s Lee Ross and Constance Stillinger called the second habit reactive devaluation, in which a proposal is thought worse when it seems to come from an opponent.

In an experiment, pedestrians in the United States were asked about a plan to reduce nuclear arms on both sides. When told that the plan came from President Ronald Reagan, 90 percent said it would be favorable or even-handed for the United States. When told that it came from a group of unspecified policy analysts, 80 percent said so. When told that it came from the Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev, only 44 percent did.

The plan was the same in each case. Other studies found the same thing. Israeli participants rated a peace proposal lower when told that it came from the Palestinian side than when told that it was an Israeli proposal. It is a problem for negotiation, since a good offer from a rival may be rejected only because of its source. One remedy is to have a neutral party present it.

# The fire that was not caused by the closet
@series think
@src Johnson & Seifert, J. Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory and Cognition, 1994

When a news report is corrected, people do not always correct their thinking. In 1994 Hollyn Johnson and Colleen Seifert tested this with a story about a warehouse fire. Participants read a series of messages. Early on, it was said that the fire had probably been started by volatile materials, such as paint and gas cylinders, that were carelessly stored in a closet.

Later in the story, they were told that the closet had in fact been empty. Afterward the participants were asked questions about the fire. Even people who clearly remembered the correction still gave answers that used the false cause, such as saying that the fire was so intense because of the stored materials.

This is called the continued influence effect. A false explanation fills a gap in our understanding, and when it is taken away, the gap remains. Later research has found that the effect is robust across stories, and it suggests that a correction works better when it gives a replacement explanation. People would rather have a wrong account than no account.

# The child who sees an insult in a smile
@series mind
@src Nasby, Hayden & DePaulo, 1980; Dodge and colleagues

Suppose that two children are laughing as you walk by. One child thinks that they are enjoying a joke. Another thinks they are laughing at him. Psychologists call the second kind of thinking hostile attribution bias, a tendency to read hostile intent into behavior that is ambiguous or harmless.

The term was coined in 1980 by Nasby, Hayden and DePaulo. They, and others such as Kenneth Dodge, had noticed that a group of children more often took ambiguous social situations to be hostile. In young children this is common and normal. But those who keep a high level of it through childhood are much more likely to be aggressive, with hitting, verbal attacks or damaging a friendship.

Researchers think that the bias is one path by which other troubles lead to aggression. Children who are teased by peers or treated harshly at home may come to expect the worst from others, and then react in kind. Programs have been designed to help children consider other explanations for what a peer did, on the idea that the problem begins in the interpretation.

# Why we blame them and excuse us
@series mind
@src Pettigrew, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 1979

Suppose a member of your own group does something kind, and a member of another group does the same. Do you explain the two acts in the same way? In 1979 the psychologist Thomas Pettigrew said that people do not. He named the pattern the ultimate attribution error.

When someone from an outgroup does something bad, we tend to explain it by their character, as in that is the kind of people they are. When they do something good, we explain it away by the situation, luck, or special effort. For our own group the reverse holds. Good acts show good character, and bad acts are blamed on circumstances.

The pattern strengthens prejudice, since each act that is seen seems to confirm what was already believed. The research suggests that it shows up more strongly in Western cultures than in Eastern ones, and that strong emotions such as fear and anger can make it worse. Pettigrew built on the earlier work of Gordon Allport, and his point was that stereotypes persist partly because of the way we interpret what we see.

# More candy from a bigger scoop
@series mind
@src Geier, Rozin & Doros, Psychological Science, 2006

How do we decide how much to eat? In 2006 Andrew Geier, Paul Rozin and Gheorghe Doros proposed that part of the answer is unit bias. People tend to treat one unit of something as the right amount, whatever size the unit is.

In one study, people were offered Tootsie Rolls in a large or a small size, and could take as many as they wished. Those offered the large ones took and presumably ate much more by weight. With pretzels, the people given whole pretzels ate about twice as much as those given a half pretzel as the unit. In another study, people helped themselves to M&M's with either a large spoon or a small one, and took substantially more with the large spoon, still usually taking one scoop.

The authors thought that a cultural norm, to finish what is served and to take one portion, was behind the effect. It suggests why smaller packages and plates can help people eat less, as they would not make up the difference by taking more. Later researchers have compared unit bias with other effects, and have found that the picture is more complicated.

# Why the night sky is dark
@series sci
@conf mixed
@src Olbers, 1823; Poe, Eureka, 1848; Kelvin, 1901; Harrison, 1964

If the universe were infinite and full of stars, then in whatever direction you looked, your line of sight would eventually end on a star. The sky should be as bright as the surface of a star. The night sky is dark. This puzzle is called Olbers' paradox, after the German astronomer Heinrich Olbers, who wrote about it in 1823, though he was far from the first to raise it. Halley and Cheseaux had thought about it earlier.

Several people have been credited with the answer. Edgar Allan Poe, in his 1848 prose poem Eureka, suggested that the universe is only so old, so light from the most distant stars has not reached us yet. Some scientists say this was the first correct answer. Others credit Lord Kelvin, in 1901, with the first satisfactory treatment.

The modern view is that the finite age of the stars is the main reason. In 1964 Edward Harrison showed that this was what limits the brightness of the sky. The expansion of the universe also stretches the light of the most distant objects to redder wavelengths, and cuts its energy. A dark night sky is a clue that the universe has a finite history.

# The demon that could break the rules of heat
@series sci
@src Maxwell, 1871; Szilard, 1929; Landauer, 1961; Bennett, 1982

The second law of thermodynamics says that heat flows from hot to cold, and that disorder tends to increase. In his 1871 textbook on heat, James Clerk Maxwell described a thought experiment. A tiny being, later called a demon, guards a door between two chambers of gas. It lets fast molecules go one way, and slow ones the other, so that one side grows hot and the other cold, without any work. That would break the law.

In 1929 the physicist Leo Szilard simplified the problem to an engine with a single molecule. In 1961 Rolf Landauer showed that erasing one bit of information must give off at least a minimum amount of heat, equal to k times T times the natural log of 2. In 1982 Charles Bennett argued that this resolves the puzzle. The demon must record what it sees, and to keep working it must erase its memory, and the erasing pays the entropy cost.

This view has been tested in experiments with small systems, and it is widely accepted. Some physicists argue that it does not cover every version of the puzzle. Whatever the final word, the demon led to the idea that information is a physical thing, with a cost in energy.

# Two losing games that win together
@series num
@src Harmer & Abbott, Nature, 1999; Parrondo, 1996

Suppose that you are offered two games of chance, and each one loses money on average. Playing either one over and over would ruin you. Is there any way to win by alternating them? In 1999 the physicists Gregory Harmer and Derek Abbott showed that there is, in a result known as Parrondo's paradox, after Juan Parrondo, who discovered it in 1996.

Game A is a coin toss with a coin that is slightly biased against you. Game B uses two coins. If your money is a multiple of 3, you toss a coin with a very poor chance of winning, about one in ten, less a small bias. Otherwise you toss a coin with a good chance, about three in four, less the same bias. Played alone, each game loses. If you switch between them in a random or fixed order, you can win steadily.

The effect resembles a ratchet, where random motion in one direction is blocked and the other allowed. The result was published in Nature as Losing strategies can win by Parrondo's paradox. It has been debated whether it is truly a paradox. It is also of little use for investing in stocks, since the games are very special. It shows how combining two bad things can produce a good one.
