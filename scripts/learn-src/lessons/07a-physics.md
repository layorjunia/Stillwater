# The speed of light
@src BIPM definition of the meter

Light in empty space travels at exactly 299,792,458 meters per second. It's exact because we define it that way. Since 1983 the meter has been defined as the distance light covers in 1/299,792,458 of a second, so the speed fixes the length, and not the other way around.

It's also the same for every observer, however fast they're moving, and that is the strange fact that Einstein built relativity on. If you chase a beam of light at half its speed, it still races away from you at the full speed. Nothing that has mass can ever reach it, because the closer you get, the more energy it takes to speed up even a little more. It acts as a cosmic speed limit.

# Sunlight is old news
@src NASA

Light takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to get from the Sun to Earth. So when you look at the Sun, which you shouldn't do directly, you're seeing it as it was a little over eight minutes ago. If the Sun suddenly disappeared, we wouldn't know for those eight minutes.

The next-nearest star, Proxima Centauri, is just over four light-years away, so its light shows you the star as it was more than four years ago. Looking at the night sky is a way of looking into the past, and the farther the star, the older the picture. Some of the points of light you can see now are from stars that may no longer exist, though their light is still on its way.

# Newton's three laws
@src Newton, Principia (1687)

In 1687 Isaac Newton published three rules of motion in his book Principia. An object keeps doing what it's doing, staying still or moving in a straight line, unless a force acts on it. A force makes an object accelerate, more for a lighter object than for a heavy one. And forces come in pairs: when you push on something, it pushes back on you equally.

A rocket works through the third law. It throws gas backward, and the gas pushes the rocket forward. It doesn't need air to push against, which is why rockets work in space. That surprised people for a long time, since many assumed that a rocket would have nothing to push on. The same law is at work when you jump from a small boat and feel the boat slide away behind you.

# Feather and hammer
@src Apollo 15, NASA

Galileo is said to have shown that heavy and light objects fall together. On Earth, we don't see this with a feather, because air resistance holds it back. In 1971, astronaut David Scott tested it on the Moon, where there's no air.

He held a geologist's hammer and a falcon feather at shoulder height, let go, and both hit the lunar dust at the same moment, on live television. Without air, gravity treats everything the same. A heavier object is pulled harder, but it also takes more force to move it, and the two effects cancel out exactly. It's a very clean demonstration, and it's still a pleasure to watch the video.

# Conservation of energy
@src Helmholtz, 1847

Energy can't be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another. A pendulum swaps motion for height and back. A battery turns chemical energy into electricity and then into light or motion. A candle turns the energy stored in wax into heat and light.

Several scientists reached the idea in the 1840s, and Hermann von Helmholtz set out the law in its general form in 1847. It's one of the most reliable rules in physics, and no machine that gets energy from nothing has ever worked. That's why the patent offices turn away perpetual motion machines. Energy can seem to disappear, as it does when a ball stops bouncing, but it has only been turned into heat, in tiny amounts, spread everywhere.

# Absolute zero
@src Kelvin scale; NIST

The lowest temperature there could be is about minus 273.15 degrees Celsius, or zero on the Kelvin scale. At that point, the particles in a substance would have as little motion as physics allows. Nothing can be colder, since temperature is a measure of how much the particles move.

Scientists have cooled atoms to within a billionth of a degree above it, but the laws of thermodynamics say you can't reach it exactly. The coldest natural place we know of, the Boomerang Nebula, is about one degree above. At very low temperatures, strange things happen. Some materials lose all electrical resistance, and some fluids flow without any friction, and they can climb up the walls of their container.

# Ohm's law
@src Ohm, 1827

In 1827 the German schoolteacher Georg Ohm published what became the first rule of electrical circuits. The current through a conductor equals the voltage across it divided by its resistance. Double the voltage across a fixed resistor, and the current doubles. Double the resistance, and the current halves.

It was dismissed at first, and Ohm struggled for years in poorly paid teaching jobs. Now every electrician uses it, and the unit of resistance carries his name. A helpful picture is a water pipe: voltage is the pressure, current is the flow, and resistance is how narrow the pipe is. With any two of the three numbers, you can find the third, and the arithmetic is simple enough to do in your head.

# Lightning is hotter than the Sun's surface
@src NOAA

A bolt of lightning heats the air around it to about 30,000 kelvin, roughly five times hotter than the visible surface of the Sun, which is about 5,800 kelvin. The heating is so sudden that the air expands faster than the speed of sound, producing a shock wave.

That's the thunder you hear. You can estimate how far away a strike was by counting the seconds between the flash and the sound, and dividing by five to get the distance in miles. The flash arrives almost at once, and the sound takes much longer. A strike that you can hear is close enough to be dangerous, so the old advice stands: when thunder roars, go indoors.

# Earth's magnetic field
@src Standard geophysics reference

Earth's core acts like a giant magnet, and its field deflects charged particles from the Sun, which helps protect life. It's why we have auroras, where some of the particles are funneled toward the poles and make the air glow.

The field isn't fixed, though. Rocks record that its direction has reversed many times. The last full flip was about 780,000 years ago, and after one, a compass needle would point south. A reversal takes thousands of years, and nothing suggests one would be a catastrophe, but nobody knows when the next will be. The north magnetic pole is also wandering, and it has been moving toward Siberia at a good pace in recent decades.

# The speed of sound
@src Standard physics reference

In air at 20 degrees Celsius, sound travels at about 343 meters per second, roughly a kilometer every three seconds. It moves faster through liquids and solids: about 1,480 meters per second in water, and over 5,000 in steel.

The reason is that denser, stiffer materials pass vibrations along more quickly, since the particles spring back faster. It's why you can hear an approaching train by putting an ear to the rail before you hear it in the air. It's also why whales can communicate over great distances, since sound carries so well in water. Sound can't travel in a vacuum at all, because it needs something to push on.

# The Doppler effect
@src Doppler, 1842; Buys Ballot, 1845

When a source of sound moves toward you, the waves are squeezed together and the pitch sounds higher. When it moves away, the waves are stretched and the pitch drops. You hear it when an ambulance passes, with that falling "neeee-yow."

Christian Doppler proposed the idea in 1842, and in 1845 the Dutch scientist Buys Ballot tested it with trumpeters playing a steady note on a moving train. Astronomers use the same effect with light to measure how fast distant galaxies are moving. If a star is moving away, its light shifts toward the red, and if it's coming toward us, it shifts toward the blue. It's a simple idea that lets us measure the motion of things that are billions of miles away.

# Why the Tacoma Narrows Bridge fell
@src Billah & Scanlan, 1991

In November 1940 a new suspension bridge in Washington state twisted wildly in a moderate wind and collapsed. A film of the event is still shown in engineering classes. Many physics textbooks used to call it a simple case of resonance.

Later analysis showed it was something more subtle, called aerodynamic flutter. The twisting motion of the deck changes how the wind pushes on it, which feeds more energy into the motion, and the twisting grows. Engineers have designed bridges to avoid it since, using wind tunnels to test models. The story is a good example of how an explanation can be repeated for decades before someone checks it, and of how failures teach engineers more than successes do.

# Newton's prism
@src Newton, Opticks (1704)

Before Newton, many people thought that a prism added colors to white light, staining it as it passed through the glass. In 1666 he showed otherwise. He sent a beam of sunlight through one prism, and it spread into a rainbow.

Then he sent the colors through a second prism turned the other way, and they came back together as white. So white light is a mixture of all the colors, and the prism only separates them. A rainbow does the same thing using raindrops. Each color bends by a slightly different amount, and so they come out in order. It was a neat experiment that needed only two pieces of glass and a small hole in a window shutter.

# The double-slit experiment
@src Young, 1801; Davisson & Germer, 1927

In 1801 Thomas Young shone light through two narrow slits and saw a pattern of bright and dark stripes on a screen behind them, which is what waves do when they overlap. It seemed to settle the matter: light is a wave.

A century later, scientists found that light also comes in particles. Then in 1927 Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer found that electrons behave like waves too. Later experiments sent single electrons, one at a time, through the slits, and the stripes still built up, one dot at a time. Each electron seems to go through both slits and interfere with itself. It's one of the strangest experiments in physics, and physicists have lived with the puzzle for a hundred years.

# The uncertainty principle
@src Heisenberg, 1927

In 1927 Werner Heisenberg showed that you can't know both the exact position and the exact momentum of a particle at the same time. The more precisely you pin down one, the fuzzier the other becomes.

It's often described as the effect of measuring disturbing the thing measured. But the deeper point is that it's built into nature. A particle simply doesn't have a perfectly sharp position and a perfectly sharp velocity together. The limit is tiny and doesn't matter for a baseball or a car, so you aren't going to be uncertain where you left your keys. But for electrons and atoms, it's a basic fact of how the world works.

# Superposition
@src Schrödinger, 1935

In quantum mechanics, a particle can be in a combination of states at once, until it's measured. It isn't that we don't know which state it's in. The particle really is in a blend of both.

Erwin Schrödinger found this so strange that in 1935 he made up a thought experiment in which a cat in a box is linked to a quantum event, so that it's both alive and dead until someone opens the box. He meant it as a criticism, to show how odd the idea gets when scaled up. Physicists still debate what a measurement actually does. The experiments agree with the theory to remarkable accuracy, even though nobody agrees on what it means.

# Quantum entanglement
@src Nobel Prize in Physics, 2022

Two particles can be linked so that measuring one instantly tells you something about the other, however far apart they are. Einstein didn't like it and called it "spooky action at a distance."

But experiments, from Alain Aspect's in the early 1980s to tests with no loopholes in 2015, have confirmed that it's real. Aspect, John Clauser and Anton Zeilinger shared the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work. It doesn't let you send messages faster than light, because the results of each measurement look random until the two sides compare notes. But it's the basis for new technologies, like quantum cryptography, and it shows that the world is stranger than our everyday experience suggests.

# Quantum tunneling
@src Gamow, 1928

In everyday life, a ball can't roll through a hill that's higher than its energy. In the quantum world, particles sometimes can. This is called tunneling, and it happens because a particle's position is spread out, with a small chance that it turns up on the other side.

It's how the Sun shines. Protons in its core don't have quite enough energy to overcome their mutual repulsion and fuse, but a few tunnel through, enough to keep the Sun burning for billions of years. The effect is also used in scanning tunneling microscopes, which can see individual atoms, and in the flash memory in phones. Without tunneling, there would be no sunlight and no smartphone storage.

# The photoelectric effect
@src Einstein, 1905

When light hits a metal surface, it can knock electrons loose, but only if its frequency is high enough. A dim blue light does it, while a very bright red light doesn't. Physicists couldn't explain that with waves, since a brighter wave should have more energy.

In 1905 Einstein proposed that light comes in packets of energy, later called photons, and each must carry enough energy to free an electron. A red photon doesn't have enough, however many you send, and a blue one does. This, and not relativity, won him the 1921 Nobel Prize. It's the principle behind solar panels and the sensor in a digital camera, and it was one of the first steps toward quantum theory.

# Atoms are mostly empty
@src Rutherford, 1911

In 1911 Ernest Rutherford's team fired particles at thin gold foil and found that most went straight through, but a few bounced right back. He later said it was as surprising as firing a shell at tissue paper and having it come back at you.

He concluded that an atom has a tiny, dense nucleus with mostly empty space around it. The nucleus is about 100,000 times smaller than the atom. If the atom were a stadium, the nucleus would be a pea at the center. Things feel solid because of the electric forces between atoms, and not because they're packed with stuff. When you lean on a table, your atoms never touch the table's atoms. They only repel one another.

# General relativity
@src Einstein, 1915; Dyson, Eddington & Davidson, 1920

In 1915 Einstein published a new theory of gravity. Instead of a force, he described it as the bending of space and time by mass and energy. Matter tells space how to curve, and curved space tells matter how to move.

In 1919, during a solar eclipse, Arthur Eddington's expeditions measured starlight bending as it passed the Sun, by an amount consistent with Einstein's prediction. The reports made him world-famous overnight. A century later, the theory has passed every test. It describes the orbits of planets and the behavior of black holes, and it's built into the GPS in your phone, which wouldn't work without it.

# Gravitational waves
@src LIGO, Abbott et al., 2016

Einstein predicted in 1916 that moving masses would send out ripples in spacetime. They're incredibly faint, and for a century they could not be detected. Einstein himself doubted that anyone ever would.

On September 14, 2015, the LIGO detectors in Louisiana and Washington picked up a signal from two black holes that had merged 1.3 billion light-years away. The stretching of space they measured was thousands of times smaller than the width of a proton. Rainer Weiss, Kip Thorne and Barry Barish won the 2017 Nobel Prize for it. Since then, many more have been found. It opened a new way to observe the universe, by feeling it as well as seeing it.

# Black holes
@src Event Horizon Telescope, 2019, 2022

A black hole is a region where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape from inside its boundary, called the event horizon. They form when very massive stars collapse, and some, in the centers of galaxies, are millions or billions of times the mass of the Sun.

The first image of one, in the galaxy M87, was released in 2019 by the Event Horizon Telescope. In 2022 the same team showed the black hole at the center of our own galaxy, which is about four million times the mass of the Sun. The images show a bright ring of glowing gas around a dark center. They are blurry, but they are real pictures of something that was once only a prediction of the mathematics.

# Hawking radiation
@src Hawking, 1974

In 1974 Stephen Hawking showed that, thanks to quantum effects near the event horizon, black holes should slowly lose mass by emitting a faint glow of radiation. It means that they aren't completely black, and that they can eventually evaporate.

For a black hole of the size we know, the process would take far, far longer than the current age of the universe. No one has ever detected the radiation, since it's far too weak. It's a prediction that's accepted by most physicists because it comes from putting together two of the best theories, quantum mechanics and gravity. It's a clue to what a deeper theory might look like.

# Time runs slower near mass
@src Chou et al., 2010

General relativity predicts that time passes more slowly in stronger gravity. A clock at sea level ticks slightly slower than one on a mountaintop. It isn't that the clock is broken. Time itself runs at a different rate.

In 2010 researchers at NIST measured it with extremely precise atomic clocks that were only about 33 centimeters, a foot, apart in height. The difference was tiny but real. It also matters in practice. GPS satellites orbit high above the surface, where time runs faster, and they need to correct for it, or they'd drift by kilometers each day. Every time your phone finds your location, it's using Einstein's theory.

# The Big Bang
@src Planck Collaboration, 2018

The evidence says that the universe began as an incredibly hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago, and has been expanding and cooling since. The Belgian priest and physicist Georges Lemaître first proposed it in 1927.

Three main things support it. Galaxies are moving apart, the whole sky glows with leftover radiation, and the proportions of hydrogen and helium match the predictions. It describes what happened after the beginning, not what caused it, and not where it happened. There was no center and no outside. Space itself was small and has grown. The name "Big Bang" was coined in 1949 by the astronomer Fred Hoyle, who favored a rival theory, and it stuck.

# The cosmic microwave background
@src Penzias & Wilson, 1965

In 1964 two radio astronomers at Bell Labs, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, were bothered by a faint hiss in their big horn antenna. They even cleaned out pigeon droppings, thinking they might be the cause. The hiss didn't go away.

It turned out to be the afterglow of the Big Bang, light released when the universe was about 380,000 years old and had cooled enough to become transparent. It has been stretched by the expansion of space into microwaves, and it comes from every direction. They won the 1978 Nobel Prize. An old analog television that isn't tuned to a channel shows a small part of it as static, so you've perhaps seen the afterglow of the beginning of time.

# The expanding universe
@src Hubble, 1929

In 1929 Edwin Hubble showed that galaxies are moving away from us, and that the farther away they are, the faster they're receding. This built on the 1927 work of Georges Lemaître, who had reached a similar result from the theory.

It doesn't mean we're at the center. Space itself is stretching, so every galaxy sees the others moving away, like dots on the surface of an inflating balloon. It was the first key observation behind the Big Bang model, and it changed our view of the cosmos from a fixed, eternal stage to an evolving one. If everything is moving apart today, then in the past, everything must have been closer together.

# Dark matter
@src Rubin & Ford, 1970; Planck, 2018

In the 1970s the astronomer Vera Rubin measured how fast stars orbit at the edges of galaxies. They moved as fast as the stars near the center, which didn't match the visible matter. If only the visible matter were there, the outer stars should have been flung away.

The best explanation is that there's an unseen halo of matter whose gravity holds everything together. It makes up about 27 percent of the universe. We don't know what it's made of, and experiments around the world are trying to find out. The name is a little misleading, since it isn't dark so much as invisible. It doesn't give off or absorb light, and we only know it through its gravity.

# Dark energy
@src Riess et al., 1998; Perlmutter et al., 1999

In 1998 two teams studying distant exploding stars expected to find that the expansion of the universe was slowing down because of gravity. Instead, they found that it was speeding up.

Something is pushing space apart, and it's been named dark energy. It makes up about 68 percent of the universe, and nobody knows what it is. The leaders of the two teams shared the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics. It's one of the biggest open questions in science. We have a name and a measurement, but not an explanation, and it might be a property of space itself or something else we haven't thought of.

# Ordinary matter is rare
@src Planck Collaboration, 2018

Everything you can see, from the Earth to the stars to you, is made of ordinary atoms, and all of that is only about 5 percent of the universe. The other 95 percent is dark matter, about 27 percent, and dark energy, about 68 percent.

The measurements come mainly from satellites studying the cosmic microwave background. We've learned that most of the universe is something we don't yet understand. It's humbling. After centuries of science, the stuff we know best, and are made of, turns out to be the minority. The numbers may shift with new measurements, but the point is that we have a lot left to discover.

# The observable universe
@src Standard cosmology reference

The part of the universe we can see is about 93 billion light-years across. That may sound odd, since the universe is only 13.8 billion years old, and nothing should have been able to travel farther than that. But space has been stretching all the while the light was traveling to us.

So the distance to the places that sent us the oldest light is much larger now. There may well be more beyond the edge, but its light hasn't had time to reach us. We can never see it, because the stretching carries it away faster than light can cross the gap. The observable universe is the region we can see from here, and it doesn't have to be all there is.

# The Milky Way
@src NASA; van der Marel et al., 2019

Our galaxy is a spiral of somewhere between 100 and 400 billion stars, about 100,000 light-years across. We live in one of its arms, about halfway out from the center. From the inside, it looks like a faint band of light across the night sky.

The Sun takes about 230 million years to go once around, so it has made about twenty trips since it was born. In roughly four to five billion years the Milky Way will merge with its nearest large neighbor, the Andromeda galaxy, which is heading our way. The stars are so far apart that few will actually collide. The two galaxies will pass through one another and settle into a new shape.

# The Sun
@src NASA

The Sun is a middle-aged star, about 4.6 billion years old. Every second, in its core, it turns about 600 million tons of hydrogen into helium, and the small amount of mass that vanishes in the process becomes the energy that warms us.

It has enough fuel for roughly another five billion years. After that it will swell into a red giant, and then shrink into a white dwarf. The energy that you feel as warmth on your face started in the core, and took tens of thousands of years to work its way to the surface, and then eight minutes to reach you. It's a very old gift, and we're lucky that the Sun burns so steadily.

# Stars have life cycles
@src Standard astronomy text

A star's life is decided mainly by its mass. Small ones, called red dwarfs, burn their fuel slowly and can last trillions of years. Massive stars burn fast and bright, lasting only millions of years, and end in a supernova explosion that leaves behind a neutron star or a black hole.

Our Sun, in the middle, will end more quietly as a white dwarf, a small, dense, cooling ember. It's a strange rule that the bigger the star, the shorter its life, but it follows from the way they burn. A larger star has more fuel, but it's squeezed harder, so it uses it up at a furious pace. Stars are born, live and die, and the universe has been recycling their material for billions of years.

# We are stardust
@src Burbidge, Burbidge, Fowler & Hoyle, 1957

Just after the Big Bang, the universe was almost entirely hydrogen and helium. Every heavier element, including the carbon in your cells, the oxygen you breathe and the iron in your blood, was made later inside stars, and was scattered into space when stars died.

Some heavy elements also come from the collisions of neutron stars. The 1957 paper by Margaret and Geoffrey Burbidge, William Fowler and Fred Hoyle worked out how stars make the elements. In a real sense, you're made of material that was cooked in stars, and has been through more than one. It's a fact that can make an ordinary day feel a little less ordinary, since every atom of you has a long history.

# Neutron stars
@src NASA

When a massive star explodes as a supernova, its core can collapse into a neutron star, a ball only about 20 kilometers across but with more mass than the Sun. The atoms are crushed so that the protons and electrons merge into neutrons.

A teaspoon of its material would weigh about a billion tons. Some neutron stars spin hundreds of times a second, and they send out beams of radio waves that sweep across space like a lighthouse. We detect those beams as pulses, and the objects are called pulsars. Neutron stars are among the densest things in the universe, short of black holes, and they're a natural laboratory for physics under extreme conditions.
