# Tycho Brahe
@src Thoren, The Lord of Uraniborg, 1990

Tycho Brahe was a Danish nobleman, born in 1546. When he was twenty, he fought a duel with another student over a point of mathematics, and lost part of his nose. He wore a replacement made of metal for the rest of his life. He became an astronomer, after seeing a new star in the sky in 1572, and the king of Denmark gave him an island, Hven, where he built a large observatory.

With no telescope, since it hadn't been invented, he used large instruments of his own design to measure the positions of the stars and planets more accurately than anyone before. His records covered decades. In 1600 he hired a young mathematician, Johannes Kepler. When Tycho died in 1601, Kepler used the data to discover that the planets move in ellipses. Good observations were the key.

# William Herschel
@src Herschel, Philosophical Transactions, 1781

William Herschel was born in Germany in 1738, and moved to England as a young musician. He earned his living as an organist and teacher, and astronomy was his hobby. Unable to buy a good telescope, he made his own, grinding the mirrors by hand. On the night of March 13, 1781, in the garden of his house in Bath, he saw a faint object that didn't look like a star.

At first, he thought that it was a comet. It was a new planet, the first discovered since ancient times, now called Uranus. He wanted to name it Georgium Sidus, George's Star, after King George III, who gave him a pension. The name didn't last. Herschel went on to discover moons of Saturn and Uranus, and in 1800 he found infrared radiation, by measuring the temperature beyond the red end of a spectrum.

# Caroline Herschel
@src Hoskin, Caroline Herschel's Autobiographies, 2003

Caroline Herschel was the younger sister of William. Born in Germany in 1750, she had smallpox as a child, which left her scarred, and her growth was stunted. Her family expected her to be a servant. In 1772 she moved to England to live with her brother, and became his assistant, helping with his telescopes, and recording his observations at night.

She began her own search of the sky, using a small telescope. Between 1786 and 1797 she discovered eight comets. In 1787 the king gave her a salary of fifty pounds a year for her work. It's often said that she was the first woman in England to be paid for science. She also made a catalog of stars. In 1828 the Royal Astronomical Society awarded her its gold medal, and she lived to be ninety-seven.

# Maria Mitchell
@src Wright, Sweeper in the Sky, 1949

Maria Mitchell grew up on Nantucket, Massachusetts, in a Quaker family that valued education for girls. Her father, a teacher and amateur astronomer, taught her to use a telescope. She worked as a librarian, and spent her evenings watching the sky from the roof of the bank where her family lived.

On October 1, 1847, she saw a faint comet that no one had seen before. The King of Denmark had offered a gold medal for the discovery of a comet by telescope, and it was awarded to her. She became the first woman elected to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences. In 1865 she became professor of astronomy at the new Vassar College. She taught her students to observe for themselves, and she was paid less than her male colleagues, which she protested.

# Annie Jump Cannon
@src Sobel, The Glass Universe, 2016

Annie Jump Cannon was born in Delaware in 1863. As a young woman, she lost most of her hearing, probably from an illness, and threw herself into her work. She studied physics at Wellesley College, and in 1896 went to work at the Harvard College Observatory, where a group of women analyzed photographic plates of the sky. They were called computers.

Cannon sorted stars by their spectra, the patterns of colors in their light. She could classify three stars a minute by eye. The system that she devised, with the letters O, B, A, F, G, K and M, in order of temperature, is still used. She classified about a third of a million stars in her life. A well-known memory aid for the order is Oh, Be A Fine Girl, Kiss Me. In 1938 she was given an official post at Harvard, when she was seventy-five.

# Chandrasekhar and the limit
@src Chandrasekhar, Astrophysical Journal, 1931; Miller, Empire of the Stars, 2005

In 1930 a nineteen-year-old Indian physics student, Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar, sailed from India to England to study at Cambridge. On the voyage, he worked out what happens to a white dwarf, the dense remains of a star like the Sun, when it has used up its fuel. He combined quantum mechanics and relativity, and found that there is an upper limit to the mass of such a star, about one and a half times the mass of the Sun.

A heavier star could not support itself against gravity, and would collapse. This is now called the Chandrasekhar limit. The famous astronomer Arthur Eddington ridiculed the idea in public, and Chandrasekhar was discouraged. He moved to the United States, and kept working. His work was later seen as the prediction of neutron stars and black holes. He received the Nobel Prize in 1983, fifty-three years after the voyage.

# Enrico Fermi
@src Fermi, Chicago Pile-1 records, 1942

Enrico Fermi was born in Rome in 1901, and was a rare physicist who was brilliant at both theory and experiment. He won the Nobel Prize in 1938, for work on radioactivity caused by neutrons. He used the trip to Stockholm to receive it to leave Fascist Italy with his Jewish wife, and he went to the United States.

On December 2, 1942, in a squash court under the stands of a stadium at the University of Chicago, he led the team that started the first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction. The device, called Chicago Pile-1, was a stack of graphite blocks and uranium. It ran for about four and a half minutes. Fermi also had a gift for quick estimates, and he asked a famous question about extraterrestrial life: where is everybody? He died in 1954, at fifty-three.

# Humphry Davy
@src Davy, Elements of Chemical Philosophy, 1812

Humphry Davy was an English chemist who lived from 1778 to 1829. He was a showman as well as a scientist, and his public lectures at the Royal Institution in London were enormously popular. He used electricity to split compounds apart. By doing so, in 1807 and 1808, he discovered sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, barium and strontium.

He also experimented on himself with nitrous oxide, laughing gas, and noted that it might be used to relieve pain. In 1815 he invented a safety lamp for miners, in which the flame was surrounded by a wire gauze, that reduced the risk of igniting explosive gas. In 1813 he hired a young bookbinder's apprentice, Michael Faraday, as an assistant. Davy is said to have remarked, late in life, that his greatest discovery was Faraday.

# Alfred Nobel
@src Nobel Foundation; Fant, Alfred Nobel: A Biography, 1991

Alfred Nobel was a Swedish chemist and engineer, born in 1833. In 1867 he patented dynamite, a safer way of handling the unstable explosive nitroglycerin. It was used in mining and construction, and also in war. He became very rich, with factories in many countries, and held more than three hundred patents.

In 1888 his brother Ludvig died, and a French newspaper mistakenly published an obituary for Alfred, with the headline that the merchant of death is dead. The story is that he was shocked by it, and wanted to be remembered differently. In his will, signed in 1895, he left most of his fortune for prizes in physics, chemistry, medicine, literature and peace. The first prizes were given in 1901, five years after his death. The economics prize was added in 1968, by the Swedish central bank.

# Dorothy Hodgkin
@src Ferry, Dorothy Hodgkin: A Life, 1998

Dorothy Hodgkin was a British chemist, born in 1910 in Egypt. She became fascinated by crystals as a girl. She used X-rays to find the structure of molecules. When X-rays pass through a crystal, they are scattered in a pattern, and from the pattern, one can work out where the atoms are. Her subjects were molecules of huge complexity.

In 1945 she worked out the structure of penicillin. In 1956 she found that of vitamin B12, which has more than a hundred and eighty atoms, with the help of an early computer. She began to work on insulin in 1934, and published the structure in 1969, after thirty-five years. She won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1964. Arthritis crippled her hands, but she kept working. One of her students at Oxford was Margaret Thatcher, who studied chemistry.

# Linus Pauling
@src Hager, Force of Nature, 1995

Linus Pauling was an American chemist who lived from 1901 to 1994. His work on the nature of the chemical bond, in the 1930s, showed how quantum mechanics explains how atoms join to form molecules. He also showed that sickle cell anemia is a molecular disease, the first time that a disease had been traced to a change in a single molecule. He won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1954.

He campaigned against nuclear weapons testing, and in 1962 he received the Nobel Peace Prize. He's the only person to have won two unshared Nobel Prizes. He also made mistakes. He proposed a wrong structure for DNA, a triple helix, a few months before Watson and Crick. And in later life he promoted large doses of vitamin C as a way of preventing colds and treating cancer, claims that have not been supported by trials.

# Lynn Margulis
@src Margulis, Origin of Mitosing Eukaryotic Cells, 1967

Lynn Margulis was an American biologist who lived from 1938 to 2011. In the 1960s she became interested in an old idea, that some parts of cells arose from bacteria that were swallowed by larger cells, and went on living inside them. She argued that mitochondria, the power plants of cells, and chloroplasts, which carry out photosynthesis in plants, are descended from free-living bacteria. This is called endosymbiosis.

She wrote a paper in 1966 that was rejected by about fifteen journals, before it was published in 1967. Many biologists were hostile. But the evidence grew. Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own DNA, which looks like that of bacteria, and they divide on their own. By the 1980s, the theory was accepted, and it's now in the textbooks. She also supported the controversial Gaia hypothesis, with James Lovelock.

# Elizabeth Blackwell
@src Blackwell, Pioneer Work in Opening the Medical Profession to Women, 1895

In the 1840s, no medical school in the United States would accept women. Elizabeth Blackwell, born in England in 1821 and raised in the United States, applied to more than a dozen schools, and was turned down by all of them. Geneva Medical College in New York accepted her, in part because of a prank. The faculty put her application to the students for a vote, and the students, thinking it was a joke, voted yes.

She was not treated as a joke once she arrived. She studied hard, and graduated first in her class in 1849, the first woman to receive a medical degree in the United States. She later opened a hospital and a medical college for women in New York, and helped to start a school in England. Her sister Emily also became a doctor. She spent her life encouraging women to enter medicine.

# Virginia Apgar
@src Apgar, Current Researches in Anesthesia and Analgesia, 1953

Virginia Apgar was an American doctor who lived from 1909 to 1974. She wanted to be a surgeon, but was discouraged, and became an anesthesiologist, one of the first women to lead a hospital department in the field. At Columbia University, she worked in obstetrics, and noticed that newborn babies were not always carefully assessed in the first minutes of life.

In 1952 she devised a quick test that could be given to every baby, one minute after birth, and again at five minutes. It scored five signs on a scale of zero to two: skin color, pulse, reflexes, muscle tone and breathing. The sum is the Apgar score. It helped staff to see quickly which babies needed help, and it's used in hospitals all over the world. Her last name was later made into a memory aid for the five signs.

# Maryam Mirzakhani
@src International Congress of Mathematicians, 2014

Maryam Mirzakhani was born in Tehran in 1977. As a girl, she was more interested in reading novels than in mathematics, but a teacher inspired her. At high school she won gold medals at the International Mathematical Olympiad. She went to Harvard for her doctorate, and later became a professor at Stanford.

She worked on the geometry of curved surfaces, and on how they can be deformed. She liked to think about problems by drawing on large sheets of paper, and her daughter said that her mother was painting. In 2014 she was awarded the Fields Medal, the highest honor in mathematics, the first woman to receive it in the prize's eighty-year history. She died of breast cancer in 2017, at forty. Her birthday, May 12, is now celebrated as a day for women in mathematics.

# Grigori Perelman
@src Gessen, Perfect Rigor, 2009

In 2002 and 2003 a Russian mathematician, Grigori Perelman, posted three papers on the internet, which gave a proof of the Poincaré conjecture. It's a question about the shape of three-dimensional space that had been open for a hundred years, and was one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems with a reward of a million dollars. He did not send the papers to a journal.

Mathematicians spent years checking the proof, and found it to be correct. In 2006 he was offered the Fields Medal, and he turned it down. In 2010 the Clay Institute awarded him its million-dollar prize, and he declined that as well. He said he wasn't interested in money or fame, and he had left the field. He lives quietly in Saint Petersburg. It's the only Millennium Problem solved so far.

# Thomas Edison
@src Israel, Edison: A Life of Invention, 1998

Thomas Edison was born in Ohio in 1847. He was partly deaf from childhood, and had only a few months of formal schooling. He became a telegraph operator, and then an inventor. In 1876 he set up a laboratory in Menlo Park, New Jersey. He called it an invention factory. It had a team of machinists and scientists, and aimed to turn out a minor invention every ten days and a big one every six months.

He held over one thousand patents in the United States, for things such as the phonograph, an improved light bulb and a motion picture camera. His method was trial and error on a huge scale. For the light bulb, his team tested thousands of materials for the filament, such as carbon and bamboo. He also built the first power station in New York, in 1882. His real talent was in organizing invention.

# Alexander Graham Bell
@src Bruce, Bell: Alexander Graham Bell and the Conquest of Solitude, 1973

Alexander Graham Bell was born in Scotland in 1847, and moved to Canada, and then to Boston. He was a teacher of the deaf, and his mother and wife were both deaf. His father had developed a system of symbols for the sounds of speech, that he used. He was trying to improve the telegraph, when he realized that a telephone might be possible.

On March 10, 1876, in his laboratory in Boston, he spoke into the device to his assistant, Thomas Watson, in another room: Mr. Watson, come here, I want to see you. Watson heard it. Bell's patent had been granted three days earlier. It had been filed on the same day that another inventor, Elisha Gray, filed a notice of a similar idea. The dispute went on for years, and Bell won. He devoted much of his later life to teaching the deaf.

# James Watt
@src Dickinson, James Watt and the Steam Engine, 1927

James Watt was a Scottish instrument maker, who was born in 1736. In 1763 he was asked to repair a model of an engine invented by Thomas Newcomen, which was used to pump water out of mines. He saw that it wasted a great deal of heat, since the cylinder was heated and then cooled on each stroke. In 1765, according to his own account, he had the idea of a separate condenser.

With the help of the manufacturer Matthew Boulton, in Birmingham, he built engines that used much less coal. He invented ways of making them turn a wheel, so that they could drive machines, and not just pumps. To sell them, he compared their power with that of horses, which led to the term horsepower. The unit of power, the watt, is named for him. His engines helped to power the Industrial Revolution.

# Ibn Battuta
@src Ibn Battuta, Rihla, 1355; Dunn, The Adventures of Ibn Battuta, 1986

Ibn Battuta was born in Tangier, Morocco, in 1304. In 1325, when he was twenty-one, he set out on a pilgrimage to Mecca. He planned to be away for a year or two. He did not come home for almost thirty years. During this time, he visited most of the Muslim world, and more. He traveled to Egypt, Syria, Persia, East Africa, Anatolia, India, the Maldives, Southeast Asia and China.

He covered about seventy-five thousand miles, more than Marco Polo, and he worked as a judge in places such as Delhi. He was robbed, shipwrecked and caught up in wars. He survived the plague. After his return, the Sultan of Morocco asked him to dictate an account of his journeys. It's called the Rihla, and it's an important source on the societies of the fourteenth century.

# Mary Shelley
@src Shelley, Frankenstein, 1818; Seymour, Mary Shelley, 2000

In the summer of 1816, eighteen-year-old Mary Godwin, who later became Mary Shelley, was staying near Geneva, Switzerland, with her future husband, the poet Percy Shelley, and with Lord Byron. It was the year without a summer. The eruption of the volcano Tambora in Indonesia in the previous year had filled the air with dust, and there was cold and rain across Europe. The group stayed indoors.

Byron proposed that each of them should write a ghost story. Mary struggled, and then, after a conversation about the nature of life, and the new experiments with electricity, she had a waking dream about a scientist who brought a creature to life. This became Frankenstein, published when she was twenty. It's often called the first science fiction novel. It asks what responsibility a creator has for what it makes, a question that still matters.

# John Maynard Keynes
@src Keynes, The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money, 1936; Skidelsky, 2003

John Maynard Keynes was a British economist who lived from 1883 to 1946. He worked in the government, and was an adviser at the peace conference after the First World War. He resigned, and wrote a book arguing that the harsh terms imposed on Germany would cause disaster. He was also a collector of art, and a successful investor, and a leading member of the Bloomsbury group of writers and artists.

In the Great Depression, he argued that governments should spend more, even if it meant a deficit, to raise demand, and to create jobs. His book of 1936 changed the study of economics. He said that markets are driven by animal spirits, the waves of confidence and fear. He is also remembered for the remark that in the long run we are all dead, made in 1923. At the Bretton Woods conference in 1944, he helped to plan the postwar financial system.

# Elinor Ostrom
@src Ostrom, Governing the Commons, 1990

Elinor Ostrom was an American political scientist who lived from 1933 to 2012. She grew up poor in Los Angeles, and was told that girls shouldn't study mathematics. She became a professor at Indiana University. In 1990 she published a book that challenged a very influential idea, the tragedy of the commons, which holds that shared resources will be overused and ruined, unless they are privatized or controlled by a government.

She studied real communities that had managed commons for centuries, such as Swiss alpine meadows, Japanese forests and irrigation systems in Spain and the Philippines. She found that people often cooperate successfully. She identified features of the successful ones, such as clear boundaries, rules that fit local conditions, monitoring, and graduated penalties. In 2009 she became the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in Economics.

# Amartya Sen
@src Sen, Poverty and Famines, 1981; Development as Freedom, 1999

Amartya Sen was born in India in 1933. As a boy of nine, he saw the Bengal famine of 1943, in which about three million people died. He remembered the sight of starving people at his family's door. Later, as an economist, he asked what causes famines. The common idea was that they occur when there's not enough food.

In his book Poverty and Famines, in 1981, he showed that famines often occur even when the food supply is adequate. People starve because they have lost the means of buying food, owing to a fall in wages or a rise in prices. He also observed that no famine has taken place in a functioning democracy with a free press, since governments face pressure to act. He won the Nobel Prize in 1998. His work shifted the focus of development to people's real freedoms.

# Herbert Simon
@src Simon, Models of Man, 1957; Nobel Prize in Economics, 1978

Herbert Simon was an American scientist who lived from 1916 to 2001. He worked in economics, psychology, political science and computer science, at Carnegie Mellon University. In economics, he argued against the idea that people are perfectly rational. He proposed bounded rationality. People have limited time and ability, and so they don't find the best option. They look for one that is good enough, a strategy that he called satisficing.

With Allen Newell, in 1956, he wrote the Logic Theorist, a program that proved theorems from the mathematics textbook Principia Mathematica. It's often called the first artificial intelligence program. Simon claimed that it solved the mind-body problem. He won the Turing Award in 1975, and the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1978. He was a curious man, who learned several languages, and played chess and piano.

# The chimpanzee test
@src Rosling, Rosling & Rönnlund, Factfulness, 2018

Hans Rosling was a Swedish physician and statistician, who lived from 1948 to 2017. He worked on public health in Africa, and became known for lively talks about world statistics, using animated graphs. He co-founded the Gapminder Foundation. In the talks, he asked the audience questions about the state of the world, such as how many of the world's children have been vaccinated, and what share of people live in extreme poverty.

Each question had three answers. If you picked at random, you would be right a third of the time. Even a chimpanzee, he said, picking from bananas, would get one in three. In his surveys of people in many countries, including students, doctors and leaders, the average score was below that, often only a couple of correct answers out of twelve. People held outdated, gloomy pictures of the world. Rosling's message was that the facts are generally better than we think.

# Vera Rubin
@src Rubin & Ford, Astrophysical Journal, 1970

Vera Rubin was an American astronomer who lived from 1928 to 2016. As a girl, she built a telescope. At Vassar College she was the only astronomy major in her year, and when she applied to Princeton for graduate study, she was not allowed to apply, because it did not accept women in astronomy. She studied at Georgetown, and became an astronomer at the Carnegie Institution in Washington.

In the 1970s she and Kent Ford measured how fast stars at different distances from the center of a galaxy move. According to Newton's laws, those at the edge should move more slowly. They didn't. They moved just as fast, as if the galaxy was held together by much more mass than could be seen. It was strong evidence for dark matter. She never received a Nobel Prize, though many thought that she deserved one. Dark matter is now thought to make up most of the matter in the universe.
