# How long is a meter?
@src Bureau International des Poids et Mesures; Alder, The Measure of All Things, 2002

In 1791 the French Academy of Sciences proposed a new unit of length, to be the same for all people and all time. It would be one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator, along the meridian through Paris. Two astronomers, Jean-Baptiste Delambre and Pierre Méchain, spent seven years surveying the distance from Dunkirk to Barcelona to work it out.

The meter was later defined by a platinum bar, and then by the wavelength of light from krypton. Since 1983 it has been defined by the speed of light. A meter is the distance that light travels in a vacuum in one 299,792,458th of a second. The unit began with the size of the Earth, and now rests on a constant of nature. Méchain, who found a small error in his data, was troubled by it until he died.

# The kilogram without a lump of metal
@src BIPM, 2019; Stock et al., Metrologia, 2019

For 130 years, the kilogram was defined by a lump of platinum and iridium, kept in a vault near Paris, known as Le Grand K. Copies were sent to other countries. Over time, the copies and the original drifted apart by about fifty micrograms, roughly the weight of a grain of sand. Since the definition was an object, no one could say which had changed.

On May 20, 2019, the kilogram was redefined in terms of the Planck constant, a fixed number from quantum physics. It's measured using a machine called a Kibble balance, which compares mechanical power with electrical power. Now every unit in the metric system is defined in terms of nature, and not an artifact. A scientist in any laboratory can, in principle, make a kilogram, and the old cylinder is just a historical object.

# The second and the atom
@src Essen & Parry, Nature, 1955; BIPM, 1967

For most of history, the second was defined as a fraction of a day. It was one eighty-six-thousand-four-hundredth part. But the Earth's rotation is not constant. It slows slightly because of tides, and varies unpredictably. In the 1950s, Louis Essen and Jack Parry at the National Physical Laboratory in Britain built the first accurate atomic clock, using the vibrations of cesium atoms.

In 1967 the second was redefined as 9,192,631,770 cycles of the radiation from a cesium-133 atom. Modern atomic clocks are so precise that they would not lose a second in hundreds of millions of years. Newer clocks, which use light, may do better still. Occasionally, a leap second is added to the calendar to keep our clocks in step with the Earth's slowing spin. The accuracy of clocks is used in GPS and in the internet.

# Fahrenheit, Celsius and Kelvin
@src Fahrenheit, 1724; Celsius, 1742; Kelvin, 1848

In 1724 the German instrument maker Daniel Fahrenheit built mercury thermometers that were more accurate than earlier ones. He set zero as the temperature of a mixture of ice, water and salt, which was the coldest he could reach. He put the freezing point of plain water at thirty-two, and set his scale so that human body temperature came to about ninety-six.

In 1742 the Swedish astronomer Anders Celsius proposed a scale with one hundred degrees between the freezing and boiling points of water. Oddly, he put zero at the boiling point and a hundred at the freezing point. It was reversed after his death. In 1848 William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, proposed a scale starting at absolute zero, minus 273.15 degrees Celsius. The kelvin has no negative values, and is used in science.

# The decibel
@src Bell Telephone Laboratories, 1920s

Sound is measured in decibels, a unit named for Alexander Graham Bell, who made the first telephone. It was invented in the 1920s by engineers at Bell Telephone Laboratories, to measure the loss of signal in telephone cables. The scale is logarithmic, which means that each increase of ten decibels means ten times as much sound energy.

It's a good match for the way we hear. We don't notice absolute changes in loudness, but ratios. A rise of ten decibels sounds about twice as loud. A whisper is about thirty decibels, normal conversation about sixty, and a rock concert more than a hundred. Sound above eighty-five decibels over a long time can damage hearing, and a jet engine nearby reaches a level that causes pain. The scale shows how the senses respond to ratios.

# What a magnitude means
@src Richter, Bulletin of the Seismological Society of America, 1935; Hanks & Kanamori, 1979

In 1935 the seismologist Charles Richter, working at Caltech, devised a scale to compare earthquakes. It was based on the size of the waves recorded by a seismograph. The scale is logarithmic. For each whole number, the size of the ground motion is ten times larger. A magnitude seven earthquake shakes the ground ten times as much as a magnitude six.

The energy released rises even faster, by about thirty-two times for each step. So a magnitude eight earthquake releases about a thousand times the energy of a magnitude six. Today seismologists use the moment magnitude scale, devised in 1979 by Thomas Hanks and Hiroo Kanamori, which works better for large quakes. The numbers are reported on the same scale. The largest recorded earthquake was in Chile, in 1960, at magnitude 9.5.

# The days that went missing
@src Duncan, Calendar, 1998; Gregory XIII, Inter gravissimas, 1582

The Julian calendar, introduced by Julius Caesar in 45 BC, had a year of 365.25 days, with a leap year every four years. But the true length of the year is about 365.2422 days. The difference of about eleven minutes a year added up, and by the sixteenth century, the calendar was ten days out of line with the seasons. Easter was drifting away from spring.

In 1582 Pope Gregory XIII decreed a new calendar. Ten days were dropped. In Catholic countries, the day after October 4 was October 15. The new rule was that century years are leap years only if divisible by four hundred. Protestant countries were slow to adopt it. Britain and its colonies changed in 1752, when eleven days were dropped, and there were reportedly protests. Russia changed only in 1918.

# Why we have time zones
@src Blaise, Time Lord, 2000

Before the nineteenth century, each town kept its own time, set by the Sun, and noon was a little different in every place. It didn't matter when travel was slow. But with the railroads, the confusion became a problem. A traveler in the United States might have to deal with dozens of local times, and timetables were a mess.

The Canadian engineer Sandford Fleming, who was angry after missing a train in Ireland in 1876, because the timetable said p.m. rather than a.m., campaigned for a worldwide system. In 1883 the American railroads adopted standard time zones. In 1884 an international conference in Washington chose the meridian through Greenwich in England as the starting point, from which time zones were measured. Governments were slower to adopt the system.

# Daylight saving
@src Franklin, 1784; Prerau, Seize the Daylight, 2005

In 1784 Benjamin Franklin, who was then the American ambassador in Paris, wrote a humorous letter to a newspaper. He had been surprised to learn that the Sun rises early, and that Parisians slept through the morning light and then burned candles at night. He suggested a tax on shutters and a rule that church bells should ring at sunrise. He meant it as a joke, but it's often cited as the first mention of the idea.

The first country to adopt daylight saving time was Germany, in 1916, during the First World War, to save coal. Other countries followed. It has been introduced and dropped many times since. Whether it saves energy is disputed, and studies have linked the change in the spring to a rise in heart attacks and accidents. Many places have debated ending it. It's one of the more argued-over inventions in the calendar.

# Revolutionary time
@src Alder, The Measure of All Things, 2002

The French Revolution did more than change the government. The new regime wanted to remake everything on rational lines, including time. In 1793 it introduced the French Republican calendar, with months of three ten-day weeks, and with names such as Brumaire, for fog, and Thermidor, for heat. It took a year of twelve months of thirty days, and five or six extra days at the end.

The revolutionaries also introduced decimal time. The day was divided into ten hours, each of a hundred minutes, each of a hundred seconds. Some watches were made with two dials. It was unpopular, since people had no wish to change the way they lived, and the ten-day week gave workers one rest day in ten, instead of one in seven. Decimal time was suspended in 1795. The calendar lasted until 1806, when Napoleon abolished it.

# Paper sizes and a square root
@src Lichtenberg, 1786; Deutsches Institut für Normung, 1922

If you fold a sheet of A4 paper in half, you get two sheets of A5, which have the same shape as the original. Fold again, and you have A6. The shape stays the same at every size. This works only if the ratio of the long side to the short side is the square root of two, about 1.414. The German scientist Georg Lichtenberg noticed this property in 1786.

The system was used in Germany from 1922, and it was adopted as the international standard in 1975. A0 is a sheet with an area of one square meter, and each size down is half the area. A4 is the size used throughout the world, except in North America, which uses letter size. A neat feature is that you can enlarge or reduce a document by a standard factor, and it will fit on the next size of paper.

# A dozen and the finger joints
@src Ifrah, The Universal History of Numbers, 2000

Why do we have a dozen eggs and twelve inches in a foot, when we count in tens? One theory is that it's based on the hand. If you use your thumb to count the segments of the other four fingers, there are three on each finger, which gives twelve. With the other hand, you can keep count of the dozens, up to five, and so reach sixty. This is a system that is still used in some parts of Asia.

It may be the origin of the Babylonian sixty-based number system, and of the twelve hours of the day and night, and the twelve months. Twelve has many divisors, two, three, four and six, which makes it handy for sharing things. A ten has only two. Some mathematicians have argued that a base of twelve would have been better than ten. The Dozenal Society still campaigns for it.

# A thousand paces
@src Roman itineraries; Oxford English Dictionary

The word mile comes from the Latin mille passus, a thousand paces. A Roman pace was two steps, from the point where one foot leaves the ground to the point where that same foot lands again, so a mile was about a thousand double steps, or about fifteen hundred meters. Roman soldiers marched in this way, and milestones were set up along the roads.

The modern mile is about sixteen hundred and nine meters, a little longer, as it was changed to fit other measures. In England in 1593, a statute fixed it at eight furlongs, and a furlong was originally the length of a furrow in a plowed field, a furrow-long. An acre was said to be the area that a team of oxen could plow in a day. Many of our units have their origins in human work.

# The nautical mile
@src International Hydrographic Organization; Bowditch, American Practical Navigator, 1802

A nautical mile is a little longer than an ordinary mile, at 1,852 meters. It has a logical basis. It's the length of one minute of latitude, one sixtieth of a degree, on the surface of the Earth. This makes it handy for navigators, who work with charts. If you travel one nautical mile north, your latitude changes by one minute.

A knot is one nautical mile per hour. The name comes from the way that sailors once measured speed. They threw a piece of wood on a rope, which had knots at regular distances, off the stern of a ship, and counted how many knots ran out in a set time. The nautical mile was set at its present length by an international conference in 1929. Airplanes also use nautical miles and knots.
