# The lost letters
@src Milgram, Mann & Harter, Public Opinion Quarterly, 1965

How can you measure public opinion without asking people? In the 1960s Stanley Milgram and his colleagues developed the lost-letter technique. He placed hundreds of stamped, addressed envelopes in public places, such as on the sidewalk, as though they had been dropped. Each was addressed to an organization. Some went to a group such as Friends of the Nazi Party, some to a Friends of the Communist Party, and others to a medical research society, or to a private individual.

How many letters were mailed by passersby depended on where they were addressed. Many more of the letters to the medical society were posted than those to the Nazi or Communist groups. And the rates differed by neighborhood, matching the way people in those places voted. It was a way of testing attitudes by what people did, and not by what they said. It has been used in many places since.

# Bats that share their blood
@src Wilkinson, Nature, 1984

Vampire bats feed on blood, and a bat that fails to find a meal for two or three nights can starve. In 1984 Gerald Wilkinson studied a colony in Costa Rica. He noticed that bats that had fed would sometimes regurgitate blood into the mouth of a hungry roost mate. Was it just kin looking after kin?

He found that many of the donors and receivers were not relatives. The bats were more likely to share with those who had shared with them before, and who they had roosted with for a long time. They kept track of favors. It's an example of reciprocal altruism, in which a cost today is repaid by a benefit later. It requires a good memory for individuals, and the ability to punish cheats. It's one of the best-known cases of cooperation among nonrelatives.

# Two brothers or eight cousins
@src Hamilton, Journal of Theoretical Biology, 1964

Why would an animal help others, at a cost to itself? In 1964 the British biologist William Hamilton offered an answer. A gene can spread not only by helping the individual that carries it to reproduce, but also by helping relatives, who have copies of the same gene. He wrote a simple rule, now called Hamilton's rule. A gene for helping will spread if the benefit to the relative, multiplied by the degree of relatedness, is greater than the cost to the helper.

The geneticist J. B. S. Haldane is said to have joked, in a pub, that he would lay down his life for two brothers or eight cousins. A brother shares half of your genes, and a cousin one eighth. The idea explains the sterile workers of social insects, who help their mother to raise sisters. It's called kin selection. It has become one of the foundations of modern evolutionary biology.

# The trust game
@src Berg, Dickhaut & McCabe, Games and Economic Behavior, 1995

In 1995 Joyce Berg, John Dickhaut and Kevin McCabe devised a game to measure trust. A person, the sender, is given ten dollars, and can send any part of it to an anonymous partner, the receiver. The amount that is sent is tripled by the experimenter. The receiver can then send back any share of the larger amount, or keep it all. The players never meet.

A purely selfish receiver would keep everything, and a selfish sender, expecting that, would send nothing. But in the experiment, most senders sent about half, and receivers sent back about as much as the senders had sent. Trust and trustworthiness were common, even between strangers. The game has been played in dozens of countries, and by many groups. It's used to study how much people trust one another, and what affects it.

# The dictator game
@src Forsythe, Horowitz, Savin & Sefton, Games and Economic Behavior, 1994

In the 1990s economists wanted to test whether people share when they don't have to. In a game used by Robert Forsythe and his colleagues in 1994, one student is given a sum of money, such as ten dollars, and is told to divide it between themselves and another student, whom they will never meet. The other student has no say. It's called the dictator game. Under the theory that people are selfish, the dictator would keep it all.

Many of the dictators did keep it all. But a large share gave something, and some split it equally. On average, the gifts were around a fifth of the money, though it varied widely between experiments. People don't give away all that they could, but many give something. The result suggests that fairness matters to people, even when no one is watching. Later studies found that giving drops if the dictator can hide the choice.

# Different cultures, different offers
@src Henrich et al., American Economic Review, 2001

In the ultimatum game, one player offers to split a sum of money, and the other can accept, or reject it, in which case both get nothing. In industrial societies, the typical offer is nearly half, and low offers are often rejected. It was thought to show a universal sense of fairness. In the late 1990s Joseph Henrich and a team of anthropologists played it in fifteen small societies around the world, among foragers, herders and farmers.

The results varied widely. The Machiguenga, in the Peruvian Amazon, made offers of about a quarter, and nearly always accepted. The Lamalera, whale hunters in Indonesia, offered more than half, since they share their catch. The way that people played matched how much they depended on cooperation in daily life, and on markets. It suggests that ideas of fairness are shaped by culture.

# Punishing the unfair
@src Fehr & Fischbacher, Evolution and Human Behavior, 2004

If you see someone cheat another person, and it doesn't affect you, would you pay to punish them? In 2004 Ernst Fehr and Urs Fischbacher tested this. In one experiment, a person was given a sum of money, and could share it with another player, who had nothing. A third person watched, and could pay a price to reduce the first player's pay.

Many of the observers were willing to pay, and punished those who shared too little, in proportion to the degree of unfairness. They didn't gain anything themselves. It's called third-party punishment. It has been found in many cultures, though its strength varies. Some scholars see it as a basis for social norms. It may help to explain why people form groups that enforce rules, without a police force.

# One kindness leads to another
@src Fowler & Christakis, PNAS, 2010

Does being generous encourage others to be generous? In 2010 James Fowler and Nicholas Christakis tested this in a series of public goods games. Players were put into groups of four, and each was given money, which they could contribute to a pot, which was multiplied and shared equally. The groups were changed after every round, so that no one played with the same people twice.

Despite this, people who had received generosity in one round gave more in the next, to new people. A donation made in the first round could lead to an additional contribution of about three times the original, as it spread through the network of people. They called it a cascade of cooperation. The effect shows that behavior is contagious, and that a single act can ripple through a community.

# Watched by a picture of eyes
@src Bateson, Nettle & Roberts, Biology Letters, 2006

In a university department in Newcastle, England, staff kept a shelf of coffee and tea supplies, with an honesty box for people to pay what they owed. The price list was displayed. In 2006 Melissa Bateson and her colleagues did an experiment. Each week, they put a new image above the price list. It was either a picture of flowers, or a pair of eyes looking at the viewer. They alternated the two for ten weeks.

In the weeks with the eyes, people paid almost three times as much for their drinks as in the weeks with flowers. No one noticed the pattern. It's an example of how the feeling of being watched, even by a picture, makes people behave better. The effect has been found in other studies, though a few have not found it. It suggests that reputation is a strong force.

# Does a white coat change your thinking?
@src Adam & Galinsky, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 2012
@conf contested

In 2012 Hajo Adam and Adam Galinsky proposed the idea of enclothed cognition, that the clothes we wear can influence the way we think. In one experiment, people put on a white coat, and did an attention task, in which they were asked to spot differences in pictures. Those who were told that it was a doctor's coat did better than those who were told that it was a painter's coat, or who just saw a doctor's coat.

The authors said that it was both the symbolic meaning of the coat, and the act of wearing it, that mattered. The finding was widely reported. Later attempts to repeat the studies have been mixed, and some large studies found no effect. It's one of many results from social priming that are now regarded with caution. Clothes may well affect how we feel, though, and how others see us.

# Hot days and hit batters
@src Larrick, Timmerman, Carton & Abrevaya, Psychological Science, 2011

Does heat make people more aggressive? In 2011 Richard Larrick and his colleagues examined data on almost sixty thousand Major League Baseball games over several decades. They looked at when pitchers hit batters with the ball, and in particular whether the pitchers did so in retaliation after one of their own batters had been hit.

When the temperature was higher, a pitcher was more likely to hit an opposing batter after a teammate had been hit, and the effect grew with each degree. Without a previous incident, heat made no difference to the rate of hit batters, so it was not that the ball was harder to control. It was that heat increased the urge to retaliate. The study fits with others, which link hot weather to violent crime.
