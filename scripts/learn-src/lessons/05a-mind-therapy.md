# Cognitive behavioral therapy
@src Beck, 1979; Hofmann et al., 2012

Aaron Beck was a psychiatrist who set out in the early 1960s to test a Freudian theory of depression and ended up noticing something else. His patients had a running stream of negative thoughts that they hardly noticed, and those thoughts seemed to be driving their mood. They would think things like "I always fail" or "nobody cares," and then feel exactly as bad as those thoughts would suggest.

He built a short, practical therapy around spotting those thoughts and testing them against the facts. It has since been studied in hundreds of trials, and it helps with depression and anxiety. Many of its tools can be learned from a good book, which is part of why it spread so widely. The core idea is gentle. You don't have to argue yourself into feeling happy. You only have to check whether the thought was true.

# The thought record
@src Beck, Cognitive Therapy of Depression (1979)

The thought record is one of the core exercises of cognitive therapy, and it is very simple. You write down a situation, the thought that came to mind, and how strongly you felt it. Then you list the evidence for the thought and the evidence against it, and you come up with a more balanced version.

The aim isn't to replace a bad thought with a positive one. A forced cheerful thought usually doesn't convince anybody, including you. The aim is to check whether the first thought was accurate, because it's usually only one of several ways to read what happened. A friend who doesn't call back might be angry, or busy, or ill. Writing the alternatives down slows your mind enough to see that the first story isn't the only one.

# All-or-nothing thinking
@src Burns, Feeling Good (1980)

David Burns, who popularized Beck's ideas in his 1980 book Feeling Good, listed this one first among the common thinking traps. Things are either perfect or a failure, with nothing in between. "I missed one step in the plan, so the whole day is ruined."

Real life is mostly in the gray area, and so is most of what we do. A day can have a bad hour and a good evening. A diet can have a slip and still be a diet. A good habit is to catch words like "always," "never" and "completely," and ask what a more accurate description would be, such as "mostly went well, with one miss." It isn't a way of excusing yourself. It's a way of describing what happened without exaggerating in either direction.

# Catastrophizing
@src Beck, 1979; Burns, 1980

A headache becomes a brain tumor. A late reply becomes the end of the friendship. Catastrophizing means leaping to the worst possible outcome and treating it as likely, and the mind can do it in a few seconds, with no new information at all.

One standard way to deflate it is to ask three questions. What's the worst that could happen? What's the most likely thing to happen? And how would I cope if the worst did happen? Answering them honestly usually shows that the worst case is unlikely, and that you would manage better than you fear. You don't have to talk yourself into believing that everything will be fine. It's enough to see that you have more options than the panic suggested.

# Mind reading
@src Burns, 1980

"She didn't text back, so she must be angry with me." We often feel sure that we know what others are thinking, especially about us, and the guess is usually negative. It feels like perception, and it's actually a guess.

In truth, people are busy, distracted and mostly thinking about their own lives. Most of them are far less focused on you than you imagine. When you notice yourself mind reading, it can help to list other explanations for what you saw. If it matters, ask the person. A guess is not a fact, even when it feels like one, and a quick, kind question is often enough to replace a week of worrying.

# Emotional reasoning
@src Burns, 1980

"I feel guilty, so I must have done something wrong." "I feel anxious, so something bad is about to happen." Emotional reasoning treats a feeling as evidence about the world, as though the strength of the feeling proved the thing it seems to say.

Feelings do tell you something, but mostly about your body and your state of mind. Guilt can come from a rule you were taught long ago, and anxiety can come from too little sleep or too much coffee. A helpful move is to name the feeling and then check the facts separately, as if you were a fair-minded friend. You can take the feeling seriously without letting it be the judge. "I feel afraid" is true. "Therefore I am in danger" needs its own evidence.

# Should statements
@src Ellis, 1962; Burns, 1980

Words like "should," "must" and "ought" set rigid rules for how we, or other people, have to behave. Aimed at ourselves, they produce guilt: "I should be over this by now." Aimed at others, they produce anger: "They should know better."

The psychologist Albert Ellis called this habit "musturbation," and he meant it to make people smile. The point was that rules which feel like laws of nature are usually preferences in disguise. Swapping "should" for "I would like to" or "it would help if" tends to keep the standard but drop the whip. You still care about doing well, and you can still hope others will behave well. You just stop punishing yourself and everyone else every time the world falls short.

# Labeling
@src Burns, 1980

One mistake becomes a whole identity: "I'm a failure," "I'm an idiot," "I'm unlovable." It's an extreme form of overgeneralizing, and it's harsh in a way we would never be with a friend. If a friend dropped a plate, we wouldn't tell them they're a disaster.

It also makes change seem impossible, because you can't change what you are. A label feels like a fact about your nature, so there's nothing to do but suffer. Instead, describe the behavior: "I made a mistake on that task." A mistake is something you did, and you can learn from what you did. That small switch in wording turns a verdict into a problem, and problems, unlike verdicts, can be worked on.

# Personalization
@src Burns, 1980

When something goes wrong, we may assume it's our fault, even when it wasn't mainly. A child has a bad day at school. A partner is in a mood. A team misses a target. The mind quickly asks what I did, and finds something.

Usually there are many causes: other people's choices, circumstances, luck. One way to see this is to write a pie chart of everything that contributed, and put your own share last. Most people find that their share is much smaller than the one they'd been blaming themselves for. This isn't about dodging responsibility. If your share is real, you can own it. It's about giving the right size to the part that is yours, and no more.

# The ABC model
@src Ellis, 1962

Albert Ellis taught in the 1950s that what upsets us isn't events themselves, but our beliefs about them. A is the activating event, B is the belief, and C is the consequence, the emotion. We tend to see A and C and miss B, which sits in between and does the work.

Two people miss the same train. One thinks, "This is a disaster, I'll be late and in trouble," and feels panic. The other thinks, "Well, time for a coffee," and feels fine. The event was identical, and the beliefs made the difference. That's good news, because beliefs can be questioned in a way that events can't. When you're upset, try asking what you were telling yourself in the moment before the feeling arrived.

# Behavioral activation
@src Jacobson et al., 1996; Dimidjian et al., 2006

When you're low, it's natural to wait until you feel better to do things. But the lack of activity makes the low mood worse, and a cycle starts: you do less, you feel worse, and so you do even less. Behavioral activation reverses it.

You schedule small activities that matter to you, such as a walk or a phone call, and do them whether or not you feel like it. Mood often follows action, and not the other way around. In a 1996 trial, activation alone worked about as well as full cognitive therapy for depression, and a later trial found it comparable to medication. It's a humble idea with solid support. You don't have to feel ready to begin. You only have to start with something small.

# Opposite action
@src Linehan, DBT Skills Training Manual (2015)

Marsha Linehan's dialectical behavior therapy includes a skill for when an emotion pushes you to do something that isn't helpful. Sadness says to hide in bed. Anger says to lash out. Fear says to avoid. Each emotion comes with an urge, and following the urge usually makes the feeling last.

Opposite action means doing the reverse in small ways: reaching out to a friend, being gentle, approaching what you fear. You do it with your whole attention, not grudgingly. It works when the feeling is out of proportion to the situation. It isn't meant to override feelings that fit the facts. If you're afraid of a real danger, avoiding it is wise. But if you're afraid of a phone call, making it will show your mind that the call is survivable.

# Radical acceptance
@src Linehan, 2015

Marsha Linehan draws a line between pain and suffering. Pain is what happens: the loss, the diagnosis, the end of the marriage. Suffering is what we add by fighting the fact that it happened, with thoughts like "this shouldn't be happening" or "it isn't fair."

Radical acceptance means fully acknowledging what is, in your mind and in your body. It doesn't mean approving, liking it, or giving up on changing things. You can accept that something happened and still work to make the future different. It means no longer spending your energy on the argument with reality, an argument that nobody wins. Pain is hard enough. Acceptance is a way of not paying twice.

# Wise mind
@src Linehan, 1993

In dialectical behavior therapy, there are said to be three states of mind. Emotional mind is driven by feelings and tends to be impulsive. Reasonable mind is driven by facts and logic, and it can miss what matters. Wise mind is where they overlap, the place where you know something is right and can feel it too.

Most of us have had moments like that, when a decision felt calm and true. Linehan teaches people to pause, breathe and ask from that place, "What do I really want to do here?" It's not a mystical idea. It's a way of putting your feelings and your reasoning in the same room and letting them both speak, before you act on either one alone.

# TIPP skills
@src Linehan, DBT Skills Training Manual (2015)

When distress spikes so hard that thinking clearly is out of reach, Linehan's group suggests changing the body first. TIPP stands for Temperature, Intense exercise, Paced breathing and Paired muscle relaxation. Each one acts on your physical alarm state directly.

Cold water on your face, a minute of hard exercise or a long slow exhale quickly shifts your body out of its emergency mode. They're meant for the moments when you're flooded, not for solving the underlying problem, which you can tackle once you're calmer. Trying to reason with yourself at the peak of panic rarely works. First lower the volume with your body, and then take up the question again.

# Cognitive defusion
@src Hayes, Strosahl & Wilson, 1999

In acceptance and commitment therapy, you learn to treat thoughts as passing events in the mind, and not as truths. Instead of "I'm worthless," you say, "I'm having the thought that I'm worthless." It's a small change in wording, and it changes your relationship to the thought.

The thought may not vanish, but it loosens its grip, because you're now watching it instead of being it. It can sound like a small trick, but with practice it creates a little space between you and what your mind says, and in that space you can choose how to act. You don't have to win an argument with your own mind. You only have to notice that a thought is a thought, and keep doing what matters.

# Values versus goals
@src Hayes, Strosahl & Wilson, 1999

In acceptance and commitment therapy, a goal is something you can finish, like attending your child's recital. A value is a direction you keep moving in and never complete, like being a loving parent. You can tick off a goal, but you can't be finished with a value.

Goals can be missed, and then it can feel like failure. Values can always be lived, in some small way, today. When life has been knocked off course, as it can be by a divorce or a loss, asking what you value and what one small step fits it can show you where to go. Even on a bad day, there's usually some way to move in the direction you care about, even if it's only a kind word.

# Mindfulness-based stress reduction
@src Kabat-Zinn, 1982; Goyal et al., 2014

In 1979 Jon Kabat-Zinn started a stress clinic at the University of Massachusetts Medical School. Patients came for an eight-week course that combined meditation, body awareness and gentle yoga. Many of them had chronic pain or illnesses that medicine had not been able to fix.

It has been tested in many trials since. A large review in 2014 found moderate evidence that it helps with anxiety, depression and pain, about as much as other active treatments. It's not a cure-all, and it doesn't work for everyone, but it gives many people a skill they can keep using. The practice is simple to describe: pay attention on purpose, to the present moment, without judging it. It is much harder to do than to say.

# Mindfulness and relapse
@src Teasdale et al., 2000

Depression often comes back. Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale developed a version of mindfulness training meant to help prevent that, by teaching people to notice the early signs of a downward slide and not get pulled in.

In a 2000 trial, people who had had three or more episodes of depression were followed for a year. The relapse rate was 37% for those who took the course and 66% for those who got usual care. For people with fewer episodes, there was no difference. So the benefit was clearest for those with the most to lose, which makes sense, since those are the people whose minds have learned the path best. It's an encouraging result for anyone with a history of low periods.

# Expressive writing
@src Pennebaker & Beall, 1986; Frattaroli, 2006
@conf mixed

In the 1980s James Pennebaker asked people to write about the most upsetting experience of their lives for 15 to 20 minutes on each of several days. Some later showed improvements in health, such as fewer doctor visits. The idea was that putting a painful experience into words helps the mind make sense of it.

Later reviews found that the average effect is small and varies from person to person. It's not a treatment, and it can hurt a little at the time, since writing about something painful brings it back. Still, it seems safe for most people, and many find that it helps them sort out what they feel. If you try it, write for yourself and don't worry about how it reads. Nobody needs to see it.

# Scheduled worry time
@src Borkovec et al., 1983

If you can't stop worrying, one method is to give worry its own appointment. Thomas Borkovec's work in the 1980s led to a technique where you pick a time, say 15 to 30 minutes in the early evening, and postpone your worries until then.

When one comes up during the day, you jot it down and tell yourself "later." Many people find that the urge fades by the time the appointed hour comes, and some of the worries no longer seem worth the time. During the appointment you can think about them as hard as you like. It works because the mind finds it easier to put something off than to be told to stop. It also teaches that worries don't have to be answered the moment they appear.

# Motivational interviewing
@src Miller & Rollnick, 1991

William Miller and Stephen Rollnick developed this style of conversation in the 1980s while working with people who had addictions. They noticed that arguing for change usually makes people argue back. The more you push, the more they defend what they're doing.

So instead, the helper asks open questions, listens carefully and invites the person to talk about both sides of their mixed feelings. When people hear themselves say why they might change, they're more likely to do it. It works well when someone is unsure, and it's useful in everyday life as well. If a friend is stuck on something, you'll often do more good by asking what they think than by telling them what to do.

# The stages of change
@src Prochaska & DiClemente, 1983
@conf mixed

In the 1980s James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente studied how people quit smoking and described a series of stages. In precontemplation, they aren't thinking about it. In contemplation, they're weighing it up. Then come preparation, action and maintenance.

Relapse is common, and it counts as part of the cycle, not as failure. Many people go around several times before a change lasts. Researchers debate whether the stages are truly distinct, and some studies have not supported the model in its strict form. But the idea that readiness matters is widely used. It reminds us that someone who isn't ready can't be pushed into it, and that we ourselves may be at a different stage for different things.

# Self-determination theory
@src Deci & Ryan, 2000

Edward Deci and Richard Ryan studied what keeps people motivated and found three basic needs. Autonomy is the sense that you're making your own choices. Competence is feeling capable at what you do. Relatedness is feeling connected to other people.

When these needs are met, people are more motivated, more creative and happier. The pattern holds across many cultures. It helps explain why pressure works poorly for long. It takes away autonomy, so even good activities start to feel like chores. If you want to keep up a habit, ask whether you've chosen it yourself, whether you're getting any better at it, and whether anyone shares it with you.

# Locus of control
@src Rotter, 1966

In 1966 Julian Rotter described a difference between people who believe their lives are shaped mainly by their own actions, and those who believe it's mainly luck, fate or other people. He called these an internal and an external locus of control.

An internal locus tends to go with better coping, healthier habits and more achievement. But the world isn't fully controllable, and an accurate sense of what is in your power matters too. Someone who believes they control everything can feel crushed when things go wrong. The useful skill is telling apart what you can influence from what you can't, and putting your effort into the first while making peace with the second.

# Self-efficacy
@src Bandura, 1977

Albert Bandura introduced the term in 1977 for your belief in your ability to do a specific task. It's different from general confidence. You might feel sure you can cook a dinner and unsure you can give a speech.

It predicts whether you'll try, how hard you'll work and whether you'll keep going after a setback. It grows mainly from small successes, from watching someone like you succeed, from encouragement and from managing your stress. That's practical advice in disguise. If you want to build your belief that you can do something big, set a goal you can actually reach, reach it, and then set the next one. Each success is evidence you can show yourself.

# Learned helplessness
@src Seligman & Maier, 1967; Maier & Seligman, 2016

In the 1960s Martin Seligman and Steven Maier gave dogs shocks that they could not escape. Later, when the dogs could easily avoid shocks by jumping over a barrier, many just lay down and took them. It looked as though they had learned that nothing they did mattered.

In 2016 Maier and Seligman revised the theory. They concluded that passivity is the brain's default response to prolonged pain, and that what has to be learned is that you do have control. That's a hopeful reading. It suggests that helplessness isn't something we acquire so much as something we need to unlearn, and that even small experiences of making a difference can start to turn it around.

# Explanatory style
@src Seligman, Learned Optimism (1990)

Martin Seligman found that people differ in how they explain bad events to themselves. Pessimists tend to see causes as permanent, personal and pervasive: "It will always be like this, it's my fault, and it ruins everything."

Optimists see them as temporary, specific and partly outside their control: "This is a rough patch, it's about this one situation, and some of it wasn't up to me." Explanatory style predicted sales success at an insurance company, and it predicted how people recovered from low mood. It can be changed with practice. The trick is to notice the words "always," "my fault" and "everything" in your own thinking, and to try a more precise version.

# Resilience is the usual outcome
@src Bonanno, 2004

George Bonanno studied how people cope with losses like the death of a spouse. Many experts had expected most of them to go through a long period of deep distress, and to be worse off if they did not.

He found that the most common pattern was a short time of grief followed by a return to normal functioning, and that a minority did have prolonged difficulty. Being resilient doesn't mean not hurting. It means that people are, on the whole, sturdier than they expect. If you are in the middle of something terrible, it's worth knowing that most people who go through it come out the other side, and the odds are with you.

# The dual process model of grief
@src Stroebe & Schut, 1999

In 1999 Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut proposed that grieving well isn't a straight line through stages. People swing back and forth between two kinds of coping. In loss-oriented moments, they face the pain, think about the person and miss them.

In restoration-oriented moments, they deal with daily life and new roles, and take breaks from grief. The back and forth is normal and healthy. Taking a break from sadness isn't avoiding it, and it isn't a betrayal of what you've lost. It's part of how people carry a loss over a long time. If you find yourself laughing at a movie in the middle of a hard month, there's nothing wrong with you.

# Prolonged grief disorder
@src DSM-5-TR, 2022

Grief is not an illness. Most people find that the sharpest pain eases over time, even if the missing never entirely goes away. It comes in waves, and the waves get further apart.

For some, intense longing and distress continue for a year or more and make daily life difficult. In 2022 the diagnostic manual added prolonged grief disorder for that situation. It is a name for grief that's stuck, not a judgment about how much someone loved. It responds to treatment, which can help people find a way to live with the loss. If the weight isn't lifting after a long time, asking for help is reasonable, and it isn't a sign of weakness.

# Post-traumatic growth
@src Tedeschi & Calhoun, 1996
@conf mixed

In 1996 Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun described how some people report positive changes after a crisis: closer relationships, a greater appreciation of life, new priorities or a stronger sense of purpose. They called it post-traumatic growth.

It's a real experience for many people. But not everyone has it, and no one should feel that they have to. Researchers also point out that people's own reports of growth don't always match how they're actually doing later, so the picture is uncertain. A fair way to hold it is that growth can come alongside pain, and doesn't cancel it. You can wish it hadn't happened and still be glad of what you learned.

# Divorce is a process
@src Amato, 2000

Paul Amato, who has studied divorce for decades, describes it as a long series of changes, not a single event. Conflict before the split, the move, the finances, parenting arrangements and new relationships each affect how people cope.

Most adults adjust over time. But the path varies a lot, and the stress people feel depends heavily on what follows the separation. So how things go afterward is partly within your influence. A steady routine, a stable place to live and decent relations with your former partner can make a real difference to how you feel a year on. The first months are rarely a good guide to how it will go.

# Most children of divorce do fine
@src Hetherington & Kelly, 2002

E. Mavis Hetherington followed families through divorce for decades in the Virginia Longitudinal Study. About 75 to 80 percent of the children from divorced families were functioning well as adults, compared with about 90 percent of those from intact families.

So most children cope, though a minority struggle more, and the difference between 80 and 90 percent is real. What helped them was steady parenting, low conflict between the parents and a warm relationship with at least one adult. Those are things that a parent can work on, even in the middle of a hard time. The study doesn't say divorce is painless for children, but it does say that most children come through it, and that what happens afterward matters a great deal.

# Social support buffers stress
@src Cohen & Wills, 1985

In 1985 Sheldon Cohen and Thomas Wills reviewed the research on how social support affects health. They found that having people to lean on protects against the harm of high stress. It is like a cushion that matters most when the blow is hardest.

What matters most is the sense that if you needed help, someone would be there. That perception may be as protective as actually receiving help. It's one reason that staying in touch with the people who care about you pays off during hard times. You don't have to be using them constantly. Knowing that they are there is already doing some of the work, so keep the calls and the coffees going.

# Loneliness and health
@src Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010

In 2010 Julianne Holt-Lunstad and her colleagues combined 148 studies covering more than 300,000 people. They found that people with strong social relationships had a 50 percent greater likelihood of survival over the follow-up periods than those with weak ones.

The size of the effect was comparable with quitting smoking, and larger than that of obesity or inactivity. It's not just that the lonely are sicker to begin with. Connection seems to matter for staying well, though the research can't prove cause and effect. Still, it's a good reason to treat friendships as a part of your health and not as a luxury. A regular call or meal is worth planning, especially when life is hard.

# Gratitude practices
@src Emmons & McCullough, 2003; Davis et al., 2016
@conf mixed

Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough asked people to write weekly about things they were grateful for. In 2003 they reported that, compared with people who wrote about hassles, the gratitude group felt better about their lives and were more optimistic.

A later review found the average benefit is modest. So it's a helpful habit that costs nothing, though it isn't a cure for depression and shouldn't be used to push down real pain. It seems to work best if you write about specific things and about people, and not generalities. "My neighbor shoveled my walk" does more than "I'm grateful for kindness." Ten minutes once a week may be all it takes.

# Three good things
@src Seligman et al., 2005
@conf mixed

Martin Seligman and his colleagues asked people to write down three things that went well each day, and why they went well, for one week. In a 2005 study, those who did so were happier and less depressed for up to six months afterward.

Later studies have found smaller or shorter-lived effects, so the results are mixed. It takes only a few minutes a night, and many people find that it shifts their attention toward what's good, which can be hard to see on a rough day. The "why" part matters. Asking why a good thing happened reminds you of what you did, or what others did for you. It's a small thing, and worth trying for a week to see if it helps you.

# Savoring
@src Bryant & Veroff, 2007

Fred Bryant and Joseph Veroff, who wrote the book on savoring, describe it as deliberately paying attention to a pleasant experience so that you enjoy it more and it lasts longer. It's the opposite of rushing through a good moment on the way to the next one.

They suggest sharing it with someone, taking mental snapshots, and thinking about it afterward. You can savor the past through memories, the present through full attention, and the future through anticipation. It's a skill that can be practiced. Next time you have a good cup of coffee or a nice view, try giving it your full attention for a minute. Most of us have more pleasant moments than we notice, because we're busy thinking about something else.

# Awe
@src Keltner & Haidt, 2003; Piff et al., 2015

The psychologist Dacher Keltner studies awe, the feeling you get before something vast that changes how you see the world. It could be a mountain range, a piece of music or a big idea. It is a mixture of wonder and a sense of being small.

In his research, awe makes people feel smaller in a good way, more connected to others and more generous. It also seems to quiet self-focused worry. You don't need a trip to the Grand Canyon to feel it. Walks in nature and looking at the night sky can do it, and so can a great piece of music or a story of someone's courage. When your own problems feel huge, a dose of something larger can put them in a different light.

# Emotional granularity
@src Barrett et al., 2001

Lisa Feldman Barrett found that some people describe their feelings with precision, telling "anxious" from "disappointed" from "ashamed," and others lump everything together as "bad" or "awful." She called the first skill emotional granularity.

The people with finer distinctions cope with stress more flexibly. They seem to have more options, since a precise label points to a particular response. Disappointment calls for adjusting your plans, while shame calls for self-compassion, and a vague "bad" calls for nothing in particular. Building your emotional vocabulary can be as simple as pausing to ask what exactly you're feeling. Often a better word is the first step to knowing what to do.

# The stress response
@src Cannon, 1915

Walter Cannon, a physiologist at Harvard, described in 1915 how the body prepares for fight or flight. Your heart races, your breathing quickens and your muscles tense. Blood moves to your limbs, and digestion slows down.

The system evolved for physical danger, but it responds to an urgent email just as well. Freezing is another common response, when neither fight nor flight seems possible. The good news is that the system can be switched off. Slow breathing, a walk and talking with a friend all tell the body that it's safe to stand down. When you feel that surge in a situation that doesn't need your muscles, it helps to remember that it's an old alarm, and that you can settle it.
