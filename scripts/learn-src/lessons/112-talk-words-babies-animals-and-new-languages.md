# Gravy, said two ways
@src Gumperz, Heathrow airport cafeteria study, 1970s

In the mid-1970s the linguist John Gumperz was called to Heathrow Airport in London to look at a problem. New cafeteria workers from India and Pakistan and the baggage handlers who ate there each said that the other side was rude. Neither group could point to anything that had been said that was actually insulting.

Gumperz recorded their conversations and played the recordings back to both groups. When a customer pointed at the gravy, the British servers said "gravy?" with a rising voice, which sounded like an offer. The South Asian servers said "gravy" with a falling voice, which the baggage handlers heard as a flat statement, as if it meant take it or leave it. The newcomers had meant a polite offer.

By its own habits, each group was behaving well. Gumperz argued that the trouble lay in small signals, such as the rise and fall of the voice, which people use to show what they mean and rarely notice. The case later became part of a training film, called Crosstalk. A great deal of what feels like rudeness may be a difference in habit that no one has ever named.

# Students who acted like lodgers
@src Garfinkel, Studies in Ethnomethodology, 1967

In the 1960s the sociologist Harold Garfinkel, who taught at UCLA, asked his students to do something odd at home. For anywhere from fifteen minutes to an hour they were to behave like polite lodgers in their own family's house, using the formal manners one shows a stranger. He called such tasks breaching experiments, and he described them in his 1967 book, Studies in Ethnomethodology.

The families reacted with astonishment, bewilderment, shock, embarrassment or anger, and some demanded to know what had come over the student. Many of the students found it hard to keep up the act, because looking at their own family the way a stranger would turned out to be uncomfortable.

Garfinkel was not trying to prove anything with controls and numbers. He wanted to show that everyday life runs on rules that nobody states, and that they become visible only when somebody breaks them. The anger of the families was the evidence that the rules were real, and that everyone had been following them without ever being taught them aloud.

# Did the Hopi have no word for time?
@conf mixed
@src Whorf, 1930s and 1940s; Malotki, Hopi Time, 1983

Benjamin Lee Whorf spent twenty-two years working as a fire prevention engineer for the Hartford Fire Insurance Company, and he studied languages in his own time. He died in 1941, at the age of 44. In his writings on the Hopi language of Arizona he argued that Hopi speakers think about time differently from English speakers, partly because Hopi does not treat units of time as nouns, in the way that English treats days and hours.

The idea spread, and it turned into the popular claim that the Hopi had no concept of time at all. In 1983 the linguist Ekkehart Malotki published a study of about 600 pages on the grammar of time in Hopi, with hundreds of Hopi sentences taken from his own field notes. It showed that the language has words and forms that refer to time, and many linguists took it as a refutation.

Some scholars say that Malotki attacked a popular myth more than Whorf's actual claim, which was about how time is conceptualized, and Malotki is reported to have agreed. The Hopi case does not settle the wider question of whether the language we speak shapes the way we think. That question is still studied, with other languages and other methods.

# The dog who learned 1,022 names
@src Pilley & Reid, 2011; PBS NOVA and NPR reports

Chaser was a border collie whose owner, John Pilley, was a psychology professor at Wofford College in South Carolina. Pilley and a colleague, Alliston Reid, taught her the names of toys one at a time. He repeated a name, and then hid the toy for her to find. After about three years of this she knew 1,022 names, and they published the results in 2011.

The one-at-a-time method was deliberate. Most people who train a dog put many objects on the floor at once, and Pilley's team used successive teaching instead, with a single object and a single name. Play was a large part of the training, and Chaser was asked again and again to pick out a named toy from among many.

The study does not show that a dog has language. Chaser learned names of objects, and there is no sign that she learned grammar. But it does show that a dog can store more than a thousand arbitrary links between a sound and a thing, and fetch the right one from a pile. Pilley died in 2018 at the age of 89, and Chaser died in 2019, at fifteen.

# The dog who guessed a new word
@src Kaminski, Call & Fischer, Science, 2004

Rico was a border collie in Germany who, by his owners' account, knew the names of more than 200 items. In 2004 the researchers Juliane Kaminski, Josep Call and Julia Fischer tested him with something new. They put a toy he had never seen among seven toys he knew, and asked him to fetch it by a name he had never heard. Rico fetched the new toy in seven of ten tests, and picked it out again four weeks later.

He seems to have reasoned by exclusion. The word was new, so it probably meant the one toy that had no name yet. Children do much the same, and psychologists call the quick learning of a word after one meeting fast mapping. In the same issue of Science, the psychologist Paul Bloom wrote a commentary called Can a Dog Learn a Word?

Later critics said that the test four weeks afterward did not prove fast mapping, since Rico could have gotten the right toy by exclusion a second time. So the careful conclusion is that a dog can use a rule of exclusion, which is a part of how children learn words, and the question of how closely it matches a child's learning is still argued.

# One thing, one name
@src Markman & Wachtel, 1988; Lewis et al., 2020

Picture a toddler who knows the word ball. On the floor there is a ball, and a strange kitchen tool. A grown-up says, "Give me the dax." Most toddlers hand over the strange tool, even though dax could in principle be another name for the ball. In 1988 Ellen Markman and Gwyn Wachtel reported six studies of this kind, and they gave the effect the name mutual exclusivity.

Their idea was that children begin by assuming that each thing has only one name. A new word then most likely points to something that has no name yet. This is a useful shortcut, since a child learning a language faces endless possible meanings for each new word. A 2020 review of many studies found that young children do tend to match a new word to a new object.

Researchers still argue about why. Some say children are using what they know about words. Others, such as Gil Diesendruck and Lori Markson in 2001, argued that children are reasoning about what the speaker knows. The rule is not perfect, since things do have more than one name, and children learn that later.

# The wug test
@src Berko, Word, 1958

In 1958 Jean Berko, a graduate student at Radcliffe College in Harvard's circle, wanted to know whether children learn the rules of English or simply memorize each word. She made up a set of pictures of imaginary creatures and actions, and gave them nonsense names. She showed a child a picture and said, "This is a wug. Now there is another one. There are two of them. There are two what?"

Children as young as four said "wugs." They could not have heard the word before, so they must have worked out the rule for plurals and applied it to a word that was new. They did the same with other endings, making the past tense, the possessive and the present participle of nonsense words.

The work was part of her dissertation, and her adviser was Roger Brown. It is often credited as the first clear demonstration that young children pull out the rules of grammar and do not merely repeat what they have heard. She later became known as Jean Berko Gleason. The wug has since become a famous creature in the study of language.

# Why children say "goed", and how rarely they do
@conf mixed
@src Marcus, Pinker et al., Monographs of the SRCD, 1992

Most parents have heard a child say "goed" or "foots." It is called overregularization, because the child applies the regular rule to a word that is irregular. It is often described as a stage. First the child says "went," by copying. Then the child learns the rule for adding "ed" and says "goed." Finally the child goes back to "went."

In 1992 Gary Marcus, Steven Pinker and their colleagues studied more than 11,000 irregular past tense forms in the speech of many children. They found that the children said the right form most of the time. The median rate of overregularization was only about 2.5 percent of the irregular past tense forms. The errors began only after children had started to mark regular verbs reliably, and they stayed at a low rate for years.

The figures have been challenged. Michael Maratsos argued in 2000 that overall rates look low partly because of how samples are inspected, and that some children, such as one named Abe, went through long periods when the error was common. Later work by Maslen and colleagues in 2004 also questioned how low the rates are. Children's errors are real, but they are not the whole story.

# The same sound, heard two ways
@src Ganong, J. Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 1980

Speech sounds can be made ambiguous on a computer. In 1980 William Ganong made a series of sounds that moved in small steps from a d to a t, and put the same ending after each. With the ending "ash," one end of the series was "dash" and the other was "tash." Only one of them is a word.

Listeners labeled the in-between sounds as the one that made a real word. They heard "dash" far more often than "tash." With an ending such as "ask," where a t makes the word, the boundary shifted the other way. The identical sound was heard as a d in one case and as a t in the other. The effect was strongest for the sounds that were the most ambiguous.

Ganong took it as evidence that what we know about words reaches down into the basic perception of speech sounds. Others argue that it arises later, when we decide what label to give a sound, and the debate has continued, with brain scans and recordings of brain waves. Either way, hearing is not simply receiving a signal. It is shaped by what we expect to hear.

# The listener who helps tell the story
@src Bavelas, Coates & Johnson, J. Personality and Social Psychology, 2000; Yngve, 1970

When someone tells you a story you do not sit silent. You nod, say "mm-hm," wince, or say "no way." In 1970 the linguist Victor Yngve gave these small responses the name back channel. He pointed out that in a conversation both people are speaking and listening at the same time, and that the listener's part is never really empty.

In 2000 Janet Bavelas, Linda Coates and Trudy Johnson tested what these responses do. In two experiments one person told a story and another person listened. Some listeners were given a distracting task, so that they could not follow the story closely. These listeners made fewer responses, especially specific ones that fit the content, such as a wince at a painful moment.

The narrators then told their stories significantly worse, according to the ratings. The authors concluded that listeners are co-narrators. Their responses help illustrate the story, and they change how well the teller performs. A person who is a good listener is part of how a story gets told well.

# We are polite to computers
@src Nass, Moon & Carney, J. Applied Social Psychology, 1999; Reeves & Nass, 1996

Ask a friend whether you are a good cook, and the friend is likely to say yes. Ask a stranger, and you may hear the truth. In 1999 Clifford Nass, Youngme Moon and Paul Carney asked whether people use the same politeness toward machines. In the first study 30 people did a task on a text-based computer. Then they rated how well that computer had done, in one of three ways.

Some answered on the same computer, some on paper, and some on a different but identical computer. Those who answered on the same computer gave kinder and more uniform ratings than the others. A second study, with 30 more people and voice-based computers, got the same result. People gave the machine the courtesy they would give to a person, though a computer has no feelings to hurt.

This was part of a bigger idea in the 1996 book The Media Equation, by Byron Reeves and Clifford Nass. They argued that people tend to treat computers, television and other media as if they were real people and real places. Each of these two studies was small, so the finding is a pointer more than a proof. It suggests that politeness is automatic, and not a choice.

# The code the enemy could not read
@series history
@src Navajo Code Talkers, United States Marine Corps, 1942 to 1945

In 1942 Philip Johnston, the son of missionaries who had grown up on the Navajo reservation, suggested that the Marine Corps use the Navajo language to send secret messages. He argued that it should not be a simple translation of Navajo, but a code built out of Navajo words. In May 1942 the first 29 Navajo recruits began training in California, and they created the code themselves.

When the war began, fewer than thirty people who were not Navajo, and none of them Japanese, could understand the language. The code gave Navajo words to military terms that the language did not have, and the full wartime version grew to about 411 terms. The messages could be sent quickly, and by voice, without the slow work of a cipher machine.

At Iwo Jima in 1945, six Navajo radio networks worked around the clock during the first two days, and by one account they sent more than 800 messages without an error. Major Howard Connor, a signal officer of the 5th Marine Division, credited the Navajo with the success of the landing. The code is reported never to have been broken. Between 350 and 420 Navajo men served as code talkers, and the original 29 were awarded the Congressional Gold Medal in a law signed in 2000.

# The last speaker of Eyak
@src Marie Smith Jones, 1918 to 2008

Marie Smith Jones was born on May 14, 1918, in Cordova, Alaska. She grew up speaking Eyak, a language of the southern coast of Alaska. When her older sister Sophie Borodkin died in 1992, she became the last fluent speaker. People called her Chief Marie.

She died at home in Anchorage on January 21, 2008, at the age of 89. With her, the Eyak language lost its last native speaker. A language can vanish in this way, when the last person who learned it as a child is gone, and it takes with it a way of describing the world that nobody else holds.

But a language is not always lost for good if people kept records. The Manx language of the Isle of Man lost the fisherman Ned Maddrell, who is generally reported as its last native speaker, on December 27, 1974. He was 97. A revival had begun in the 1960s, and the number of people who speak Manx has grown since. Whether a language survives depends on people choosing to learn it.

# Women, fire and dangerous things
@conf mixed
@src Dixon, The Dyirbal Language of North Queensland, 1972; Lakoff, 1987

Dyirbal is an Aboriginal language of northeast Queensland, and it has very few speakers now. Its nouns are sorted into four classes, and each noun is marked with a small word that shows its class. In his 1972 grammar the linguist Robert Dixon described one of these classes, marked with the word balan. It includes human females, water, fire and fighting, and also dogs, the platypus and many birds.

In 1987 George Lakoff took the class as the title of his book, Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things. He used it to argue that categories in the human mind need not be defined by features that all members share. Instead they can be linked in chains, or by stories and beliefs, to a central example.

Not everyone agrees. Dixon wrote in 2015 that Lakoff had misrepresented his account. Other linguists, in a paper titled Women are not dangerous things, argued against the idea that the grouping rests on meaning alone. The Dyirbal classes remain a famous example of how differently languages can sort the world, and of how hard it is to be sure why.

# A language with more than a hundred consonants
@conf mixed
@src Taa language descriptions; Traill; DoBeS project; Maddieson

English has about two dozen consonants, and Hawaiian has just eight. In its central dialect, Rotokas, a language of Papua New Guinea, has only six consonants and five vowels, which makes eleven sounds in all. At the other end of the scale is Taa, a language of southern Africa, which has the largest sound inventory that is commonly cited.

How many sounds it has depends on how linguists count. One tally gives 122 consonants, and another gives 160 sounds counting vowels as well. If some combinations are treated as clusters of two sounds and not as single ones, the number of consonants comes down to about 88. Estimates of the number of click sounds range from 20 to 43.

Clicks are central to the language. One count found that 82 percent of its basic vocabulary words begin with a click. The case shows how much room there is in the human voice, and also that counting sounds depends on rules about what is to count as one. Even the smallest and largest inventories belong to languages that are fully capable of saying anything.

# How a blind child learns what "look" means
@src Landau & Gleitman, Language and Experience: Evidence from the Blind Child, 1985

How can a child who has never seen anything learn the meaning of "look" or "see"? In the 1980s Barbara Landau and Lila Gleitman studied blind children as they learned to talk. Their 1985 book, Language and Experience, described a young blind girl named Kelli, and tested what she understood by words about vision.

When Kelli was told to "look" at something, she explored it with her hands. When she was asked to hide an object from her mother, she put it in her pocket. Her meaning for "look" was not a simple copy of what sighted children mean, and it was not an empty word. She used it for a way of finding out about an object, and she used it in sensible ways.

Landau and Gleitman argued that children can learn a great deal about a word from the sentences it appears in, and not only from what they see. A verb that is used with certain kinds of phrases gives clues to its meaning. It suggests that language is a source of knowledge about the world, and not only a way to report what the senses supply.

# Babies who babble with their hands
@src Petitto & Marentette, Science, 1991

Hearing babies begin to babble in the second half of their first year, repeating syllables such as ba-ba-ba. Is babbling a matter of the mouth, or of language? In 1991 Laura-Ann Petitto and Paula Marentette studied a small group of babies, some who were deaf and were being raised by signing parents, and some who could hear.

The deaf babies produced repetitive hand movements that were like the rhythmic babbling of hearing babies, with simple shapes made over and over. A much larger share of their hand movements were of this kind than was true of the hearing babies. They appeared to be practicing the building blocks of sign, in the same way that the others were practicing the building blocks of speech.

The authors argued that babbling comes from a capacity for language in the brain, and not from the mouth alone. A baby is ready to pick up the patterns of language in whatever form it is offered. Other researchers have debated the interpretation, including a later study that followed deaf and hearing babies over time, so the case is a strong clue and not a final answer.

# Learning words for thoughts
@conf mixed
@src Pyers & Senghas, Psychological Science, 2009

Nicaraguan Sign Language arose in the 1970s and 1980s, when deaf children were brought together in new schools. Each new group of children changed it, so that older signers had learned an early, simple form, while younger signers learned a richer one. This allowed researchers to ask what a language does for the mind.

In 2009 Jennie Pyers and Ann Senghas tested adult and adolescent signers on a false belief task, which asks where a person will look for something that has been moved without her knowledge. They also counted the signs that people used for mental states, such as think and know. Adults who had learned the early form as children used few of these signs, and failed the task. Signers whose language grew over the two years of the study came to understand false belief as it grew.

The authors concluded that learning a language, over and above social experience, drives the development of an understanding of other minds. A later study of deaf homesigners, who have no shared language, found that watching others was not enough to pass the task, even when it used little language. The work does not show which part of language matters.

# A grammar that grew in one village
@src Sandler, Meir, Padden & Aronoff, PNAS, 2005

In a Bedouin village in the Negev desert of Israel, the rate of inherited deafness is high. Within about three generations, around 150 deaf people were born into the community. Deaf villagers are fully part of village life, and a sign language arose there that is not related to the spoken languages or to the sign languages of the region. It is called Al-Sayyid Bedouin Sign Language.

In 2005 Wendy Sandler, Irit Meir, Carol Padden and Mark Aronoff reported on it. They found that the language had a systematic word order, with the subject first, then the object, then the verb. That order differs from the spoken languages around it, and from the sign languages nearby. A consistent order appeared in the signing of the second generation.

The case shows that a language can acquire regular grammar within a few generations, with no teachers and no model to copy. Like Nicaraguan Sign Language, it is one of the few places where researchers have been able to watch grammar being formed. It suggests that some parts of grammar come from the way people communicate, and from the minds that use the language.

# A language that started in how adults spoke to children
@conf mixed
@src O'Shannessy, Australian Journal of Linguistics, 2005

In Lajamanu, a Warlpiri community in the Northern Territory of Australia, adults often switched between Warlpiri, Kriol and English as they talked. The linguist Carmel O'Shannessy found that the switching had settled into a regular pattern, and that children there now learn it as one of their first languages. She named it Light Warlpiri.

It is a mixed language. Most nouns and noun endings come from Warlpiri, while most verbs and verb endings come from Kriol or English. It appears to have started in the 1980s, in the pattern of speech that adults used when talking to young children.

Her later work suggested two steps. Adults switched languages in a consistent way when speaking to small children. Then the children took the mixed speech as a single system, and added changes of their own. Estimates of the number of speakers are around 300 to 350, mostly under the age of 35 or 40. Warlpiri itself is still spoken too, so children there grow up with both.

# Washoe and the signs
@conf mixed
@src Gardner & Gardner, from 1966; Britannica and National Geographic reports

In 1966 Allen and Beatrix Gardner, at the University of Nevada in Reno, began to raise a young chimpanzee named Washoe, and to teach her American Sign Language. Earlier attempts to teach chimpanzees to speak aloud had failed. Washoe lived in a trailer in the Gardners' yard, and the people around her were not allowed to speak.

By the Gardners' strict test, which was that a sign had to be used spontaneously and appropriately, she learned at least 130 signs. Other counts give 250 or 350, depending on how learning is defined. The Gardners reported that she put signs together in new ways, such as "water bird" for a swan.

Skeptics said that such a phrase could be just the naming of two things, the water and the bird, one after another, and that trainers may have cued her signs without meaning to. The question of whether an ape can learn language is therefore still open. Washoe later lived at Central Washington University, and she died in 2007.

# Can an ape create a sentence?
@conf mixed
@src Terrace, Petitto, Sanders & Bever, Science, 1979

Herbert Terrace, a psychologist at Columbia University, raised a chimpanzee named Nim Chimpsky, a joke on the name of the linguist Noam Chomsky, who argued that language is a uniquely human ability. The aim of Project Nim was to teach him sign language, and to see whether he could make sentences.

In 1979 Terrace and his colleagues published a paper in Science called Can an Ape Create a Sentence? They had reviewed more than 19,000 utterances in which Nim used more than one sign. They concluded that there was no clear evidence that apes master the structure of language. Terrace later studied the videotapes closely, frame by frame, and found that many of Nim's signs had been prompted by his teachers' signs just before. The teachers were cuing him without knowing it.

The paper had a large effect, and made researchers far more careful about cues and controls. Terrace said later that Chomsky had been right that a chimpanzee cannot create a sentence, but for the wrong reason, since Nim had not even clearly learned words. Other researchers, working with other apes, did not accept that this settled the matter.

# Kanzi, the bonobo who watched
@conf mixed
@src Savage-Rumbaugh et al., 1980s and 1993; Britannica and Wikipedia accounts

The psychologist Sue Savage-Rumbaugh was trying to teach a bonobo named Matata to use symbols on a keyboard. Matata made slow progress. Her young son Kanzi, who had seemed uninterested, had been watching all along, and within days of being separated from her he began to use the keyboard himself. He had learned by observation, with no training of his own.

In one test, Kanzi and a two-year-old girl named Alia were given 660 spoken instructions that asked them to do things with objects in new ways. Kanzi responded correctly to 74 percent of the instructions and Alia to 65 percent. Savage-Rumbaugh concluded that Kanzi understood the words in order, and she estimated that he understood more than 3,000 spoken English words.

Both the figure and the interpretation have been questioned. The vocabulary count is her own estimate, and a 1996 review said that the training and testing were not fully described, which matters for the claim that the skills were learned without training. Linguists also pointed out that an instruction could be followed by taking the words one at a time, without any grammar at all. The results are striking, but they are not proof of language.
