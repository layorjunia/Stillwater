# The missing link that was found in a gravel pit
@series sci
@src Piltdown Man, 1912 to 1953; De Groote et al., Royal Society Open Science, 2016

In 1912 an amateur archaeologist from Sussex, Charles Dawson, wrote to Arthur Smith Woodward, a Keeper of Geology at the Natural History Museum in London, with astonishing news. He had found part of a human-like skull in the gravel beds near Piltdown, in East Sussex. Later that summer, he and Woodward found more, including a jawbone, a set of teeth and some primitive tools. Dawson announced that he had found the missing link between apes and humans.

The find was taken seriously because Dawson had a reputation as a discoverer of local antiquities. Doubts were voiced almost at once, but Piltdown Man was accepted by many for decades, partly because British scientists were glad to have an early human of their own. Only in 1953 was it shown to be a fraud. The remains were made up of a human skull of modest age and the jaw of an orangutan, stained to look old and with the teeth filed down.

A large review in 2016 concluded that Dawson had done it alone, evidently wanting the recognition of other archaeologists. Some of his other finds were also false. The affair misled the study of human origins for forty years, and was ended by newer methods of dating and testing bones.

# The fairies that fooled a famous author
@series history
@src Cottingley Fairies, 1917 to 1920; Strand Magazine, Christmas 1920

In 1917 two cousins, Elsie Wright, who was 16, and Frances Griffiths, who was 9, took a photograph at Cottingley, near Bradford in England. It showed Frances with a group of little winged figures, which looked like fairies. They took a second photograph, and eventually five in all. The girls said that they were pictures of real fairies.

The photographs reached Sir Arthur Conan Doyle, the writer of the Sherlock Holmes stories, who had a strong interest in spiritualism. He had been asked to write an article on fairies for the Christmas 1920 issue of The Strand Magazine, and he used the photographs. He thought they were clear evidence of the supernatural. The public was divided, and some believed them, while others thought they were faked.

Interest faded, but the pictures remained famous. In the early 1980s, when both women were old, they admitted that four of the photographs were fakes, made with cardboard cutouts of fairies that were copied from a popular children's book. Frances insisted that the fifth was genuine. The case shows how a great mind can be fooled, when it wants to believe.

# The diaries that a magazine paid millions for
@series history
@src Stern, 1983; Konrad Kujau; Hugh Trevor-Roper

In 1983 the German news magazine Stern announced a sensational discovery. It had bought a set of sixty volumes of diaries said to have been written by Adolf Hitler, for 9.3 million Deutsche Marks. Stern then sold the right to publish extracts to newspapers around the world. One of them, the Sunday Times of London, asked the historian Hugh Trevor-Roper to examine them, and he declared them genuine.

At the press conference to announce the publication, Trevor-Roper said that, on reflection, he had changed his mind. Other historians also raised doubts. A proper forensic analysis, which had not been done before the purchase, quickly showed that the diaries were fakes.

They had been forged between 1981 and 1983 by Konrad Kujau, a man from East Germany with a record of small crimes. He had started by selling Nazi souvenirs, and found that he could raise prices by adding forged papers that tied them to the leaders. A Stern journalist, Gerd Heidemann, who was obsessed with the Nazis, had found the diaries and arranged the purchase. The scandal damaged the magazine and the reputation of a great historian, and it is a reminder of what happens when excitement outruns checking.

# The giant that a tobacconist buried
@series history
@src Cardiff Giant, 1869; George Hull; P. T. Barnum

On October 16, 1869, workers digging a well behind a barn in Cardiff, New York, found a huge stone figure of a man. It was ten feet tall and weighed about 3,000 pounds, and it looked like a petrified man. The owner of the farm, William Newell, put up a tent over it and began to charge visitors, and it soon became a major attraction.

It was a hoax, made by a New York tobacconist, George Hull. He was a skeptic, who had lost an argument with a Methodist minister at a revival meeting, about the verse in Genesis that says there were giants on the earth. He wanted to show how easily people could be fooled by an ancient wonder. In 1868 he had a block of gypsum cut from a quarry in Fort Dodge, Iowa, and had it carved into a figure.

The idea of a petrified man was not new. A fake letter in a California paper in 1858 had told of a prospector turned to stone. When showman P. T. Barnum could not buy the giant, he had a copy made and showed it as the real one. Both the Cardiff Giant and the copy can still be seen today.

# The mermaid that was half monkey
@series history
@src Feejee mermaid; P. T. Barnum's American Museum, 1842

In 1842 the showman P. T. Barnum put on show in New York something that he called the Feejee mermaid. It was the upper body of a young monkey sewn to the back half of a fish. It was dried and dark, about three feet long, with its mouth open and its arms thrown up, as if it had died in great pain. Barnum himself described it as an ugly, dried-up, black-looking specimen.

He said that it had been caught near the Fiji Islands. In fact it had been made much earlier. An American sea captain, Samuel Barrett Edes, had bought it from Japanese sailors in 1822, and brought it to the West. Similar objects had been made for sale in Japan for some time, as charms or curiosities.

Barnum advertised it with pictures of beautiful mermaids, and crowds came, though what they saw was quite different. The original is believed to have been destroyed in one of the fires that burned Barnum's collections, and many copies have been shown since. Barnum treated the public's curiosity as something to be sold, and argued that people liked to be fooled, so long as they were entertained.

# The men on the Moon who were never there
@series history
@src The Sun, New York, August 25 to 31, 1835

On August 25, 1835, a New York newspaper, The Sun, began to publish a series of six articles about a great discovery. The astronomer Sir John Herschel, it said, had used a huge new telescope at the Cape of Good Hope, in South Africa, and had seen life on the Moon. The articles said he was helped by a man named Andrew Grant, who did not exist.

The descriptions were detailed. They told of bison, goats with a single horn, small zebras, unicorns, beavers that walked on two legs and had no tails, and bat-winged humanoids, called Vespertilio-homo, who built temples. There were trees, oceans and beaches. The telescope was said to have a lens twenty-four feet across, and to weigh seven tons.

The paper was one of the new penny papers, which were sold cheaply, and readers wanted entertainment as much as news. The articles were never withdrawn, but on September 16 The Sun admitted that they were made up. Herschel, who really was in South Africa, was amused. The hoax is a reminder of an age before the rules of ethical journalism, when a good story counted for more than the facts.

# The monster in the loch and the photograph of a toy
@series history
@src Loch Ness Monster; Surgeon's Photograph, 1934; Adomnán, Life of St. Columba

The Loch Ness Monster is a creature said to live in a lake in the Scottish Highlands. The earliest report of a monster near the loch is in the Life of St. Columba, by Adomnán, written in the seventh century about events of the year 565. But the modern legend began in August 1933, when a local newspaper, the Inverness Courier, reported that a man, George Spicer, had seen a creature crossing the road. Letters poured in, and the name that finally stuck was the Loch Ness monster, which has been affectionately called Nessie since the 1940s.

The most famous picture, the Surgeon's Photograph, was published in 1934. It showed a long neck rising from the water, and was said to have been taken by a London surgeon. For sixty years it was regarded as the best evidence. In 1994 it was revealed to be a hoax. The monster was a toy submarine with a sculpted head and neck.

The scientific community explains the sightings as hoaxes, wishful thinking, and mistakes about known animals, floating objects and natural events. Searches with sonar have found no sign of a large animal. The monster remains a symbol of the pleasure of a mystery, and a lesson in how a single striking image can hold the public for decades.

# The map that put Vikings in the New World
@series history
@src Vinland Map, Yale University; Beinecke Library; Yale announcement, 2018

The Vinland Map seemed to be a wonder. It looked like a world map from the fifteenth century, and it showed, southwest of Greenland, an island named Vinlanda Insula. A note on it said that the land had been visited by Europeans in the eleventh century. It came to light in 1957, and was bought by Yale University. In 1965, Yale announced it with a book, and a publicity campaign, as a genuine map showing that the Vikings knew of America before Columbus.

Specialists in old maps and medieval documents were suspicious as soon as photographs were available. Later chemical tests found that a main ingredient of the ink was a pigment that was made in the twentieth century. After years of debate, Yale announced in 2018 that the latest research had shown that the map was a modern forgery.

The timing is a twist. In 1960, three years after the map appeared, archaeologists did find a real Norse settlement, at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland. So the Vikings did reach North America, as the sagas told. The map was not needed as proof. It remains in the collection of Yale's Beinecke Library, as a record of a forgery.

# The prank that fooled the navy
@series history
@src Horace de Vere Cole; Dreadnought hoax, 1910

In 1910 a prankster named Horace de Vere Cole pulled off one of the most famous practical jokes in British history. He persuaded the Royal Navy to give a tour of its flagship, the battleship HMS Dreadnought, to a group of visitors who were supposed to be the royal family of Abyssinia, now Ethiopia. They were in fact friends of Cole, in disguise, with dark makeup, turbans and robes, speaking in made-up language.

The group included members of the circle that became known as the Bloomsbury Group, among them Adrian Stephen, and his sister, Virginia, who later became the writer Virginia Woolf. The fake delegation was met with full honors, and shown around the ship. The hoax was a repeat of an earlier one, when Cole and Stephen had tricked the mayor of Cambridge, while they were students in 1905.

The affair caused embarrassment in the navy when it became public, and it made headlines. It drew attention to the Bloomsbury circle. Cole was born in Ireland in 1881, and spent much of his life on pranks. The hoax worked because the officials expected a visit from important people, and because no one wanted to be rude to foreign royalty.

# The boy who was in the attic
@series history
@src Balloon boy hoax, October 15, 2009; Larimer County sheriff

On October 15, 2009, a homemade helium balloon shaped like a silver flying saucer drifted up from a yard in Fort Collins, Colorado. The parents, Richard and Mayumi Heene, told the authorities that their six-year-old son, Falcon, was inside it. The balloon rose to 7,000 feet and flew for 90 minutes. National Guard helicopters and police followed it for about 50 miles, and the world watched on television.

When it landed, near Denver, there was no boy. Another object had been seen falling, and a search began. Later in the day the boy was found hiding in the attic of his house, where he had been all along. That evening, in an interview on television, a reporter asked Falcon why he had been hiding, and the boy said to his father that he had said it was for the show.

The sheriff, on October 18, concluded that it was a hoax, a stunt for publicity. The parents faced felony charges, and on November 13 Richard Heene pleaded guilty to attempting to influence a public servant. The case showed how quickly a news story can spread, and how a family's craving for attention can pull in a whole country, but a young child gave it away.

# The professor and the carved stones
@series history
@src Beringer's Lying Stones, Würzburg, 1725

In 1725 Johann Beringer, a physician and professor at the University of Würzburg, in Germany, found some remarkable stones. They were pieces of limestone that had been carved into shapes of animals that had never existed, and some had Hebrew letters on them. Beringer believed that they were fossils. Because some seemed to carry the name of God, he suggested that they might be of divine origin.

At the time, scientists were unsure what fossils were. The idea that they were the remains of once-living creatures was only one of several theories. Beringer hired three young men to dig for him, and they turned up more and more of the stones. A group of colleagues, including a professor of geography and the university's librarian, had decided to play a trick on him, because they found him arrogant.

Beringer published a book on the discovery, and then he found out that it was a hoax. He took the pranksters to court, and won the case, but his reputation was ruined. The stones are called Beringer's Lying Stones. The story is often told as a warning that a person can see what he hopes to see, and that excitement can make a scholar blind to signs of fraud.

# The spaghetti that grew on trees
@series history
@src BBC Panorama, April 1, 1957

On April 1, 1957, the BBC's current affairs program Panorama broadcast a short report about the spaghetti harvest in Switzerland. It showed a family picking strands of spaghetti from trees and laying them out in the sun to dry. The respected announcer Richard Dimbleby spoke the commentary, and talked of a mild winter and the end of a pest called the spaghetti weevil.

The report was an April Fool's joke. At that time spaghetti was not a common food in Britain, and many viewers did not know how it was made. A number of them telephoned the BBC to ask how they could grow their own spaghetti tree. According to a well-known story, the BBC told them to place a sprig of spaghetti in a tin of tomato sauce, and hope for the best.

The broadcast is often called one of the best television hoaxes of all time. It worked because of the trust that people placed in a BBC news program, and in a famous voice, and because of a gap in what they knew. It is also an example of how a hoax can be harmless fun, as the aim was to amuse, and not to cheat or to gain.
