# The periodic table
@src Mendeleev, 1869

Dmitri Mendeleev, a Russian chemist, published his table in 1869. He arranged the known elements by weight and noticed that their properties repeated in a pattern, so that every so often a new row began with an element much like one from the row before.

Where the pattern broke, he did something bold. He left a gap and predicted that an element would be found to fill it, along with what it would be like. Gallium, scandium and germanium were discovered over the next seventeen years, and each matched his descriptions closely. That's what convinced chemists that he was right. A table that had begun as a way of organizing facts had turned into a tool for predicting new ones.

# Atomic number
@src Moseley, 1913

What makes an element what it is? Not its weight, as early chemists thought, but the number of protons in its nucleus. That number is called the atomic number, and it's the real address of an element in the table.

In 1913 the young English physicist Henry Moseley measured the X-rays given off by different elements and found that their frequencies followed a neat series of whole numbers. That fixed the true order of the table, and showed exactly where the gaps were. Change the number of protons in an atom and you've changed the element. Moseley was killed at Gallipoli two years later, at the age of 27, and many consider his death a great loss to science.

# Noble gases
@src Standard chemistry text

Helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon sit in the last column of the table, and they hardly react with anything. Their outer shells of electrons are full, so they have no reason to take part in chemistry. Each atom is happy alone.

That's why airships and balloons now use helium instead of flammable hydrogen, and why the glowing gas in a neon sign doesn't burn or wear out. They were nicknamed "noble" because they keep to themselves, like aristocrats who won't mix with the common crowd. In fact, chemists did make a few compounds of the heavier ones, starting with xenon in 1962, so the name isn't completely true. But "hardly ever reacts" is a good rule.

# Alkali metals
@src Standard chemistry text

The metals in the first column, lithium, sodium, potassium and the rest, are soft enough to cut with a knife and react strongly with water. Lithium fizzes, sodium skates around and burns, and potassium bursts into a lilac flame.

Each atom has a single loose electron on the outside that it's eager to give away. The further you go down the column, the looser the electron and the more violent the reaction. Cesium and rubidium can explode on contact with water. That's why these metals are stored under oil and never found in nature in a pure form. They've always been combined with something, like sodium in table salt.

# Halogens
@src Standard chemistry text

Fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine and astatine are one electron short of a full outer shell, so they're eager to grab one from another atom. That makes them very reactive, and they readily form salts. In fact, "halogen" comes from Greek words meaning "salt-former."

Fluorine is the most reactive of all the elements, and will even react with glass. Chlorine and iodine are used as disinfectants. The halogens are the mirror image of the alkali metals. One group gives away an electron, and the other takes one. When a metal like sodium meets a halogen like chlorine, each gets what it wants, and you get table salt.

# Rare earths are not rare
@src USGS, Rare Earth Elements

The fifteen elements called the lanthanides, plus two relatives, are known as the rare earths, but the name is misleading. Cerium is about as common in Earth's crust as copper. What's rare is finding them concentrated in a spot where mining is worthwhile.

They're also hard to separate from one another, because their chemistry is so alike. They're in many things we use daily: magnets in headphones and electric motors, phone screens, lasers and lighting. The name comes from the 1700s, when chemists found them in unfamiliar minerals and thought they were scarce. Today the bottleneck is less about how much there is than about who is able to refine it.

# Hydrogen, element 1
@src Lavoisier, 1783

Hydrogen has one proton and one electron, the simplest atom there is. It accounts for about three-quarters of all ordinary matter by mass, and it's what stars burn to make light. Almost everything else in the universe came later.

The French chemist Antoine Lavoisier gave it its name in 1783, from the Greek words for "water-former," because when hydrogen burns, it makes water. It's also being developed as a clean fuel, since burning it produces only water. It's the lightest of all gases, which is why it was used to lift airships until the Hindenburg disaster in 1937 ended that era. Today it's mostly used to make fertilizer and to refine oil.

# Helium, element 2
@src Lockyer, 1868; Ramsay, 1895

In 1868, during a solar eclipse, astronomers saw a bright yellow line in the Sun's spectrum that didn't match any known element. They named the new element after Helios, the Greek word for the Sun. For nearly thirty years, it was known only from the sky.

Only in 1895 did the chemist William Ramsay find it on Earth, in a uranium mineral. On Earth it forms from radioactive decay deep underground and gets trapped in natural gas, from which we collect it. Once it escapes, it floats off to space, so it's not renewable in any practical way. It's used to cool the magnets in MRI scanners, and it's a bit sad to let it go in a party balloon.

# Lithium, element 3
@src Arfwedson, 1817

The lightest metal is so soft that you can cut it with a knife, and it floats on water. A Swedish chemist, Johan Arfwedson, found it in 1817 while analyzing a mineral. Its name comes from the Greek word for stone.

Today most people carry some in their pocket, in the lithium-ion battery that powers a phone. Lithium salts have also been used since the mid-twentieth century to treat bipolar disorder, as a mood stabilizer. It was an Australian psychiatrist, John Cade, who first reported the effect in 1949. It's an odd thing that the same element helps run our phones and steadies the moods of many people.

# Beryllium, element 4
@src NASA JWST

Beryllium is a light, stiff metal that is toxic as dust, so it's handled with care. Breathing the dust can cause a serious lung disease. But once it's shaped into solid parts, it's safe to use.

Its qualities make it ideal for space. The 18 mirror segments of the James Webb Space Telescope are made of beryllium, coated with a thin layer of gold to reflect infrared light. It stays stable even at extremely low temperatures. In nature it also colors gems. Emerald and aquamarine are both forms of the mineral beryl, and the green of an emerald comes from a trace of chromium within it.

# Boron, element 5
@src Standard chemistry text

Boron compounds are in more everyday things than you might guess. Borosilicate glass, the type used for lab glassware and some oven-safe dishes, can handle sudden changes in temperature without cracking.

Borax, a boron mineral, has been mined for thousands of years and was used in ancient times for soldering gold. It's now an ingredient in some laundry products and in fiberglass. Plants also need a trace of boron to build healthy cell walls. It's a quiet element that rarely makes the news, but you'd miss it if it were gone, since it holds up your cookware and helps your garden grow. Boron is also the element that makes green flames in some fireworks, and it turns up in the control rods of nuclear reactors, where it soaks up stray neutrons.

# Carbon, element 6
@src Geim & Novoselov, 2004

Carbon atoms can link up in an astonishing variety of ways, which is why the chemistry of life is built on it. About 18 percent of your body's mass is carbon. Every living thing, from bacteria to blue whales, has a skeleton of carbon chains and rings.

Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon, differently arranged. In 2004 Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov peeled single-atom-thick layers of graphite from a lump using sticky tape, making graphene, and they won the 2010 Nobel Prize for it. A sheet of graphene is only one atom thick and yet stronger than steel. It shows that even the humblest tools can lead to big discoveries.

# Nitrogen, element 7
@src Haber, 1909; Smil, 2001

Nitrogen makes up 78 percent of the air, but plants can't use it in that form. The two atoms in each molecule are held together so tightly that most living things can't break them apart. For centuries, farmers depended on manure and natural deposits to feed their crops.

Between 1909 and 1913 the chemists Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch worked out how to turn nitrogen from the air into ammonia on a large scale, making artificial fertilizer possible. Roughly half the nitrogen atoms in your body were fixed by the Haber-Bosch process. It's one of the most important inventions in history, and it let the world's population grow to many times what it could have been. The same chemistry also made explosives, which is a sobering part of the story.

# Oxygen, element 8
@src Lavoisier, 1777

Oxygen is about 21 percent of the air, and about 65 percent of your body's mass, mostly as the oxygen in water. The Swedish chemist Carl Scheele and the English minister Joseph Priestley each found it independently in the 1770s, and Antoine Lavoisier worked out that it was what made things burn.

Most of the oxygen in the air was produced by photosynthesis, by plants, algae and bacteria, over billions of years. About 2.4 billion years ago, it began to build up in the atmosphere, which was a disaster for many of the microbes of the time. For us, it's the reverse. Every breath you take draws on that long labor of tiny green organisms, and every cell of your body uses it to release energy from food.

# Fluorine, element 9
@src Moissan, 1886

Fluorine is the most chemically reactive of all the elements, so it's hard to isolate. For decades, chemists were injured, and some were killed, by the poisonous fumes while trying. It attacks nearly everything it touches, including most containers.

The French chemist Henri Moissan finally succeeded in 1886 and won a Nobel Prize for it. Fluoride, a compound of fluorine, is added to many toothpastes and water supplies because it strengthens tooth enamel and reduces cavities. It's an example of how a dangerous element can have a benign side. In small amounts, as fluoride, it's a protector of teeth, though the pure gas is among the nastiest substances known.

# Neon, element 10
@src Ramsay & Travers, 1898

Neon was found in 1898 by William Ramsay and Morris Travers, who chilled air until it turned liquid and then let it boil off piece by piece. They watched the gases come off one at a time, and each had its own color when electricity passed through it.

Neon itself glows a bright red-orange. It's not used in all signs called neon, though. Many use other gases, such as argon, or coatings inside the glass tubes, to produce other colors. The name comes from the Greek word for "new." When Travers saw the brilliant red glow of the new gas, he said later that it was a sight to be remembered, and it still lights up many a city street.

# Sodium, element 11
@src Davy, 1807

Sodium is a soft, silvery metal that reacts violently with water. In 1807 Humphry Davy was the first to isolate it, by passing an electric current through molten sodium hydroxide. He did the same with potassium a few days earlier.

Table salt is sodium combined with chlorine, a gas that's poisonous by itself. It's a striking example of how two dangerous substances can join to make something we eat every day. Your nerves also use sodium to carry signals, which is one reason the body needs a regular supply. Almost all the sodium on Earth is in the form of salt, and the sea is full of it.

# Magnesium, element 12
@src Standard chemistry text

Magnesium burns with a dazzling white flame, which is why it was used in the old camera flashes and in fireworks. Once lit, it's very hard to put out, and it can even burn in carbon dioxide.

It's also at the center of every chlorophyll molecule, the green pigment plants use to catch sunlight. In your body, it helps more than 300 enzymes work, and it's found in foods like nuts, seeds and leafy greens. So the same element that makes a brilliant flash in a firework is quietly at work in every green leaf. It's a light, strong metal too, and is used in some car parts and laptop cases.

# Aluminum, element 13
@src Hall & Héroult, 1886

Aluminum is the most abundant metal in the Earth's crust, but it's always combined with other elements, and was very difficult to extract. For a time in the 1800s it was more valuable than gold. Napoleon III is said to have served his most honored guests with aluminum cutlery while the rest used gold.

In 1886 two young inventors, Charles Hall in the US and Paul Héroult in France, independently found a way to extract it using electricity, and the price collapsed within a few years. Hall and Héroult were both in their early twenties, and were born in the same year. Today aluminum is in everything from soda cans to airplanes. It's a good example of how a cheaper process can turn a luxury into an everyday material.

# Silicon, element 14
@src USGS

Silicon is the second most abundant element in Earth's crust after oxygen. Sand and most rocks are silicon compounds. If you've been on a beach, you've walked on silicon dioxide.

In its ultrapure crystal form it's a semiconductor, meaning it can be made to conduct electricity or not, which makes it the foundation of computer chips. California's "Silicon Valley" is named for it. A modern chip has billions of tiny switches etched into a thin slice of silicon. It's remarkable that the stuff of sand, with a lot of purification and cleverness, has become the brain of our machines. The silicon used for chips has to be purified until fewer than one atom in a billion is something else, which is cleaner than almost anything else people make.

# Phosphorus, element 15
@src Brand, 1669

Phosphorus is essential to life. It's in DNA, in cell membranes and in ATP, the molecule that carries energy within cells. Your bones and teeth also contain a lot of it, as calcium phosphate.

It was discovered in 1669 by Hennig Brand, a German alchemist who was searching for the philosopher's stone and boiled down enormous quantities of urine. He ended up with a white substance that glowed in the dark. The name means "light-bringer" in Greek. Today, much of it goes into fertilizer, and the supply of phosphate rock is an important concern for farming. It's an element that links the kitchen of an alchemist to the cells of every living thing.

# Sulfur, element 16
@src Goodyear, 1839

Sulfur is a yellow solid that has been known since ancient times. The "rotten egg" smell we associate with it comes from hydrogen sulfide gas, not from pure sulfur, which has hardly any smell.

In 1839 Charles Goodyear discovered that heating rubber with sulfur made it stronger and less sticky, a process called vulcanization. It made possible things like tires, shoe soles and rubber hoses. Goodyear spent years in poverty and debt before his discovery paid off, and he died with large debts. The tire company that bears his name was founded after his death, and had no connection to him other than the name.

# Chlorine, element 17
@src Standard chemistry text

Chlorine is a greenish, poisonous gas, and it's one of the most useful chemicals in public health. Adding small amounts to drinking water kills germs and has dramatically reduced diseases such as cholera and typhoid.

But it also has a dark history. The German army first used it as a weapon on April 22, 1915, at the Second Battle of Ypres in the First World War. It was the beginning of chemical warfare. The same element that protects the water supply was turned into a weapon, which is a reminder that knowledge is neither good nor bad in itself. What matters is how we choose to use it.

# Argon, element 18
@src Rayleigh & Ramsay, 1894

In 1894 Lord Rayleigh noticed that nitrogen taken from the air was slightly heavier than nitrogen made from chemicals. The difference was small, but he trusted his measurements and kept investigating.

He and William Ramsay tracked down the difference to a previously unknown gas making up about 1 percent of the air. They named it argon, from the Greek for "lazy," because it won't react with anything. Light bulbs are often filled with it, to protect the filament. It's a fine example of how careful measurement can uncover something hidden in plain sight, since the air we breathe holds more than twenty times as much argon as carbon dioxide.

# Potassium, element 19
@src NIH Office of Dietary Supplements

Potassium is a soft metal that reacts even more violently with water than sodium, and the hydrogen it releases ignites with a lilac flame. In nature it's always found combined with other elements.

In your body it works with sodium to carry nerve signals and keep your heartbeat steady. A banana has about 420 milligrams, but many other foods, including potatoes, beans and avocados, have more, and most people don't get enough. A diet that includes plenty of fruits and vegetables is the easiest way to get it. The banana is famous, but it's not the best source. Too little potassium is linked with higher blood pressure, and getting more of it from food can help bring pressure down, especially for people who also eat a lot of salt.

# Calcium, element 20
@src NIH Office of Dietary Supplements

Calcium is the most abundant metal in the human body, and about 99 percent of it is stored in bones and teeth. The other one percent plays an important role in making muscles contract, nerves send signals and blood clot.

If you don't get enough in your diet, the body takes it from your bones to keep the blood level steady. That's why long-term shortages can lead to weak bones. Chalk, limestone and marble are all forms of calcium carbonate, often formed from the shells of tiny sea creatures long ago. Dairy foods are the best-known sources, but leafy greens, tofu and canned fish with soft bones also have plenty.

# Scandium, element 21
@src Nilson, 1879

Scandium is a good example of Mendeleev's predictions. In 1871 he described a missing element beneath boron in his table, which he called "eka-boron," and said what it should be like.

In 1879 the Swedish chemist Lars Nilson found a new element in a Scandinavian mineral, named it after Scandinavia, and it matched. Another chemist, Per Cleve, noticed that it was the element Mendeleev had predicted. Today it's used in small amounts to strengthen aluminum in bicycle frames and aircraft parts. It's a good story about the power of a prediction. A gap in a table of elements led to a search, and the search found the missing piece.

# Titanium, element 22
@src Brånemark, 1950s

Titanium is as strong as many steels but about 40 percent lighter, and it doesn't corrode. It's used in jet engines, in spacecraft and in high-end bicycles and golf clubs.

In the 1950s the Swedish surgeon Per-Ingvar Brånemark found by accident that bone grows right into titanium and holds it firmly in place, a property he named osseointegration. He had been using titanium chambers to watch blood flow in rabbits, and found that he couldn't get them out. That discovery led to dental implants and hip and knee replacements, and it's why those implants are made of titanium. Millions of people now have metal in their bodies thanks to a stuck rabbit chamber.

# Vanadium, element 23
@src Standard chemistry text

Vanadium is a hard, silvery metal that makes steel much tougher. It's named for Vanadis, a Norse goddess of beauty, because its compounds come in so many beautiful colors, including green, blue, yellow and violet.

Henry Ford used vanadium steel for the frame of the Model T because it was stronger and lighter than ordinary steel, which helped make the car affordable and durable. Today it's used in tools, springs and some batteries meant for storing electricity from the grid. It's an element few of us have heard of, but it's quietly part of the wrenches in our toolbox and the roads under our wheels.

# Chromium, element 24
@src Standard metallurgy text

Chromium is what makes stainless steel stainless. Steel with at least about 10.5 percent chromium forms an invisible, self-repairing layer of oxide on its surface that prevents rust. If you scratch it, the layer simply forms again.

The metal's name comes from the Greek word for color, because its compounds are so vivid, including the green in emeralds and the red in rubies. It's also the shiny coating on "chrome" car parts. Some chromium compounds are toxic, so factories that use them have to handle them carefully. The metal is a good example of how a small amount of one ingredient can change the character of another.

# Manganese, element 25
@src NIH

About 90 percent of the manganese mined goes into making steel, where it makes the metal harder and easier to work. Without it, steel would be brittle, and railways and bridges would not have been built the way they were.

In small amounts, it's an essential nutrient. It helps enzymes build bone and break down food. But too much, as workers in some industries have been exposed to, damages the brain, causing symptoms like those of Parkinson's disease. It's a reminder that the dose makes the difference. The same element that is needed by the body can harm it when the amount goes far beyond the normal.

# Iron, element 26
@src Standard chemistry text

Iron has shaped history. Earth's core is mostly iron and nickel, which generates the planet's magnetic field. And almost every large star ends its fusing life by making iron, which is why it's one of the most common heavy elements.

In your blood, hemoglobin uses iron to carry oxygen from the lungs to the rest of the body, and it's what makes blood red. In the Middle East, the Iron Age began around 1200 BC, when people learned to make tools stronger than those of bronze. A shortage of iron in the diet is the most common nutritional deficiency in the world. It's the metal of tools, bridges and blood.

# Cobalt, element 27
@src Standard chemistry text

For centuries, cobalt has given pottery and glass a brilliant deep blue, and "cobalt blue" is still a favorite pigment. Medieval miners cursed the ore, because it was hard to smelt and gave off toxic fumes, and named it after the German word for a goblin, "kobold."

Today it's also an essential ingredient in the rechargeable batteries in phones and electric cars. About 70 percent of the world's supply comes from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and its mining has been linked to child labor and unsafe conditions. Companies and governments have been under pressure to trace where their cobalt comes from, and to find batteries that need less of it.

# Nickel, element 28
@src Standard chemistry text

The name comes from German miners, who found an ore that looked like copper but yielded none. They blamed a mischievous spirit, "Old Nick," and called it Kupfernickel, which means "copper devil." The metal was isolated in 1751.

Today it's used in stainless steel, batteries and coins. The American five-cent coin, called a nickel, is actually 75 percent copper and 25 percent nickel. It's a bit of a joke that the metal that was named after a curse has ended up in our pockets. Nickel is also a common cause of skin allergies, so some jewelry is now labeled as nickel-free. Earth's core holds a great deal of it too, alloyed with iron, which makes meteorites a rich source of the metal.

# Copper, element 29
@src US EPA, 2008

Copper was one of the first metals people used, about 10,000 years ago, because it can be found in a pure form and is easy to hammer. The first copper tools and ornaments were made long before anyone learned to smelt it.

It conducts electricity almost as well as silver, so it's in nearly every wire in your house. It also kills many germs, and in 2008 the US Environmental Protection Agency approved copper alloys as the first solid surfaces that can claim to be antimicrobial. The ancients knew that water kept in copper vessels stayed fresher. It's a metal that has been useful to people for the whole of history.

# Zinc, element 30
@src NIH

Zinc is a bluish metal used to coat steel in a process called galvanizing, protecting it from rust even when scratched, since the zinc corrodes first and shields the iron beneath. Mixed with copper, it makes brass.

In your body, it's needed by hundreds of enzymes and plays a role in immunity and healing. Foods like oysters, beef and beans are good sources. Oysters have more zinc than almost any other food, by a wide margin. Some studies suggest that zinc lozenges might shorten a cold slightly if they're taken early, but the evidence is modest. The mineral is more useful as part of a regular diet than as a cure.
