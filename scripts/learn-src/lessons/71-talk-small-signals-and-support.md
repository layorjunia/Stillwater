# What a handshake says
@src Chaplin, Phillips, Brown, Clanton & Stein, JPSP, 2000; Schroeder, Risen, Gino & Norton, JPSP, 2019

In 2000 William Chaplin and his colleagues studied handshakes. Students were trained to rate the way that people shook hands, on grip strength, vigor, duration and eye contact, and they shook hands with more than a hundred people. The same people took personality tests. A firm handshake with good eye contact was linked to being extraverted and open to experience, and less shy and anxious. It also made a better first impression.

In 2019 Juliana Schroeder and her colleagues tested what a handshake does at the start of a negotiation. Pairs who shook hands before beginning were more likely to cooperate, and to reach deals. The effect was found in both lab and real settings. The handshake works as a signal of cooperative intent. It seems that a small ritual of touch can change how two strangers see each other.

# The sound that wasn't there
@src Warren, Science, 1970

In 1970 Richard Warren played people a recording of a sentence, in which a single speech sound had been cut out, and replaced with a cough. The sentence was, the state governors met with their respective legislatures convening in the capital city. The "s" in legislatures had been removed, and a cough was put in its place.

The listeners did not notice that a sound was missing. Most of them said that they heard the whole word, with the cough in the background. Even when told that a sound was missing, they could not say where it had been. This is called phonemic restoration. The brain uses the context to fill in missing parts of speech, and we hear what we expect to hear. It helps us to understand speech in noisy places, and it's also one reason why we mishear things.

# Vocal fry and first impressions
@src Anderson, Klofstad, Mayew & Venkatachalam, PLOS ONE, 2014
@conf mixed

Vocal fry is a low, creaky sound at the end of a phrase, produced when the vocal cords vibrate slowly. It has become common among young American women, and it has been criticized in the press. In 2014 Rindy Anderson and her colleagues tested its effect. They recorded young men and women saying a phrase, either in a normal voice or with fry, and played it to hundreds of listeners.

The listeners rated the voices with vocal fry as less competent, less educated, less trustworthy and less attractive, and said that they would be less likely to hire them. The effect was stronger for women than for men. Other studies have had mixed results, and some have found that vocal fry is a normal feature of speech, in both men and women. The bias may be a matter of habit. It shows how the sound of a voice can sway a judgment that should be based on the content.

# Singing together
@src Pearce, Launay & Dunbar, Royal Society Open Science, 2015

Singing in a group is found in all cultures. Why? In 2015 Eiluned Pearce, Jacques Launay and the anthropologist Robin Dunbar studied people who joined adult education classes. Some were in singing classes, which met weekly for seven months, and others were in creative writing or crafts classes. They asked the members how close they felt to the group at several points in time.

In the early weeks, the feelings of closeness grew faster in the singing groups than in the others. After a few months, the groups were equally close. Singing seems to be a quick way of forming bonds, and it works even with large groups. Dunbar suggested that it may have played a part in human evolution, since it allows many people to feel connected at once, as grooming does for primates. It's a good reason to join a choir.

# Talking about problems together
@src Rose, Child Development, 2002

When two friends talk about their problems, going over them again and again, and dwelling on them, it's called co-rumination. In 2002 Amanda Rose surveyed children and teenagers about their friendships, and asked how much they spoke with their friends about problems, and how they felt.

The ones who co-ruminated had closer friendships. They also had more depression and anxiety, and over time, their symptoms grew. It was more common among girls than boys. Talking about problems is good when it leads to solutions, or to feeling understood. But when it consists of going over the same ground, it can make people feel worse. It's a good question to ask, in a conversation with a friend. Are we working on a problem, or just feeding it?

# Support that you don't notice
@src Bolger, Zuckerman & Kessler, JPSP, 2000

Sometimes people help us in ways that we don't see, and this may be the best kind. In 2000 Niall Bolger and his colleagues studied couples in which one partner was preparing for the bar exam, a time of great stress. Each day, over thirty-five days, both partners recorded the stress and the support that was given.

The support came in two forms. In visible support, the person noticed that they were being helped, and recorded it. In invisible support, the partner said that they'd given help, but the person didn't notice. The people who received visible support were not less anxious. In fact, those who got invisible support were less upset. Noticing help may remind us of our own needs, and make us feel less capable. The best help may be offered in a way that doesn't call attention to itself.

# The mother tongue and the feelings
@src Harris, Gleason & Aycicegi, Bilingual Minds, 2006

If you speak two languages, you've probably noticed that some things feel different in each. In 2006 Catherine Harris and her colleagues at Boston University measured people's skin conductance, a sign of emotional arousal, while they heard words in their first and their second languages. The participants were Turkish speakers who had learned English as adults.

Their bodies reacted more strongly to words like taboo words, and to reprimands, in Turkish than in English. Even when they were fluent in English, the first language had a stronger emotional pull. It's one reason why people say I love you in their mother tongue, and why swearing seems weaker in a second language. Some immigrants find it easier to talk about painful things in the second language, because it feels more distant. It's a useful thing to know in therapy and conversation.

# How close is too close?
@src Hall, The Hidden Dimension, 1966

In the 1960s the anthropologist Edward Hall studied the use of space in conversation. He called it proxemics. He observed that people, without realizing it, keep different distances from others, depending on the relationship. He described four zones, based on his observations of middle-class Americans.

The intimate zone is closer than about eighteen inches, which is for close relationships. The personal zone is up to about four feet, for friends and family. The social zone is up to about twelve feet, for acquaintances and business. The public zone is beyond that, for speakers and audiences. The distances differ between cultures. People from Latin America and the Middle East often stand closer, and those from Northern Europe and East Asia farther. Discomfort arises when one person's norm is broken.

# Everyone believes liars look away
@src Global Deception Research Team, Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology, 2006

How can you tell when someone is lying? In 2006 a team of researchers asked people in seventy-five countries what they believed. They asked, how can you tell when a person is lying? Across all the countries, the most common answer was that liars avoid looking you in the eye. It was the same in rich and poor countries, and in every culture.

But research shows that gaze aversion is not a reliable sign. Liars and truth-tellers look at others to about the same degree. Some liars even look people in the eye more, to appear honest. The belief is so widespread that it may be one reason why we are so bad at spotting lies. The accuracy of most people is little better than chance. It's wise not to rely on a feeling that someone looks shifty.
