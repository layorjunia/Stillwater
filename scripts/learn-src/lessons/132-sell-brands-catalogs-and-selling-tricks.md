# The cartel that agreed on how long a bulb should last
@conf mixed
@src Phoebus cartel, 1925 to 1939; histories of incandescent lighting

On January 15, 1925, a group of the largest lamp makers in the world met in Geneva and formed an association called Phoebus. Its members included Osram of Germany, Philips of the Netherlands, Tungsram of Hungary and the international arm of General Electric. They agreed to divide the world into territories, and to cooperate on prices and patents. The cartel was meant to last thirty years, but World War II ended it in 1939.

It is best known for something else. The members also agreed to limit the useful life of their bulbs. It is commonly said that they set a limit of 1,000 hours, down from longer lives that some bulbs had reached. The case is often cited as the classic example of planned obsolescence, the practice of making products wear out so that people buy more of them.

The cartel did test and enforce the life of bulbs, and fined makers who produced bulbs that lasted longer. Defenders said that a shorter life allowed a brighter and more efficient light. Whatever the reason, the agreement shows how competitors can sometimes help one another by not competing. In many countries today, such agreements are illegal.

# Give them the razor, sell them the blades
@series sell
@src Razor-and-blades business model; King Camp Gillette

There is a famous rule of business that says you should give away the razor, and sell the blades. The first product is sold cheaply or free to create a market for the second, which has to be bought again and again. Modern examples are everywhere. Printers are sold cheaply, and the ink is expensive. Coffee machines that take pods, and video game consoles, which need games, work in the same way.

The slogan is credited to King Camp Gillette, who invented the double-edged safety razor with disposable blades. But Gillette did not follow the model. His razors were expensive when they came out, and the price came down only after his patents expired in the 1920s. It was his competitors who invented the idea of giving away the razor.

Gillette did something that was just as important. He pioneered the sale of disposable blades, so that a man could buy a fresh edge, and not have to sharpen the old one. The legend that he gave away razors is told as if it were his insight. It shows how a story can be attached to a famous name, and how the real origin of an idea can be forgotten.

# A crisis answered by recalling everything
@series sell
@src Chicago Tylenol murders, 1982; Johnson & Johnson response; Federal Anti-Tampering Act

In September 1982, in the Chicago area, seven people died after taking Extra-Strength Tylenol capsules that had been filled with potassium cyanide. The first victim was a twelve-year-old girl, Mary Kellerman, who died on September 28. The next day three members of one family, the Januses, died after taking capsules from a single bottle, and three other people died as well. No one has ever been convicted of the poisonings.

The maker, Johnson & Johnson, took a step that businesses had rarely taken. It pulled all Tylenol capsules from the shelves of the country, some 31 million bottles, at a cost of about 100 million dollars, though the poisoned bottles seemed to have been tampered with after they left the factory. It warned the public, and later reintroduced the product in triple-sealed, tamper-resistant packaging.

The incident led to the end of powder-filled capsules for over-the-counter medicines, to reforms in packaging, and to a federal anti-tampering law. It is now taught in business schools as an example of how to handle a crisis, by putting the safety of customers before the sales of a product. The company's trust with the public was largely kept, and the brand recovered.

# The car that was too well advertised
@series sell
@src Edsel, Ford Motor Company, 1958 to 1960

In the 1950s the Ford Motor Company set out to launch a new brand of car, a fourth make to compete with the choice offered by General Motors and Chrysler. It was named Edsel, after Edsel Ford, the son of the founder. The company ran a year-long advertising campaign before the launch, presenting the cars as the cars of the future.

The first Edsels, in 1958, had some new features. But they arrived in a recession, when sales of all medium-priced cars dropped sharply. Buyers thought the cars overhyped, of poor quality, and unattractive, especially the tall vertical grille at the front, which was likened to a horse collar. The price put them in the same range as other Ford models, and the brand had no clear identity.

Ford stopped the brand in November 1959, after a loss of more than 250 million dollars on design, production and marketing. The Edsel became a byword for a commercial failure, and is still given as an example of a product that was launched with great promotion and was not what customers wanted. The failure is a lesson in how much hype can raise expectations and make a disappointment worse.

# The woman who never lived, and answered the mail
@series sell
@src Washburn-Crosby Company, 1921; General Mills

Betty Crocker is one of the best known names in American cooking, and she was never a real person. She was created in 1921 by the Washburn-Crosby Company, which later became General Mills. In that year, the company ran a promotion in the Saturday Evening Post. Readers were asked to finish a jigsaw puzzle and mail it in, and in return they would receive a pincushion in the shape of a bag of flour.

About 30,000 puzzles came back. With them came several hundred letters, with questions about cooking. The advertising department saw that women who wrote in would want advice from another woman, and convinced the board to create a personality that the staff could use when answering. The first name Betty was chosen because it seemed cheerful and American, and Crocker honored a company director, William Crocker.

A portrait was painted in 1936 by Neysa McMein, and has been updated several times since. In 1954 General Mills added a red spoon logo, with her signature, to its flour and cake mixes. A brand can be a voice, as well as a product. A name on a box can build trust, if it seems to belong to someone who is ready to help.

# The colonel who did not start as a colonel
@series people
@src Harland Sanders, 1890 to 1980; Kentucky Fried Chicken

Harland Sanders was born in 1890 in Indiana. He held many jobs in his early life, including railroad fireman, insurance salesman and operator of a service station. During the Great Depression he began to sell fried chicken from his roadside restaurant, at North Corbin, in Kentucky. There he developed his secret recipe, and a method of cooking chicken under pressure in a fryer, which he patented.

He saw the possibilities of franchising, that is, allowing other restaurants to use his recipe and name. The first franchise opened in South Salt Lake, Utah, in 1952. When his original restaurant closed, he gave full time to selling the idea across the country.

By 1964 the company was growing too fast for a man of 73. He sold it to a group of investors for 2 million dollars, but stayed on as the brand's ambassador, and he kept the white suit and the title Colonel, an honorary title from the state of Kentucky. In later years he criticized the food, saying that the company's cost-cutting had made the gravy and chicken worse. He died in 1980. His face remains the symbol of the company.

# The salesman who knocked on every door
@series sell
@src Fuller Brush Company, 1906; Green River ordinance, 1931

Alfred Fuller began making brushes in a basement in Somerville, Massachusetts, and in 1906 moved to Hartford, Connecticut, and founded the Fuller Brush Company. He sold his brushes door to door, through a force of salesmen who went out with sample cases. His hairbrushes carried a lifetime guarantee, which was a rarity, and was one of the reasons for the company's fame.

The Fuller Brush Man became a figure of American folklore. Cartoons showed him with a foot in a door, and the phrase came to stand for any pushy salesman. The company grew, and by the middle of the twentieth century was one of the best known names in the country.

Door-to-door selling brought legal challenges. In 1931 a town in Wyoming passed an ordinance, called the Green River ordinance, that limited it. Fuller Brush fought it in court. The case went to the United States Supreme Court, which on March 1, 1937, dismissed the appeal for lack of a substantial federal question. So local governments could regulate it. The business has since moved to mail orders and shops, but the idea of bringing the product to the buyer's home was a lasting one.

# The store that was a catalog
@series sell
@src Aaron Montgomery Ward, 1872; Sears, Roebuck and Company

In 1872 a Chicago man, Aaron Montgomery Ward, started a business with a new idea. He would sell goods by mail, straight from the maker to the customer, and cut out the middlemen. At the time, farmers had to buy from local stores, which had high prices and little choice. His first catalog was a single sheet. It grew fast. By 1883 it was 240 pages long, and offered 10,000 items.

In 1896 he met his first serious competition, when Richard Warren Sears issued a general catalog. In 1900 Ward had sales of 8.7 million dollars, and Sears had 10 million. The two firms fought for the lead for most of the next century. By 1904, Ward mailed three million catalogs a year, and each one weighed about four pounds.

The catalogs brought goods to places that no shop could reach, and gave rural families a view of the wider world. They guaranteed satisfaction, and allowed returns. Montgomery Ward opened its own stores in the 1920s, and the original company closed in 2001, after nearly 130 years. The catalog was a forerunner of online shopping, since a customer chose from a list, and the goods arrived by post.

# The sign that did not say what the hotel wanted
@series sell
@conf mixed
@src Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius, Journal of Consumer Research, 2008; Schultz et al., 2008

Hotels would like guests to reuse their towels, to save water, soap and money. Many put a card in the bathroom that asks them to help save the environment. In 2008 Noah Goldstein, Robert Cialdini and Vladas Griskevicius tested whether a different kind of message would do better. They ran two field experiments in a hotel.

One card was the standard one, which said that guests could help save the environment. Another told them that the majority of guests reuse their towels at some point in their stay. This message used a social norm, what other people do. A third version made it more specific, and said that the majority of the guests who had stayed in that very room had reused their towels.

The norm messages worked better. By one summary, reuse rose from about 37 percent with the standard card to about 50 percent with the card that named guests of the same room. The effect was said to be about 25 percent better than the standard card. Other researchers found that the benefit of the room-specific wording did not always hold, and replications gave mixed results. But the central idea, that people are influenced by what others like them do, has been widely used.

# The label that people lived up to
@series sell
@src Kraut, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 1973

Telling people what kind of person they are may change what they do. In 1973 the psychologist Robert Kraut tested this in a field experiment on giving to charity. Those who answered the door of their home, in a sample of 153 working-class and middle-class people, were asked to give to a charity. Afterward, some who gave were told that they were charitable people, and some who refused were told they were uncharitable. Others were given no label.

A few weeks later a second canvasser came, asking for a different cause. The people who had been labeled charitable gave more than the unlabeled group, and those labeled uncharitable gave less than theirs. A label seems to have become part of how people saw themselves, and they acted in line with it.

The technique is called social labeling. It works by suggesting a self-image that a person then wants to keep. It has been used in schools and in fund-raising, and it can be used for good or for ill. A child who is called helpful may become more helpful, and one who is called lazy may become lazier. The idea that a person's behavior can be shaped by being told what he or she is like deserves care.
