# The football game that two campuses watched differently
@src Hastorf & Cantril, J. Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1954

On November 23, 1951, Princeton played Dartmouth in a football game that became notorious for its roughness. Princeton's star, the tailback Dick Kazmaier, left in the second quarter with a broken nose, and a Dartmouth player left in the third quarter with a broken leg. Each college's newspaper blamed the other side. Two psychologists, Albert Hastorf of Dartmouth and Hadley Cantril of Princeton, saw a chance to study the dispute.

They showed a film of the game to undergraduates at both colleges, and asked them to mark each rule violation as they saw it. The two groups saw different games. The Princeton students counted about twice as many violations by Dartmouth as by their own team, with nearly ten for Dartmouth by one account. The Dartmouth students saw about as many on each side.

The authors wrote that there is no such thing as a game existing out there, in its own right, which people merely observe. What we see depends on our loyalties. The study is a classic on partisan perception. It does not say that people lie. They see what they expect to see, and each side is sure that it is looking at the facts.

# Why each side thinks the news is against it
@src Vallone, Ross & Lepper, J. Personality and Social Psychology, 1985

In 1985 Robert Vallone, Lee Ross and Mark Lepper, at Stanford, did an experiment on how people judge the news. They chose a hot topic, the 1982 massacre of Palestinian refugees in the camps at Sabra and Shatila, in Beirut. Students who were strongly pro-Israeli and students who were strongly pro-Arab watched the same samples of network television news about it.

Both groups judged the coverage to be biased against their own side. The pro-Israeli viewers thought that the reports were unfair to Israel, and the pro-Arab viewers thought that they were unfair to the Arabs. Each said that the programs, and the people who made them, were hostile.

The result is called the hostile media effect. The authors suggested that it comes from two things. Partisans may notice different parts of the same story, and they may use different standards of what is fair. Dozens of later studies have found the same effect on other issues. It means that a story judged biased by both sides may be fair, and that a complaint of bias is not a good test of bias.

# The trip that looked better afterward
@src Mitchell, Thompson, Peterson & Cronk, J. Experimental Social Psychology, 1997

Why do people say that a vacation was wonderful, when they remember how many things went wrong during it? In 1997 Terence Mitchell, Leigh Thompson and their colleagues studied three kinds of trip: a tour of Europe, a Thanksgiving vacation, and a three-week bicycle trip in California. They asked people to rate the trip before it, during it, and afterward.

The pattern was the same in all three. People expected a trip to be better than it turned out to be, and they remembered it as better than it was. During the event they had more negative thoughts, which the authors linked to distractions, disappointment and a less favorable view of themselves. A few days later, they rated the event far more positively.

It is called rosy retrospection. Bad moments fade faster than good ones, and the memory is a summary that is smoothed. The effect helps to explain why people book another trip after a hard one. It also shows that how we feel about something in the moment is a different thing from how we judge it later, and that a memory is not a record of the experience.

# Does the internet put each of us in a bubble?
@series tech
@conf mixed
@src Pariser, The Filter Bubble, 2011; Bakshy, Messing & Adamic, Science, 2015

In 2011 the writer Eli Pariser published a book, The Filter Bubble, which warned that websites were using what they knew about us to show us only what we would like. He dated the start of the trend to December 4, 2009, when Google began to personalize search results for everyone. A bubble, he said, could also form on social networks, in recommendations and in advertising, and could leave people unaware of other views.

In 2015 researchers at Facebook, Eytan Bakshy, Solomon Messing and Lada Adamic, studied the data of 10.1 million users who gave their political affiliation. They found that about 29 percent of the hard news that people saw in their feeds reflected opposing views. They said that the user's own choices, such as which links to click, mattered more than the feed's algorithm.

Pariser replied that the effects that he was concerned about were clearly there and significant, though smaller than he had guessed. Critics noted that the sample was limited to people who stated a political affiliation, and that the data were not available to outside researchers. The question is still open. The bubble is partly made by software and partly by our own habits, and both deserve a look.

# Why safer cars may not make safer drivers
@series think
@src Peltzman, Journal of Political Economy, 1975; studies of anti-lock brakes

Safety rules and devices are meant to reduce harm. The theory of risk compensation says that people may respond by taking more risks. When they feel better protected, they become less careful, and some of the benefit is lost. A well-known example is that drivers of cars with anti-lock brakes were observed to drive closer to the car in front of them.

The idea is also called the Peltzman effect, after Sam Peltzman, an economist at the University of Chicago, who in 1975 published a paper on the effects of automobile safety regulation. He argued that rules such as seat belts had not cut deaths as much as had been expected, because drivers took more chances. Many researchers have disputed how large the effect is.

The effect is usually small, compared to the real benefits of safety measures, though it can sometimes reduce them or even reverse them. Some designers use the idea in the opposite direction. A street layout called shared space removes signs, curbs and lines, to increase the sense of risk, and so make drivers slow down. The lesson is that rules work on people, and people change their behavior in response.

# Do we learn our own minds by watching what we do?
@series mind
@src Bem, Psychological Review, 1967 and 1972

We usually assume that attitudes come first, and that they cause what we do. The psychologist Daryl Bem suggested a different view in the 1960s. He argued that sometimes, when we have no clear attitude, we find out what we think by looking at our own behavior, as an outsider would. It is called self-perception theory.

He tested it with a simulation of a famous experiment on cognitive dissonance. In the original, people did a dull task of turning pegs, and were paid either one dollar or twenty to tell the next person that it was fun. Those who got one dollar later said that they had enjoyed it more. Bem had other people listen to a tape of a man enthusiastically describing the task, and told them that he was paid either one dollar or twenty.

The observers judged that the man who got one dollar must have liked the task more, just as the participants themselves had said. Bem concluded that the actors in the original study may have worked out their own attitudes in the same way, by reasoning from what they had done. It is a theory that gives a different account of dissonance results. Both ideas have support, and may work in different situations.

# What people say, and what they do
@series mind
@src Research methods; social desirability bias

Researchers often ask people questions about themselves, and rely on the answers. A problem is that people tend to answer in the way that they think will look good to others. They report more of what is thought of as good behavior, and less of what is thought of as bad. This is called social desirability bias.

It affects topics such as drug use, sexual behavior, income, and psychological distress. Whether the answers seem to be good or bad, they may be distorted. In surveys on the number of sexual partners, for example, men tend to give higher numbers, and women lower ones, so the averages for both groups may be off.

Even self-descriptions of personality are tied to it. People who want to look good tend to rate themselves as more agreeable and conscientious. Researchers deal with it by guaranteeing privacy, by using questions that are less direct, and by comparing self-reports with records. The point for everyday life is that what people say about themselves is partly a performance for an audience, even when the audience is only the person asking.

# The belief that everyone else is biased
@series mind
@src Kruger & Gilovich, J. Personality and Social Psychology, 1999; Asch and Ichheiser, 1949

Most of us believe that we see the world as it is. When someone disagrees, we are likely to think that they are biased. In 1949 the psychologists Solomon Asch and Gustav Ichheiser wrote that when we meet people who see things differently, we tend to explain it by saying that they have some basic defect and are unable to see things as they really are.

In 1999 Justin Kruger and Thomas Gilovich gave it a name, naive cynicism, the expectation that other people are more self-serving than they really are. They tested it with married couples, video game players, debaters and dart players. Each person said how responsibility for good and bad outcomes should be divided between himself and his partner, and also how he thought the partner would divide it.

People split the responsibility fairly evenly, but they did not expect their partners to do so. They expected the other to claim more credit for good events, and less blame for bad ones. A husband might expect his wife to take the credit for the peace in the home, and she might expect the same of him. The belief runs in both directions. Seeing others as biased, while believing ourselves to be fair, can make conflicts harder to settle.

# The mind as a miser
@series mind
@src Fiske & Taylor, Social Cognition, 1984; Heider, 1958

Thinking carefully takes effort, and the mind often tries to avoid it. In 1984 the psychologists Susan Fiske and Shelley Taylor described the human mind as a cognitive miser. Just as a miser tries not to spend money, the mind tries not to spend mental effort, and takes shortcuts when it can. The term came from their book on social cognition.

Before this, the usual view was the naive scientist, an idea put forward by Fritz Heider in 1958. It said that people think in a rational and careful way, weighing evidence like a scientist in order to understand others. Fiske and Taylor argued that the evidence did not support this. People are limited in their time, attention and knowledge, and they use shortcuts such as stereotypes and rules of thumb.

The shortcuts are often good enough, and they save effort. But they lead to predictable errors, such as judging people by the groups they belong to, or assuming that behavior reflects a stable trait. The cognitive miser is not a sign of low intelligence. It describes people of all levels of ability, who think hard only when they have a reason to. The idea has had a wide influence on economics and political science.

# Why being left out of a game can hurt
@series brain
@src Eisenberger, Lieberman & Williams, Science, 2003; Williams, Cyberball

Does being rejected really hurt, or is that only a figure of speech? In 2003 Naomi Eisenberger, Matthew Lieberman and Kipling Williams tested it in a brain scanner. They used a simple computer game that Williams had created, called Cyberball. Thirteen students at UCLA lay in the scanner and played a game of catch with two other players, whose pictures were on the screen.

The other two players were not real. At first they threw the ball to the student. After a while they stopped, and threw only to each other, so that the student was left out. The student could see the game, but was no longer part of it.

When the students were excluded, a part of the brain called the anterior cingulate cortex became more active, and the more upset a student said he or she was, the stronger the activity. This area is also involved in the distress of physical pain. The authors suggested that social pain works like physical pain, as an alarm that signals a threat to our bonds. The finding was widely discussed, and has been debated, but the experiment showed that even a game with strangers on a screen can sting.

# The need to belong
@series mind
@src Baumeister & Leary, Psychological Bulletin, 1995; Maslow

Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary argued, in a famous paper in 1995, that the need to belong is as basic to human motivation as hunger. People, they said, need frequent and pleasant contact with a few others, in a relationship that is stable and in which they feel cared for. It is not enough to know many people. They argued that the need is found in all cultures and kinds of people.

They reviewed a large body of evidence. People form bonds easily, even among strangers, and resist breaking them. When the need is not met, there are serious effects on health and well-being. Their idea was part of a longer tradition. Abraham Maslow had put love and belonging in the middle of his hierarchy of needs, above food and safety.

The idea has practical meaning. A sense of belonging, to a family, a group of friends, a team or a faith, has large effects on physical, mental and spiritual health. One way to help people feel they belong is to listen to them well, so that they feel heard and accepted. It is a view of people as social animals, in which isolation is a harm to be taken as seriously as hunger or cold.

# The chicken that was fed every day
@series think
@src Bertrand Russell, The Problems of Philosophy, 1912; Taleb, The Black Swan, 2007

The philosopher Bertrand Russell, in his 1912 book The Problems of Philosophy, told a story of a chicken. Every day of its life, a farmer feeds the chicken. The chicken comes to expect that the farmer will always bring food. Then one day, the farmer wrings its neck instead. The chicken's belief, based on all of its experience, was wrong, and it had no way to know.

Russell used the story to illustrate a problem with induction, the habit of expecting that the future will be like the past. Past events, however many, do not prove that something will go on. The point is a serious one in philosophy. How can we justify the belief that the sun will rise tomorrow, or that nature will follow the same laws?

In 2007 the writer Nassim Nicholas Taleb used a version of it in his book The Black Swan, with a turkey, and called it the turkey problem. The turkey's confidence grows each day of good treatment, and is at its highest on the day before Thanksgiving. The lesson is that a long record of calm is not always a sign of safety. A risk that has never yet happened can still be the largest of all.
